Method

How to write each answer

Numerical

Formula, figures, unit, reading

  1. Write the formula in words.
  2. List the given figures; average the opening and closing balances.
  3. Substitute and calculate.
  4. State the unit: times, days, months or %.
  5. Interpret against a benchmark: a standard, the industry or the prior year.
Working backwards

One ratio at a time

  1. Start with the ratio that contains the given figure.
  2. Move step by step: current ratio gives current assets, acid-test gives inventory, turnover gives COGS, margin gives sales.
  3. Use the inverse operation at each step.
  4. Verify by recomputing the ratios you were given.
Case

Assumptions to verdict

  1. Assumptions: base year, balances used, any data anomaly.
  2. Common-size statements.
  3. Trend indices with the base year at 100.
  4. Four to six supporting ratios: liquidity, turnover, leverage, profitability, DuPont.
  5. Conclude: genuine improvement, or only growth?
Theory

Five moves

  1. Definition, in the slide wording.
  2. Formula or framework.
  3. Example, with numbers.
  4. Limitation or caution.
  5. A one-line conclusion.
Time

Plan on two minutes per mark. Do the short numericals first, keep 15 minutes for the case, and write the theory answer last in five tight sentences.

Reference

Formula sheet

Memorise
  • Liquidity: current = CA ÷ CL · acid-test = (CA − inventory) ÷ CL · DIR = liquid assets ÷ daily cash need
  • Activity: inventory turnover = COGS ÷ average inventory · collection = 12 ÷ DT · payment = 12 ÷ CT · cash cycle = holding + collection − payment
  • Leverage: D/E = liabilities ÷ equity · ICR = PBIT ÷ interest · FCCR = (PBIT + D&A) ÷ (interest + repayment ÷ (1 − t))
  • Returns: NPM = PAT ÷ sales · ROA = PAT ÷ assets · ROCE = PBIT(1 − t) ÷ assets · ROE = (PAT − pref. dividend) ÷ equity
  • DuPont: ROE = NPM × TAT × (assets ÷ equity)
  • Common-size and trend: item ÷ base × 100 · index = year t ÷ base year × 100

Averages are (opening + closing) ÷ 2. If only one balance sheet is given, use the year-end figure and say so.

Liquidity

RatioFormulaHow to read it
Net working capitalCurrent assets − current liabilitiesRupee cushion for operations; higher is better.
Current ratioCurrent assets ÷ current liabilitiesConvention 2 : 1 internationally; 1.33 is the India norm. Higher means stronger short-term solvency, but check what the current assets are made of.
Acid-test (quick) ratio(Current assets − inventories) ÷ current liabilities1 : 1 is satisfactory. Inventories are the least liquid current asset. A stricter version also removes prepaid expenses.
Cash ratio(Cash and bank + current investments) ÷ current liabilitiesThe most stringent liquidity test.
Defensive-interval ratioLiquid current assets ÷ (projected cash operating expenditure ÷ 365)Days the firm can keep operating on liquid assets alone.

Current assets: cash, current investments, trade receivables, inventories, loans and advances, prepaid expenses. Current liabilities: current provisions, loans (secured or unsecured) and bills payable due within 12 months.

Creditors' payment period12 ÷ creditors' turnover, or 365 ÷ creditors' turnoverHow long the firm takes to pay its suppliers.
Fixed assets turnoverRevenue from operations ÷ average net fixed assetsSales per rupee of fixed assets. Old, heavily depreciated plant makes it look too high.

DuPont analysis

ROA = NPM × TAT(Net profit ÷ Sales) × (Sales ÷ Average total assets)
ROE = NPM × TAT × EM(PAT ÷ Sales) × (Sales ÷ Assets) × (Assets ÷ Equity), where EM = 1 ÷ (1 − debt-asset ratio)
Driver 1

Profitability

Net profit margin: profit per rupee of sales.

Driver 2

Efficiency

Total asset turnover: sales per rupee of assets.

Driver 3

Leverage

Equity multiplier: assets per rupee of equity. It lifts ROE and risk together.

Common-size and trend analysis

Common-size

Structure

Income statement: each line ÷ sales (or total income) × 100. Balance sheet: each line ÷ total assets × 100. Lets you compare firms of different size and spot changes in the cost and funding mix.

Trend (common base year)

Growth

Index = (item in year t ÷ item in base year) × 100, with the base year at 100. An index of 110 means +10%. Use the same base year for every line.

Cash flow and modified measures

RatioFormulaHow to read it
Net cash flow (simplified)PAT + depreciation + amortisationProfit includes non-cash items; cash flow does not.
Operating working capitalOperating current assets − operating current liabilitiesExcludes excess cash and debt.
NOPAT(PBT + interest − other income) × (1 − tax rate)Operating profit after tax, independent of financing.
Free cash flowNOPAT − net investment in operating capital, or (NOPAT + depreciation) − gross investmentCash left after funding growth in operating capital.
ROICNOPAT ÷ operating capitalReturn on the capital invested in operations.

Sources of cash: an increase in liabilities or equity, or a decrease in assets. Uses: the opposite. The cash flow statement reports operating, investing and financing activities; the indirect method starts from profit and adjusts for non-cash items and working capital changes.

Concepts

Ideas to explain in words

Profit is not cash

Revenue is recognised when the sale happens and expenses when the related sale happens, so profit and cash flow differ. Depreciation cuts profit but no cash leaves. Credit sales are revenue before the cash arrives. Capital spending is paid in cash but spread as depreciation.

Composition of current assets

Two firms with the same current ratio are not equally liquid. The one holding cash and receivables is more liquid than the one holding inventory. Say this whenever you quote a current ratio.

The cash cycle

Positive: the firm's own cash is tied up and has to be financed. Negative: customers pay before suppliers are paid, so the firm holds cash that can earn a return.

Low margin, high ROE

ROE = NPM × TAT × EM. Margins in retail and FMCG are largely set by the industry, so ROE comes from fast turnover and leverage. Leverage raises risk, so state it.

Growth is not improvement

Sales and assets can rise while margins, turnover, liquidity and cover fall. Compare ratios and common-size shares, not only rupee growth. ROE can rise purely because leverage rose.

Choosing a benchmark

Trend analysis compares the firm with its own past. Cross-section compares it with an industry average. Within-year ratios relate statement items to each other. Standards such as 2 : 1 are a third reference.

Judgement

Limits, applications and what lies beyond the numbers

Problems in analysis

  1. No underlying theory. Nothing says which numbers to look at, so analysis is ad hoc and subjective.
  2. Few benchmarks. Conglomerates have no clean peer group.
  3. Window dressing. A firm may run down inventory on the last day, so look at averages.
  4. Price-level changes. Historical-cost accounting distorts balance sheets and profits.
  5. Accounting policy choices. Depreciation, stock valuation, foreign exchange, R&D, pre-operative costs, revaluation.
  6. Interpretation. A high current ratio is good liquidity or excess inventory; high fixed-asset turnover is efficiency or worn-out plant.
  7. Correlated ratios. Pick a small group that you understand.

Applications

  • Corporate excellence: net sales, net profit, market capitalisation, ROE, share-price return and three-year CAGR.
  • Creditworthiness: a credit scoring model built on character, capacity, capital, collateral and conditions.
  • Bankruptcy: single-ratio analysis versus a multivariate model such as Altman's.
  • Equity valuation: P/E = 8.2 + 1.5 × earnings growth + 6.7 × payout ratio − 0.2 × earnings variability.
  • Bond ratings: Kaplan and Urwitz (1979) found lower debt, higher interest coverage, higher ROA, larger size and lower risk help ratings.
  • Market risk: the accounting beta, the sensitivity of a firm's earnings to aggregate earnings.

Beyond the numbers

  • Are revenues tied to one key customer?
  • To one key product?
  • Does the firm rely on a single supplier?
  • What share of business is generated overseas?
  • Competition
  • Future prospects
  • The legal and regulatory environment

Guidelines: use ratios to ask the right questions, choose ratios selectively, use proper benchmarks, know the accountants' tricks, read the footnotes, and remember that the ratios are inter-related. Analysis is a mixture of art and science.

Checklist

Traps and source corrections

  • Inventory turnover. Class problems use COGS ÷ average inventory. The deck uses revenue, and some questions say "closing inventory". Follow the question's definition.
  • Asset turnover uses sales or revenue, never COGS.
  • Months or days. Say which: 12 months or 365 days. The sample answers are in months.
  • Net profit margin. The deck divides by total revenues; class problems divide by net sales.
  • Defensive-interval ratio. Divide by daily cash need (annual cash expenditure ÷ 365), not by the annual figure.
  • Borrowing capacity. State what the money is used for. Cash raises current assets and current liabilities together; machinery raises only current liabilities.
  • Acid-test. The exam form is (CA − inventory) ÷ CL; a stricter form removes prepaid expenses too.
  • Data checks. Does the balance sheet balance? Does PAT reconcile to the lines above it? State any gap as an assumption.
  • Slide typos. Horizon's EBITDA margin is 260 ÷ 1,300 = 20% (the slide prints 16%). Earning power is 210 ÷ 950 = 22.1% (the slide shows 200 in the numerator). The gross-margin row in the comparison table lists "current liabilities" as the denominator; it should be revenue from operations.

Activity ratios and the cash cycle

P1 to P5

P1

Inventory turnover and holding period

DMFS_Questions · Q1

A firm has sold goods worth Rs 300 lakh with a gross profit margin of 20%. The stock at the beginning and the end of the year was Rs 35 lakh and Rs 45 lakh respectively. What is the inventory turnover ratio? What is the inventory holding period?

Worked answer
  • Gross profit = 20% × 300 = 60, so COGS = 300 − 60 = Rs 240 lakh
  • Average inventory = (35 + 45) ÷ 2 = Rs 40 lakh
  • Inventory turnover = COGS ÷ average inventory = 240 ÷ 40 = 6.0 times
  • Holding period = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 months (365 ÷ 6 ≈ 61 days)

Stock turns over six times a year and is held for about two months before it is sold.

P2

Debtors' turnover and collection period

DMFS_Questions · Q2

A firm has made a credit sales of Rs 240 lakh during the year. The outstanding amount of debtors at the beginning and at the end of the year respectively was Rs 27.5 lakh and Rs 32.5 lakh. Determine the debtor's turnover ratio. What is the debtor's collection period?

Worked answer
  • Average debtors = (27.5 + 32.5) ÷ 2 = Rs 30 lakh
  • Debtors' turnover = 240 ÷ 30 = 8.0 times
  • Collection period = 12 ÷ 8 = 1.5 months (365 ÷ 8 ≈ 45.6 days)

Customers take about a month and a half to pay.

P3

Creditors' turnover and payment period

DMFS_Questions · Q3

The firm has made credit purchases of Rs 180 lakhs. The amount payable to the creditors at the beginning and at the end of the year is Rs 42.5 lakhs and Rs 47.5 lakhs respectively. Find out the creditor's turnover ratio. What is the creditor's payment period?

Worked answer
  • Average creditors = (42.5 + 47.5) ÷ 2 = Rs 45 lakh
  • Creditors' turnover = 180 ÷ 45 = 4.0 times
  • Payment period = 12 ÷ 4 = 3 months (365 ÷ 4 ≈ 91 days)

The firm takes three months to pay its suppliers.

P4

Cash cycle

DMFS_Questions · Q4

Find the cash cycle. (Use the holding, collection and payment periods from P1 to P3.)

Worked answer
  • Cash cycle = inventory holding period + collection period − payment period
  • = 2 + 1.5 − 3 = 0.5 month (about 15 days)
  • For reference, the operating cycle = 2 + 1.5 = 3.5 months

The firm pays suppliers about half a month before it collects from customers, so it needs a small working-capital bridge for that gap.

P5

Defensive-interval ratio

DMFS_Questions · Q5

The projected cash operating expenditure of a firm from the next year is Rs 182.5 lakhs. If the liquid current assets are amounting to Rs 40 lakhs, determine the defensive-interval ratio.

Worked answer
  • Daily cash operating expenditure = 182.5 ÷ 365 = Rs 0.5 lakh per day
  • Defensive-interval ratio = 40 ÷ 0.5 = 80 days

The firm can keep operating for 80 days on its liquid assets alone.

6. What the numbers say

  • Margins slipped. COGS grew 27.2% against sales growth of 24.2%, so gross margin fell from 37.6% to 36.1%. A small saving on operating expenses (20.6% to 20.0% of sales) did not offset it, and PBIT margin fell from 17.0% to 16.1%.
  • Profit lagged sales. Sales index 124, PAT index 115. Interest rose 40% (index 140), so it took 3.4% of sales against 3.0%. Net margin fell from 11.5% to 10.6%.
  • Efficiency fell. Total asset turnover dropped from 1.38 to 1.19 and fixed asset turnover from 2.12 to 1.83, because assets grew 43% on 24% more sales.
  • Funding became fragile. Of the 52 added to assets, 61.5% came from current liabilities (+32), 26.9% from long-term debt (+14) and only 11.5% from equity (+6). Equity fell from 52.5% to 40.1% of the balance sheet; current liabilities more than doubled (index 228).
  • Liquidity broke. The current ratio fell from 1.68 to 1.05, below the India norm of 1.33 and the 2 : 1 convention. Interest coverage fell from 5.6 to 4.7 times.
  • ROE rose only through leverage. ROE moved from 30.2% to 31.6%, yet net margin and asset turnover both fell and ROA dropped from 15.8% to 12.7%. The equity multiplier rose from 1.90 to 2.49, which carries all of the gain.
Conclusion

No, the improvement is not genuine. Vertex became bigger, not better: sales and profit grew, but margins, asset efficiency, liquidity and interest cover all weakened. The headline ROE gain comes from more debt, mostly short-term, so financial strength deteriorated.

Low margin, high ROE in FMCG

Questions · Q3 · theory

In case of FMCG sector companies, where net profit margin is low, what other factors can potentially drive its high return on equity (ROE)? Explain with an example of DuPont equation.

Worked answer
  • Definition. The extended DuPont equation splits ROE into three drivers: ROE = net profit margin × total asset turnover × equity multiplier = (PAT ÷ Sales) × (Sales ÷ Total assets) × (Total assets ÷ Equity).
  • Other drivers. A low margin can be offset by asset turnover and leverage. FMCG goods sell fast, so inventory turns quickly and debtors are collected quickly. Suppliers' credit funds much of the stock, which keeps assets lean and raises the equity multiplier.
  • Example. FMCG firm: 5% × 3.0 × 2.0 = 30%. A high-margin, slow-turning firm: 20% × 0.5 × 1.5 = 15%. The FMCG firm earns twice the ROE on a quarter of the margin.
  • Caution. Leverage raises risk, and a high turnover can reflect old, depreciated assets. Compare with industry benchmarks.
  • Conclusion. In FMCG, ROE is earned through speed and funding structure, not margin.

Reading the results

  • Smaller but more profitable. Sales fell 5% and total assets 7%, yet PAT rose 44% (27 to 39). COGS fell 9.6%, lifting gross margin from 35.0% to 38.2%; operating expenses fell from 20.0% to 18.4% of sales; PBIT margin rose from 15.0% to 19.7% and net margin from 8.4% to 12.8%.
  • Financial risk fell. Long-term debt dropped 42%, total liabilities to assets from 64.3% to 50.0%, the equity multiplier from 2.80 to 2.00, and interest coverage rose from 4.0 to 7.5 times.
  • Liquidity is steady. The current ratio moved from 1.67 to 1.60 and current liabilities were unchanged at 60. Both are above the India norm of 1.33.
  • Efficiency edged up. Asset turnover rose from 1.14 to 1.17 because assets shrank faster than sales.
  • DuPont. ROE rose from 27.0% to 30.0% even though leverage fell. Net margin (+4.4 points) drives it, which makes the ROE higher in quality and lower in risk.
Conclusion [1]

Yes, the improvement in quality is genuine: margins, cover and the balance sheet are all stronger and ROE is higher with less debt. The caution is the top line. Sales fell 5%, and cost cutting cannot lift profit indefinitely, so the Board should find out whether price or volume caused the decline.
