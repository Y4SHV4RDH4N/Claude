## R4. PARDO & LE MÉTAYER (2019) — "Analysis of Privacy Policies to Enhance Informed Consent" (the **Pilot** language)
**Problem — consent "on the fly":** most common justification for collecting data = people consented or could have objected. Legal systems differ: **CCPA — opt-out counts as consent; GDPR requires opt-in.** Authors: in practice consent is given **"on the fly"** at the moment a person wants a service → mechanical acceptance, not really informed (people don't read policies; policies vague/ambiguous). **IoT makes it worse** (tracking into physical world).

**Proposal — reverse the order:** instead of reacting to a controller's policy at the last moment, **data subjects (DSs) define their own privacy policies in advance** (time to reflect, help from experts/peers). Policies then applied automatically to decide what their devices disclose. Each side (DS and data controller, DC) has own policy; the two are **compared** to decide if collection is authorised. Reduces **power imbalance**. Users update policies when meeting new controllers/purposes → coverage grows over time.

**Language requirements:** GDPR Art 4(11) — consent must be freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous. To guarantee **unambiguity**, language needs a **formal semantics** (precise mathematical meaning so it can't be read two ways). But precise meaning ≠ understanding consequences → also give **information about risks** (**Recital 39** GDPR: data subjects "made aware of the risks, rules, safeguards and rights"). No existing language met all requirements → authors built **Pilot** (not limited to IoT; design draws on IoT users' expectations — Emami-Naeini 2017).

**Pilot in plain words:**
- **DC policies** declared by controller's devices *before* collecting any data (what controller wants to do). **DS policies** attached by DS's device to any data it sends (restrictions). Controllers can store DS policies received for **accountability**.
- **Building blocks:** **devices** (phones, laptops, access points) and **entities** (parties behind devices; each device belongs to one entity); **data items** with a **datatype** (age, address, city) and **value** (special "undefined" = deleted/never collected; **owner** = device where created); **purposes** (newsletter, advertisement); **conditions** (simple logic over data and context e.g. "only data from adults (age ≥ 18)", "only locations in Lyon", combine with not/and → policies are **contextual**). Each set has a **partial order** (ranking more general/more specific: Google < Alphabet as entity; city < address as datatype; newsletter < advertisement as purpose).
- **Nested layers:** innermost = **data usage rule** (allowed purposes + retention deadline, e.g. research until 26/04/2019); around it = **data communication rule** (adds a condition + the entity allowed to receive). **Sender checks condition and recipient entity before sending; receiver must respect usage rule.** Complete policy = **datatype + data communication rule + set of transfer rules** (further communication rules saying to whom data may be passed on). Captures two ways data moves: **collection** (controller gets from DS) and **transfer** (already-collected data sent to third parties). A person may have several policies for one datatype (employer at work, museum while visiting).
- Ordinary people can't write formulas → abstract syntax maps to **restricted natural language**: "Parket may collect data of type number plate and use it for commercial offers purposes until 21/03/2019…".

**Running example — ANPR (automatic number plate recognition) parking:** billing needs no consent (contract performance, Art 6(1)(b)); commercial offers/advertising need **prior consent**. **Parket** (French parking company) wants number plates for commercial offers until 21/03/2019 and to transfer to sister company **ParketWW** until 26/04/2019; no condition; broadcast to cars before entering ANPR area. **Alice** (driver) policy: collect number plate only if car in **Lyon**, commercial offers until 21/03/2019, **no transfers**. Alice's more restrictive → her device (car computer linked to phone) **automatically refuses consent**. Alice's device can send her policy back; Parket computes a **join** and sends Alice a new policy she accepts → **automatic negotiation**; join proven **privacy-preserving**.

**Execution model** (abstract precise description of how a Pilot system behaves step by step; fixes meaning so no ambiguity; powers the verification tool). System state: (1) values of data items on each device; (2) each device's **policy base** (own policies + received ones; used to decide if data may be communicated); (3) for controllers, data received as **triples (sender, item, policy)**. **Central idea: privacy preserved if the controller's policy is more restrictive than the DS's ("subsumed by")** — checkable **before any data collected**. **4 events:** **request** (controller asks DS for data type + supplies policy → added to receiver's policy base); **send** (DS sends item; allowed only if both sides have an active policy and controller's is more restrictive; policy "active" if applies to type and recipient entity, retention time not reached, condition holds); **transfer** (controller passes to another; needs active transfer rule in policy that data's owner sent; recipient's policy more restrictive); **use** (controller uses item for purpose; allowed only if purpose allowed and retention not elapsed). **Subsumption** = "more restrictive than": usage rule — every purpose sits below some purpose the other allows, retention no longer; communication rule — condition stronger, recipient lower, usage rule more restrictive; whole policy — datatype lower, communication rule more restrictive, each transfer rule covered. **Join** = merge two policies: keep only shared purposes (more specific), **shorter retention**, **both conditions**, lower entity/datatype, merge compatible transfer rules pairwise → result more restrictive than both.

**Risk analysis with SPIN:** precise semantics says what a policy says, not what might go wrong. Risks come from **misbehaviour** (using beyond purpose or transferring to unauthorised third parties). **Risk assumptions** about **risk sources** (entities with strong interest in using data for a purpose, or able/willing to pass data on); generic (databases by peers/NGOs on past misconduct) or DS-specific (fear a friend will pass info on). DS asks: "if I add policy p, is there a risk my data of type t is used for purpose pur? Is there a risk entity e gets my data?" Tool = **SPIN model checker** (checks every possible run to see whether a property can ever be broken); model in **Promela**; properties in **Linear Temporal Logic (LTL)** ("never"). "Is there a risk ParketWW profiles Alice?" → LTL: "ParketWW never uses Alice's data for profiling". Each device = process randomly executing events; misbehaviour = added **illegal events** (*illegal_use*, *illegal_transfer*). If no sequence breaks property → risk cannot occur; if one does → SPIN returns a **counterexample** explaining how. Not tied to SPIN (could use SMT solvers/theorem provers).

**Case study:** 4 entities — Alice, Parket, ParketWW, **CarInsure** (insurer = risk source); one datatype number plate (GD-042-PR); two purposes (commercial offers, profiling). Two risk assumptions: ParketWW may transfer to CarInsure ignoring DS policies; CarInsure has strong interest in profiling. Alice weighs two candidate policies (transfer to ParketWW allowed / no transfer). Results: Parket can receive data in every case, neither Parket nor ParketWW can use for anything other than commercial offers; ParketWW receives only if Alice allows transfer. **Decisive:** under normal behaviour CarInsure can't receive or profile whichever policy; **under misbehaviour both become possible (flagged violations) if Alice allows transfer, neither if she forbids**; counterexample: CarInsure gets data only after ParketWW executes illegal transfer. **Alice's 3 options:** neither policy (Parket uses data only for billing on contract basis); no-transfer policy (offers, no transfer); transfer policy (offers + transfer). If she wants offers but no risk of insurer profiling → option 2. = **"more genuinely informed consent"**: she chooses knowing what could happen to her data if someone breaks the rules.
**Prototype:** web app: enter Pilot policies via form, choose risk assumptions from generated list, ask questions, click "Verify!" → SPIN runs; answers change "Not Analyzed" → "Yes"/"No". Tailored to ANPR; code on GitHub.

**Versus earlier work:** **P3P** (pioneer; purpose/retention/conditions; website-focused, unsuited to IoT, no data transfers); **EPAL, A-PPL** (enterprise/accountability focus; no precise execution model → ambiguity, divergent implementations); **DHP** (closest in spirit; actions/purposes per recipient; deletion obligations; no explicit transfer rules/retention; service providers focus); De & Le Métayer's **harm trees** (manual per application; Pilot automatic once risk assumptions defined); **S4P, SIMPL** (formal trace semantics, prove global correctness, can't build enforcement, pre-GDPR); **contextual integrity** in LTL, **PrivacyAPI, PrivacyLFP** (formalise HIPAA, COPPA, GLBA — model laws not DS/controller policies); **UCON, OSL** (usage control/obligations/DRM — control use after access; no policy comparison; don't distinguish DS from controller); **PIAs/privacy risk analysis** assess risks of *systems*, Pilot assesses risks of *policies* (based on parties' motivation, reputation, history, not system vulnerabilities beyond a DS's expertise).

**Conclusions/strengths/weaknesses:** can underpin **personal data managers** (enforce automatically) and **personal data auditors** (check *a posteriori*). IoT challenge: DS must know about collection around them and communicate consent/objection to sensors directly or via registers (Cuncha). Future work: richer risk model (inference between data types — Privacy International; history of data already collected), theorem prover, verifying enforcement systems.
- **Strengths:** formal semantics removes ambiguity; each side states its own policy → **restores bargaining symmetry**; join = automatic negotiation; risk-based info implements **Recital 39**; counterexamples **explain why** a risk exists.
- **Weaknesses:** requires users to **author policies** (usability/effort; echoes Santos and **privacy paradox**); risk assumptions depend on someone curating trustworthy databases; **cannot stop misbehaviour, only predict it** (lighter, predictive alternative to Najdi whose design prevents data leaving); enforcement still relies on controllers honouring policies after receipt (**trust problem**); toy case study (one datatype, four entities); web app tailored to one scenario; **no user evaluation**; inferences not handled.
- **Where it fits:** "**automate the user's side**" answer to notice-and-consent. Restricted natural language = middle path between comprehensive and comprehensible. On ambiguity (undermines consent, texts, data): formal semantics. Like GDPR consent and Najdi it assumes an informed deliberate user (**privacy paradox** calls into question). Against scoping review (R5): non-ML formal policy analysis with user-facing tool but no user evaluation. Legal anchors: **Art 4(11)**, **Art 6(1)** (billing on contract not consent), **Recital 39**.

## R5. VAN DER SCHYFF, PRIOR & RENAUD (2024) — "Privacy policy analysis: a scoping review and research agenda"
**Why needed:** privacy policies are legal documents, longer/more complex (GDPR etc.); users spend only **seconds or minutes** scanning (Obar & Oeldorf-Hirsch) because of time, cognitive overload, distrust, convoluted sentences. Consequences: unwitting consent to disliked practices; mistrust (seen as designed to obscure); privacy-conscious people disengage from services. Fixes studied: **simplification** (plain language), **layered design** (essentials up front), **visual elements** (icons/infographics), **privacy-literacy education**.
**Earlier reviews narrow:** Del Alamo 2021 (Android 2016–2020 only); Del Alamo 2022 (automated NLP only); Bhattacharjee 2020 (visualisation); Leicht & Heisel 2019 (policy languages like P3P, now largely unused); Liu 2022 (ML as PET). Missing = broad **platform-agnostic** review.
**Contributions:** overview of techniques in **97 studies**; account of primary challenges; detailed research agenda. **RQ1:** which analytic techniques used? **RQ2:** what challenges deterred analyses?

**Method — scoping review** (Arksey & O'Malley; reported using **PRISMA-ScR**, Tricco 2018). Chosen over systematic review: suits complex areas with many concepts/techniques; maps where research likely useful (interdisciplinary); can include very different study designs without quality assessment; preliminary assessment for future systematic reviews. Search: **10 databases** (IEEE Xplore, ACM, ScienceDirect, Springer, Google Scholar…) + **56 IS journals** rated A*/A on 2022 ABDC list. Peer-reviewed only (no grey literature). Search string: "privacy policy" + classification, benchmarking, completeness, evaluation, analysis, integrity, assessment, test, visualisation; context-specific terms deliberately left out. Inclusion: empirical findings, online privacy policies, published **2004–2023** (2004 = emergence of Web 2.0). Exclusions: mostly conceptual, reviews/meta-analyses, non-technological, non-English, non-peer-reviewed. **1,429 studies (deduped) → 168 after titles/abstracts → 97 after full texts.** All authors screened titles; first two did full text; third resolved disputes. Charted data (context, country, design, theory, new/existing dataset, participants, techniques, challenges, effectiveness). Two **co-occurrence analyses** (ML and non-ML) using matrices (radial diagrams wouldn't work).

**What the 97 studies look like:** **US 41**; Germany and UK 8 each; China 7; Canada 5; Australasia 3; **no studies from Africa**. **45 studied websites** (8 used Alexa rankings e.g. top 1,000); **36 mobile apps**. **72 created own dataset**; of those using existing, most used **OPP-115** (115 annotated website policies; 14 studies); only 2 used **APP-350** (Android). **50 quantitative, 27 mixed, 8 qualitative**, 1 unclear. **Only 5 used an established theoretical framework; only 3 privacy-specific; 2 used Contextual Integrity** (to annotate social-media policies).

**RQ1 — techniques:** Non-ML: manual dominated (**22 studies**; 5 exclusively manual). Most common manual = **annotation** (8; expert annotators e.g. lawyers label statements, often vs GDPR = "tagging"); **content analysis** (6; not guided by theory); category assignments (2; beyond GDPR concepts, WhatsApp/RestAssured → policy template). Single studies: virtual-assistant dashboard questions (Bolton), heuristic evaluation of Facebook policy, readability of 113 organisations' policies, layered design vs "privacy finder" (Reeder), IoT ontology, risk assessment of 94 IoT device policies. Manual combined with **visualisations (8)** and **word counts/readability (7)** (Flesch-Kincaid, Gunning-Fog, SMOG). Stats: ANOVA for visual designs; Mann-Whitney-U for annotation. **ML dominates: 61 studies** (SVM, ML+NLP, NLP alone); annotation again most common accompanying technique (22), word counts (5), readability (4), visualisations (4). Bhatia 2016: **Hybridized Task Recomposition (HTR)** crowdsourced annotation; Manandhar: Device Attribute Mapping (2,442 smart-home policies). **Crowdsourcing (6; e.g. Mechanical Turk) = way to collect data not analyse.** **No study used PLS-SEM.** Few non-English; one Chinese study used distance calculations for readability.

**RQ2 — challenges (ML studies, 5 themes):** (1) **analytic complexity** — **ambiguity** (tools like **PolicyLint** need manual verification; seed words ambiguous; ontologies subjective); **policy harvesting** hard (longitudinal, network traffic vs claims, Android/VR). (2) **language/semantic variation** — other languages, semantic dependencies, limited lexicons (risk in Japanese); unit of analysis (paragraph inaccurate vs sentence too narrow for unfair/unlawful use); tools English only; translation loses nuance; keywords without context; Liepina suggest supervised ML + neural. (3) **visualisation & user evaluation** — often missing (Dombetzki's **Amaryllis** plugin no user study); users confused (Reeder's interactive grids, unused P3P). (4) **data collection** — few non-English; missing policies (esp. apps); many datasets pre-GDPR; **Wayback Machine/OPP-115 reliance** limits inference; narrow sampling, unknown APIs bias; crowd workers annotate inaccurately; emotionally charged user reviews. (5) **temporal nature of legal/policy terms** — definitions change over time, hurting comparative/longitudinal work (distinct from ambiguity). **Non-ML (3 themes):** (1) **analytic limitations & subjectivity** (content analysis interpretive; readability indices = "**silver bullets**" (Brooks, *Mythical Man-Month*) though test different things; unknown instruments of indeterminate validity; little user feedback); (2) **data scope & collection** (narrow industries/demographics/regions mostly US; vague terms; single case studies; nutrition labels risk **priming** — Fox); (3) **visual & technical** (software choices unclear; third-party flows hard to show; unfamiliar icons; nutrition labels confuse (Kelley, McDonald); people interpret "personal information" very differently; pre-training participants may verge on priming).

**Research agenda — 10 recommendations** (1–3 all research; 4–7 ML; 8–10 non-ML):
1. Use **established privacy-centric theory** — **Contextual Integrity (Nissenbaum)**, **Privacy Calculus (Dinev; Laufer & Wolfe)**, **Informational Self-Determination** (structured vocabulary, reduces ambiguity, bridges disciplines, enables benchmarking; "**much of privacy policy analysis research avoids the user**"; blanket GDPR compliance checking not enough).
2. Incorporate **privacy risk assessment + full DPIA** (necessity & proportionality); define scope; choose methods with comparative frameworks (IoT, blockchain).
3. Improve methodology: **user evaluations**, demographic/geographic inclusivity, comparative usability studies, diverse datasets (apps and websites), clear collection protocols/annotation guidelines, version tracking, crowdsourcing with care.
4. (ML) advance NLP for ambiguity: **BERT** embeddings, beyond OPP-115/APP-350, coreference resolution, syntactic parsing, benchmark datasets; **NER** for cross-border transfers (countries + mechanisms: SCCs, adequacy, BCRs).
5. (ML) better **policy harvesting**: adaptive scraping, page monitoring, differential analysis across versions, standardised formats/ontologies, legal experts; **blockchain/DLT** for immutable record of policy changes.
6. (ML) language/semantic: machine translation, **multilingual BERT**, classification at right granularity, topic modelling, sentiment analysis, language-specific ontologies, bespoke models for under-represented languages, linguists + legal experts.
7. (ML) **context-aware keyword extraction** (surrounding text, grammar, semantics; unsupervised word embeddings/topic modelling; detect emerging keywords).
8. (Non-ML) **standardise content analysis** (NLP aids, ML for automated content analysis, evaluator guidelines, **inter-rater reliability**).
9. (Non-ML) use **PLS-SEM** (Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling): complex/non-linear relationships, reflective and formative constructs, measurement error ("noisy data"), multiple mediation/moderation paths (beats CB-SEM), non-normal data (non-parametric), multi-method data (surveys, interviews, observations).
10. (Non-ML) multifaceted solutions for visual/technical challenges: user-centred icon design/testing, interactive visualisations, legal experts, alternative data sources/innovative scraping respecting platforms' terms (Google Play).

**Limitations:** diverse methods → hard to compare ("not heterogeneous" in paper — authors mean methodologically varied); researcher bias in selection/coding (mitigated by two independent coders); grey literature excluded; recommendations not validated/tested. Future: standardised content analysis, PLS-SEM, broader data across industries/demographics/regions via industry/government/NGO partners, taxonomies/ontologies, user-centric visual design, better scraping for longitudinal studies, privacy theory, risk assessment. Conclusion: ground analyses in theory, integrate risk assessment, need user evaluation and user-centric design; ML work should use advanced NLP; non-ML should use standardised content analysis and PLS-SEM.
**Criticisms:** recommendations untested; peer-reviewed only → misses industry/regulator work; ends 2023 → misses **LLM wave (GPT-4 era)**; PLS-SEM recommendation narrow (authors' IS background).
**Where it fits:** frame for whole field. Pałka (uses LLMs) and Pardo & Le Métayer (formal language + model checking) = the two families mapped: ML/NLP vs non-ML formal work. **Ambiguity** is the common enemy: hurts ML labelling; makes experts and GPT-4 fail (Pałka); Pilot's answer = formal semantics. Ambiguity in acts (clicks), texts (policies), data (annotations) undermines consent at every layer. **Gap in user evaluation** runs through others: Pilot has no user study, Najdi no implementation, Santos leans on others' user studies. Finding that users spend only seconds on policies = evidence against GDPR's assumption of an informed deliberating user. Second recommendation ties to GDPR's own risk tools (DPIA among **Arts 32–36**, alongside security, breach notification, prior consultation).

## R6. NAJDI, ATHAUDA, BANDARA & LUO (2025) — "Technical Framework to Ensure Data Subject's Rights in GDPR: A Conceptual Design"
**Problem: rights on paper, trust in practice.** Users trade personal data for services; because of cloud hosting data sits on providers' servers. Data = "**predominant currency**" of online services; companies monetise through **secondary use or sale**. Stats: 2023 **OAIC** survey **74%** of Australians saw data breaches as one of the biggest privacy risks; 2022 only **54%** of UK population trusted providers; 2024 **half of global data breaches involved customers' PII**. Illustration: **Cambridge Analytica — 87 million Facebook users**' data collected without consent, used for political advertising; Facebook fined. **GDPR** (May 2018) applies to every organisation processing EU citizens' data incl. outside EU; "world's strongest privacy and security law"; influenced Australia. But **legal framework, not prescribing technology** → organisational autonomy but compliance difficult; technical solutions fragmented/manual. Users feel consequences: **Bowyer et al.** — delayed/inadequate responses, data collected without consent appearing in responses, incomprehensible data files, no ability to control/modify/delete. **Pöhn et al.** — some sites compile data manually (long waits); most comply with access right but lack completeness/understandability.
**Three contributions:** review technical approaches supporting DS rights; map each to rights it supports (large gaps once trust in controller relaxed); propose own reference framework + discuss limits. Underlying question: **can technology guarantee DS rights even if the organisation that wants the data can't be trusted?**

**The paper's map of GDPR:** 99 articles; 4 roles — **DS** (data subject), **DC** (controller: purposes and methods), **DP** (processor on controller's behalf), **SA** (supervisory authority). 7 principles: lawfulness/fairness/transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; storage limitation; integrity & confidentiality; accountability; accuracy. **Rights IR1–IR11:**
| | Right | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| IR1 | Informed | what data, why, by whom, how long; existence and logic of ADM |
| IR2 | Give & withdraw consent | any time |
| IR3 | Transparent processing | concise, transparent, intelligible, accessible, clear plain language |
| IR4 | Request & response on action | told without undue delay what was done on requests under IR5–IR10 |
| IR5 | Access | confirmation + access to own data |
| IR6 | Portability | structured, commonly used, machine-readable; transmit to another controller |
| IR7 | Rectification | correct inaccurate/incomplete without undue delay |
| IR8 | Erasure | when consent withdrawn, DS objects, no longer necessary, unlawful |
| IR9 | Restrict processing | future processing stops; controller may still store |
| IR10 | Object | incl. **absolute** right to stop direct marketing/profiling |
| IR11 | Complain to SA | legal not technical → **excluded from analysis** |

**Trust gap in existing technology:** searched Google Scholar, ACM DL, IEEE Xplore 2020–2024 ("personal data", "control", "GDPR", "blockchain", "consent management") → **19 papers**, scored strictly: right **fully supported** only if ensured by design **and** shown via implementation/evaluation; **partially** if only in some contexts or not implemented/evaluated. **Findings:** (1) research focuses overwhelmingly on **consent management and processing (IR1–IR3)**; other rights sparse. (2) **Blockchain** widely used (shared record: immutable once written, transparent to parties, **decentralised trust**) — to record consent requests, **provenance**, audit logs. (3) **KEY ARGUMENT:** after consent, data is handed to DC and transitively its processors → **implicit trust** that DC won't misuse (intentionally or not). GDPR deterrent is **legal** (complaint to SA/IR11, legal action) — bites only if misuse is **detected and proven**; **no technical means to monitor or prevent misuse once data handed over.** If trust relaxed, none of existing designs fully meets IR3–IR10, and even IR1/IR2 can be violated. Conclusion (stark): "**none of the existing approaches meets any of the DSs' rights**". **Legal deterrence works only after misuse is detected and proven; technical design could prevent it in the first place.**

**The framework — 4 design decisions** (if the danger is handing data over, don't hand it over):
1. **PII never given to DC or DP**: stays under DS's control in a **Personal Data Store (PDS)** (individuals collect, store, manage, share data by own rules; e.g. **Solid Pods**, **Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI)**).
2. A **Consent Management System (CMS)** essential for IR1/IR2; formal/machine-readable consent (ontologies mapped to actions and rights) so what each consent permits can be checked automatically.
3. **Blockchain + smart contracts** (programs on blockchain) log consent actions and processing transparently, auditably, immutably.
4. **Process data in a secure environment**: DC sends its approved **algorithm** to the secure environment; consent validated, PII pulled in and processed there; only **output** goes to DC (**algorithm travels to the data, not data to the company**). Processing smart contract stores **hash** (digital fingerprint) of algorithm, hash of results, timestamp, list of consents → anyone later can confirm that the algorithm run and its results are those recorded.

**How it works:** two actors (DS, DC), two components: **Secure Consent Management System (CMS)** and **Secure Computing Environment (SCE)**.
- **Consent:** DC asks via DS's CMS; DS grants/rejects. If granted, CMS creates a **consent ID** stored with data items covered, types of access allowed, DC's ID. Consent also recorded (timestamp) in **Consent Smart Contract (CSC)** on blockchain; **rejected requests recorded too** (transparency). For sensitive data DS can ask to see/check the algorithm (no leakage, nothing beyond consent) and store its hash with consent. Consent ID returned to DC.
- **Processing:** DC calls SCE with algorithm, list of consent IDs and DS IDs, own DC ID. SCE nodes hosted by public cloud providers, authenticated via **PKI**. After authenticating DC, SCE starts a **Secure Computing Pod (SCP)** hosting a **trusted execution environment (TEE / enclave)** — hardware-isolated area where computation runs sealed off. SCP contacts each DS's CMS with DC ID, token, consent ID, algorithm hash; CMS verifies and performs **remote attestation** (checks from a distance that enclave is genuine) before releasing any PII. Algorithm runs, results return to DC, **Processing Smart Contract (PSC)** records hash of algorithm, DC ID, list of DS ID/consent ID pairs, timestamp. Net effect: **PII known only to the SCP during processing; DS keeps control throughout.**

**How each right is met (claim: fully meets 9 of 10 technical rights IR1–IR9):**
- IR1 informed: DC gives GDPR info via CMS; CSC records show who processes, why, how long.
- IR2 consent: CMS lets DS give/amend/withdraw; PII released only after consent validated; DS can verify/hash algorithm; works for new and existing systems.
- IR3 transparent: all processing logged in PSC → DS sees directly even if DC untrusted.
- IR4 request/response: by design; DS gets info from PDS, CMS, CSC, PSC without asking DC. IR5 access: PII in PDS, consents in CSC, processing records in PSC. IR6 portability: PII already in PDS; Solid Pods provide standard formats. IR7 rectification: DS edits PDS; future processing uses corrected data.
- IR8 erasure (modified sense): withdrawing consent has same effect for future processing; PII never collected without consent. IR9 restriction: DS revokes consent; SCE checks consent before any processing; GDPR's "controller may still store" irrelevant because DC never stores PII.
- **IR10 object (marketing/profiling) — the one right it cannot technically ensure:** e.g. DS consents to data used to train an ML model; **trained model = output → goes to DC**; if DS later objects, nothing stops DC continuing to use the model. Link to Custers & Vrabec: models aren't personal data, one person's erasure barely changes a model, data can be re-inferred.

**Limitations/answers/criticism:**
- Candid limitation 1: depends on algorithm respecting consent — malicious DC could write algorithm that **hides PII inside results**. Answers: DS can complain to SA; PSC lets auditors compare stored hash with real algorithm/results so SA can enforce; DS can review algorithm before consenting and store its hash. Overhead on DS (must validate algorithms) → future research on **automatic verification** that algorithm doesn't leak/go beyond consent. Limitation 2: IR10 / ML model problem; possible fix: keep ML models inside a TEE in the secure environment rather than at DC. **Only conceptual design; authors now implementing and testing.**
- **Strength:** shifts debate from "trust + legal deterrence" to "**don't hand the data over**" = **privacy by architecture**, spirit of **Art 25 GDPR**; integrates known technologies; gives DS direct visibility instead of relying on subject access requests.
- **Weaknesses:** **not implemented or evaluated** yet describes itself as giving "**fully supported**" rights while requiring other papers to show implementation to earn full credit (**asymmetry — sharp exam point**); heavy **burden on users to vet algorithms** (unrealistic; privacy paradox; Santos, Pałka, cost of self-management); outputs/aggregates can still leak or enable profiling; **blockchain immutability vs erasure** (hashes/IDs on chain may themselves be personal data and chain can't be changed); **trust shifted** not removed — to cloud providers hosting TEEs and their hardware; enclave attacks possible; performance and cost; weak **adoption incentives** (business models depend on holding data); **processors under Art 28 would disappear or change role**.
**Where it fits:** technological answer to failures of consent. In notice-and-consent debate: "**not on its own: change the architecture**" — even perfect consent fails if controller misuses data afterwards. Notice-and-consent survives only if cost of self-management falls and controllers are technically/legally constrained after consent (**Najdi + Art 28**). **Contrasts:** Pilot (predicts misuse risks) vs Najdi (prevents data leaving); Irish DPC guide (contractual/legal controls over processors) vs Najdi (not enough without technical enforcement). Shares **rational-user assumption** (people will vet algorithms). **Best answer: complementary — Art 25 is the bridge: law sets requirements and sanctions, design makes them usable, technology makes them enforceable.** Najdi's own framework proves it: when a stored hash doesn't match, **the SA must still act** → "technology narrows our reliance on law, but doesn't remove it".

## R7. DATA PROTECTION COMMISSION (Ireland) (2019) — *A Practical Guide to Controller-Processor Contracts*
**What it is:** regulator guidance (not academic), last updated **June 2019**; refers to GDPR and Irish **Data Protection Act 2018**. **Central message:** whenever a controller engages a processor, the two **must sign a legally binding contract** containing at least the terms of **Art 28(3) GDPR**. With *Planet49*, one of two readings stating what the law requires (others propose design/technical fixes). Shows how law keeps data protected once it leaves the collecting organisation.

**Roles:** **controller** = decides purposes and means (why and how); **processor** = processes on controller's behalf and **only on documented instructions**. GDPR (25 May 2018) = heavier obligations on both.
**Background duties:** **Art 28(1) duty of selection** — controllers may use only processors giving "**sufficient guarantees**" to implement appropriate **technical and organisational measures (TOMs)** so processing complies and protects data subjects. Processors also have **direct GDPR obligations** (records, security) in addition to contract → **two layers of liability**: GDPR fines/penalties **and** claim for breach of contract. Applies to all controllers in **public and private sectors**. Before GDPR a written contract was already needed; GDPR prescribes **many more provisions**.

**Contract content — part 1: describe the processing:** subject matter and **duration**; **nature and purpose**; **type of personal data**; **categories of data subjects**; **obligations and rights of the controller**.
**Part 2: nine clauses binding the processor** (guide lists without letters; letters are standard GDPR refs):
1. Process only on **controller's documented instructions**, incl. international transfers, unless law requires otherwise (**28(3)(a)**).
2. Everyone processing is under **confidentiality** duty (**28(3)(b)**).
3. Take all **Art 32 security** measures (TOMs) (**28(3)(c)**).
4. Engage a **sub-processor** only with controller's **prior specific or general written authorisation**; if general, give controller chance to **object in advance** to each new sub-processor (**28(2), (3)(d)**).
5. Impose same obligations on sub-processors, **remaining directly liable** to controller for their performance (**28(4)**).
6. **Assist controller** (appropriate TOMs) in responding to **data subject rights requests** (**28(3)(e)**).
7. Assist with **Art 32 security, Arts 33–34 breach notification, DPIAs and prior consultation Arts 35–36** (**28(3)(f)**).
8. At end, **delete or return** personal data as controller instructs (**28(3)(g)**).
9. Make available all info to **demonstrate compliance with Art 28** and allow/contribute to **audits** by controller or mandated third party (**28(3)(h)**).
**Optional extras:** liability/indemnities, detailed technical security provisions, additional cooperation provisions case by case. **Compliance task:** ensure a legally binding contract exists for every controller-processor relationship and keep it up to date with at least Art 28(3) terms.

**Links:** **accountability chain** — Art 28 uses **contract** to extend controller's obligations down the chain (controller → processor → sub-processor); = law's answer to the **trust problem** Najdi approaches technically. Najdi argue contractual/legal controls aren't enough without technical enforcement; keep data with DS so only results are sent; but under Najdi, **Art 28 processors would disappear or change role**. Notice-and-consent can survive only if cost of self-management falls and controllers constrained technically/legally after consent (Najdi + Art 28). **Pałka:** comprehensive policy format must name each recipient and **role (processor vs controller)** — exactly the distinction Art 28 turns on (mock policy: **CoolAccountants, FraudDetectors, Cloud711, Microsoft = processors; Facebook, Google = controllers**). **Santos:** CMPs may act as processors or controllers for websites, so errors in recording consent raise who is responsible. **Pilot:** transfer rules and usage rules = machine-readable "documented instructions" and limits on onward transfers.
**Limits of contractual approach:** contracts/audits work **ex post** (misuse must be detected then proven); **small controllers have little leverage over big processors** (cloud providers); "**documented instructions**" can be vague in practice. Hence law, design, technology complementary: Art 28 = binding requirements with sanctions, but (Najdi) law alone can't stop misuse once data left DS.
