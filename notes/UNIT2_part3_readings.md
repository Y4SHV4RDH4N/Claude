## R8. McDONALD & CRANOR (2008) — "The Cost of Reading Privacy Policies"
**Idea:** an old proposal said privacy could be handled by **economics instead of law**: a market where people accept/reject **micropayments** for giving up data. That market never appeared (advertisers might pay ~1 penny per name/IP address — Garfinkel — few would sell contact details for a penny). Authors **flip it**: users don't get paid, they **pay with their time** — researching privacy policies to keep their privacy. **Question:** if users read the policy of every site they visit once a year, what would that time be worth?

**Headline answer (point estimate): ~244 hours per person/yr ≈ $3,534 per person ≈ $781 billion nationally** — far more than the whole US online advertising market (**$21 bn in 2007**). Conclusion: the FTC's self-regulation vision ("**read, compare, choose**") is unrealistic; targeted advertising may have **negative social utility**.

### US background: notice and self-regulation
- **Late 1990s FTC** backed **industry self-regulation** over legislation (internet young; legislating early might stifle innovation; business more efficient than FTC staff). **Exception: children → COPPA (1998).**
- Based on **Fair Information Principles (FIPs)** — 1970s (mainframe credit databases); not law but principles for legislation (like Art 12 UDHR). FTC chose **5**: **notice/awareness, choice/consent, access/participation, integrity/security, enforcement/redress** (subset of **OECD's 8**). **Notice** underlies privacy policies. **1998 report: 92% of US commercial sites collected data, only 14% gave comprehensive notice.**
- Policies **voluntary**; no rule on format/length/readability/content. But once published, FTC enforces via **fraud/deception law**. ***Cartmanager* (2005):** FTC used **unfairness** even where company had no policy of its own (rented customer lists in breach of clients' policies).
- FTC "Internet sweeps": more sites posted policies each year, yet people still feared shopping online. FTC backed two further tools: **privacy seals** (TRUSTe, BBBOnline — criticised as weak; one study found TRUSTe-sealed sites had *less* protective policies) and **PETs** like **P3P** (machine-readable policies read by software "user agents"; built into browsers but unfamiliar to most users).

### Economic theory
- **Search attribute** (judge before buying, e.g. colour) · **experience attribute** (only after use) · **credence attribute** (never verifiable, e.g. nutrition content). **Nutrition labels turn credence into search**; a privacy policy should let you check *before* handing over your email whether you'll get spam.
- **Information asymmetry** → market failure (**Akerlof's "market for lemons"**, applied to privacy by Vila et al.). Policies are meant to reduce it — but if **cost of reading > perceived benefit**, rational actors **won't read** (Acquisti & Grossklags). Time = real cost (value of time literature from Becker 1965).
- **Why self-regulation fails:** (1) much of the web has **no substitute** (choose Amazon vs Barnes & Noble, but no substitute for Wikipedia); (2) policies are hard to read, read rarely, don't support rational decisions.
- **Explicit data markets don't exist:** Laudon's National Information Market (baskets of information); Varian: privacy = "right not to be annoyed" (sell specific data for specific uses for a fixed time). Prices tiny: ~1 cent/name; stolen data ~1/10 of former value; full clickstream ~**40 cents/user/month**. Yet outrage at **AOL search data release** shows people value data far higher → **no zone of agreement**.

### The model & inputs
**National reading time = (internet users) × (avg time to read one policy) × (unique sites per person per year).** Skimming time computed the same way. **Money:** hour at **work = 2× wage** (salary + overhead ≈ 2× take-home); hour at **home = ¼ wage**. BLS wage **$17.93/hr (Mar 2008)** → **$35.86/hr work; $4.48/hr home.** All estimates given as **low / point / high** (1st quartile / median / 3rd quartile).
- **Reading time (calculated, not measured):** word counts of policies of **75 most popular sites** (AOL data on 30,000 most-clicked sites, Oct 2005); range **144–7,669 words (~15 pages)**; reading speed **250 wpm**; short policy **2,071 words = 8 min**, median **2,514 = 10 min**, long **3,112 = 12 min**. Likely underestimates (jargon; slower online reading).
- **Skimming time (measured):** online study, **212 participants** (44 outliers removed e.g. 5-hour sessions; fast participants deliberately kept = conservative). **Six policies:** 928 words, 6,329 words, and four near 2,500. Five multiple-choice questions ("Does this policy allow Acme to put you on an email marketing list?" "Does the website use cookies?"). **Median 18–26 min** (lowest Q1 12 min, highest Q3 37 min). Time not linear in length: three ~2,500-word policies had medians 23–24 min, no significant difference (t-test p = 0.518, 0.690, 0.891). Earlier study (93 participants, 928-word policy) found ~6 min but artificially low (first question excluded as training; most time spent on Q1 = 1/3–1/2 of total).
- **Final skim figure:** time on **first question only** (find company's street address, at end of policy → forced full skim): medians 4–8 min → **3.6 min (low), 6.3 (point), 11.6 (high).** Defended: independent of number of questions; regular readers get faster.
- **Number of sites** (Nielsen March 2008): **66 unique sites/month at work, 119 at home, 105 overall**; 52 work-only, 105 home-only, 14 both. Assumes policy read at **first encounter**. Low bound: shared sites first seen at home; point = half/half (59 & 112); high = first seen at work (66 & 105). Weekly Nielsen data (25 and 40 unique sites/week) → **repeat-visit scale factors 66% work, 74% home**. Annual = monthly × 12 × scale factor → **1,354 (low: 412 work + 942 home), 1,462 (point: 467 + 995), 1,518 (high: 586 + 932)** sites/yr. Cross-check: 2008 study of 25 people found **390 unique sites over 52–195 days (mean 105 days)** → their estimate is conservative. **Population 221 million** Americans online (Nielsen, Pew, Census). (Abstract's lower bound = **119 sites** = monthly home figure.)

- **Perspective:** 244 hrs/yr = **40 min/day** = more than half of the **72 min/day** then spent online; more than time on shopping (1.9%), spam (6.2%) and games (13%) combined. Adware start-up delays ≈ 60 hrs/yr (¼ of this). In 2000 US taxpayers spent **26.4 hours each / 3.4 bn hours total** on federal tax returns. **Money:** 2005 internet access cost **$237/yr dial-up or $508 broadband** → reading time dwarfs it; **US online sales 2007 ≈ $260 bn** < employer cost of reading time at work.
- **Conclusion's headline figures: "~201 hours and $3,534 per user per year, $781 bn nationally"** — *internal inconsistency* (201 vs results' 244 hours).

### Caveats & argument
- **Cost could be higher:** self-regulation assumes comparison-shopping across sites (could double); connectivity costs not quantified. **Lower:** media consolidation (one policy covers many sites — though merged policies longer); people may care only about some threats (e.g. credit-card theft) so skip sites that don't collect that data.
- **Pilot study (behavioural):** some users believe only risk = losing **up to $50** to credit-card fraud → reading never worth it even at lower bound. Implications: (1) users **don't understand privacy risks** (even FTC said unclear whether consumers know data is collected/aggregated for ads); (2) **reduce cost of reading** — headings for skimming, hide irrelevant parts. Existing: **layered notices** (one-screen standard summary on top), **P3P** (XML), **Privacy Bird** (P3P add-on, expandable bullet-point report), **P3P Expandable Grid** (icons), **Privacy Finder** (search engine with icons; study found users **pay a premium** for more privacy-protective sites).
- **Answer to industry claim** ("people don't read, so they don't care"): sites must convey practices usably and cut reading time; if they can't, **regulation** may be needed. **Disclosure legislation alone may be insufficient** — adding text nobody reads gives transparency but "**limited practical utility**".
- **Advertising comparison:** $21 bn ads vs $781 bn time — "**out of kilter**"; not an argument to ban ads (direct-mail sales ≈ 10× cost of ads; ad spend understates market). Ads are usually an economic "**bad**" (people pay to remove them — Becker & Murphy); welfare analysis of online ads should include **cost of reading time**.

### Strengths / weaknesses / links
**Strength:** turns "policies are too long" into a **quantified opportunity cost**; transparent assumptions; ranges; deliberately **conservative**.
**Weaknesses:** US only, **2008 data** (pre-smartphone, pre-GDPR); assumes each policy read **once per site per year**; values of time (**2× wage at work, ¼ at home**) contestable; ignores that **reading is useless when policies are vague** (Pałka: even unlimited time doesn't yield answers); the **201 vs 244** inconsistency.
**Links:** **Pałka et al. cite it** ("hundreds of hours each year"; cost of self-management > expected loss) but **flip the solution** — not shorter policies/layered notices/P3P agents (McDonald & Cranor) but **longer complete policies + LLM readers**. Place in **comprehensiveness (Arts 13–14) vs comprehensibility (Art 12)** debate. Echoes Santos (consent is a "secondary task") and van der Schyff (users spend only seconds). **Pilot's user-defined policies and P3P-style agents descend from the PETs McDonald & Cranor describe.** On **notice-and-consent**: "notice and choice" asks the impossible; "limited practical utility" suggests more disclosure isn't the answer; undermines **rational-user assumption** (user who thinks only risk is $50 never reads → misjudges risk). **On self-management:** McDonald & Cranor — reading costs far exceed perceived losses → **not reading is rational and self-regulation has failed**; Pałka — agree on costs, but people *do* care; use LLMs; Santos — friction and dark patterns shape "choice" anyway. **Best conclusion:** **not reading is rational at the individual level but a regulatory failure at the system level.**

## R9. IWAYA, ALAQRA, HANSEN & FISCHER-HÜBNER (2024) — "Privacy Impact Assessments in the Wild: A Scoping Review"
**Background:** PIAs are a central tool of **privacy by design** (build privacy in from the start). Wright's definition: PIA = "**a methodology for assessing the impacts on privacy of a project, policy, programme, service, product or other initiative and, in consultation with stakeholders, for taking remedial actions as necessary in order to avoid or minimise negative impacts.**" Should start early, continue after implementation, involve stakeholders. Grew out of **Technology Assessment**, common from **mid-1990s** (Clarke).
**EU law = DPIA:** **Art 35 GDPR** requires one where processing "likely to result in a **high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons**", measured against Art 5 principles; **Art 36** = **prior consultation** with supervisory authority if high risk can't be mitigated. Authors don't strictly separate terms: Privacy (**Art 7 CFR**) and data protection (**Art 8 CFR**) are distinct Charter rights; Art 35 says "rights and freedoms", **broader than data protection** (Hallinan & Martin). "**PIAs are more than DPIAs**": PIA predates GDPR, from English-speaking countries, broad multi-dimensional privacy concept; DPIA = EU legal label, "to some extent a new label for something already existing". **WP29's own DPIA guidelines pointed to existing PIA methods** → DPIAs are part of the PIA family.
**Methodologies:** New Zealand PIA Handbook (2007), German BSI (2011), French **CNIL PIA** (2015), Australian OAIC (2020), standard **ISO/IEC 29134 (2023)**. Some general, some app-specific (RFID PIA, Smart Grid PIA); some **lightweight** (short mHealth questionnaire), some **full-fledged, risk-based** (severity × likelihood) — supervisory authorities may not accept lightweight PIAs → full-fledged recommended.
**Why the review:** literature contradicts itself — some call PIAs commonplace (Edwards; Bayley & Bennett; Stoddart), others little known/used (McKee; Iwaya) and rare even for mHealth apps. Earlier overviews limited: **Wright 2012** not systematic and pre-GDPR; **van Puijenbroek & Hoepman 2017** interviewed only Dutch DPOs. Aim: synthesise how PIAs work **in practice**, positives and negatives, appraise research quality, identify gaps. (Design of methods left to companion review **Wairimu et al. 2024** = R10.)

**Method:** **scoping review** (maps concepts/evidence; breadth over depth — Arksey & O'Malley), **PRISMA-ScR** + PRISMA-P protocol. **RQs:** RQ1 what literature discusses PIAs in practice; RQ2 main methodologies + positive/negative aspects in practice; RQ3 state of primary research. Searched **1 Nov 2023**: Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, ACM DL ("privacy impact assessment*" OR "data protection impact assessment*"), no limits on year/type + **snowballing** (references backwards, citations forwards). Qualifies only if **practice/real-world application** (theory excluded) but every study type counted (opinion & practitioner experience papers). Two reviewers screened double-blind in **Rayyan**, third resolved. **746 records → 60 relevant → 42 included + 3 by snowballing = 45 studies.** **Thematic analysis** (Braun & Clarke), coded in **NVivo** by three researchers to consensus. Quality via **CEBMa checklists** (10 questions qualitative; 12 surveys); replication package published.

**Evidence landscape (young, practice-dominated):** **two-thirds (30) primary research**; **37.8% experience papers** (Nokia, Siemens, Vodafone); **28.9% solution proposals**; evaluation research only "incipient"; **77.8% lessons learnt**. **UK = 12 studies (26.7%)**; **11 studies are chapters of one book — Wright & De Hert (2012)**, the field's most influential work. **Public sector 42.2%; private only 22.2%.** **GDPR mentioned in 60%**; **UK ICO framework most cited (37.8%)**; **62.2% after 2017**. **8 new methodologies introduced (7 post-GDPR):** PIAF (Wright & Wadhwa), **DPIA Data Wheel** (Henriksen-Bulmer), **Agile E/PIA** (Kroener), **Persona-Oriented DPIA for SMEs** (Schneider). **CNIL PIA = most used existing method, underlies PAPAYA tool**; 5 organisations used in-house methods (Nokia **PISA** template, Vodafone **Strategic PIA**).
**Quality is sobering:** of 30 primary studies, **28 qualitative** (case studies; interviews; focus groups; **Delphi** study; cognitive walk-through); only **4 (10%)** surveys. Qualitative: clear questions, suitable methods, described context — but **weak on data collection** (omitted participant numbers, interview length/guides) and **analysis** (rarely said how/by how many). Quantitative: **convenience samples, no power analysis, no response rates, no validity/reliability tests, significance assessed in only one study (Dashti et al.), no confidence intervals** → generally **not generalisable.** Exception: **Wright & Wadhwa (2013)** surveyed the **27 EU DPAs, 17 replies (63%)**, mostly free-text opinions.

**13 positive aspects (43 studies; ordered by frequency, not importance):**
1. **Legal compliance & accountability** (most frequent): meet obligations while pursuing business goals; demonstrate compliance in audits; basis for discussion with regulators/public; **formal sign-off** by owners/managers/privacy officers/executives; regulators help via guidance (WP29 "high risk").
2. **Protecting individuals & managing risk:** identify threats/controls focused on data subjects; force organisations to justify data collection; raise developers' awareness via risk matrices; **bridge legal principles and engineering**; work best integrated into general risk management with **privacy risk owners**.
3. **Good process:** **living document** (refined, reviewed); start early; **scope proportionate** to size, data sensitivity, intrusiveness (guards against **scope creep**); **independent assessors**; reuse past PIAs/templates (CNIL); **stakeholder consultation** (internal interdisciplinary experts + senior management buy-in; external public consultation, data subjects/representatives, regulator feedback).
4. **Transparency & culture:** PIAs force transparency (Clarke); reports should be **published**, easy to find, ideally in **central repositories run by supervisory authorities**; published PIAs build trust/reputation; self-assessment & training build **privacy culture**; **ISO/IEC 29134** supports standardisation. **Business case:** benefits outweigh costs, fixing at design stage cheaper, privacy = competitive advantage.

**17 negative aspects (39 studies; mirror the positives):**
- **Methods (most frequent):** risk assessment of new tech = "**shifting ground**" (risk never fully known); methods "not business friendly", "too onerous", "too dense"; little proven effectiveness; **subjectivity** (depends on assessor skill; likelihood/severity "vague").
- **How conducted:** **poorly conducted**, inconsistent, unplanned; home-made methods inadequate for GDPR; risk analyses miss issues like **law-enforcement access**; **disregard for data subjects** (focus on risk to the organisation); worst = **perfunctory PIAs** (ceremonial, pro forma, "put on a shelf", or ***ex post facto*** after design fixed); even good PIAs fail at **implementation** (clients reject recommendations or press assessors to mask core issues).
- **Organisational capacity:** lack of management support, internal audit, experts; confuse **privacy with security**; unsure who is **processor vs joint controller**; costs/delays hurt **start-ups/SMEs**; even DPAs see PIAs as burden; meaningful PIA disrupts internal workings (arguably the point).
- **Independence:** assessors have **conflict of interest** (livelihood depends on pleasing those who pay); government political barriers (privacy vs efficiency/national security; pressure "to get on" with policy).
- **Openness & regulation:** poor public consultation (comments only after legislation drafted; civil society often uninterested); government PIAs often unpublished; published private-sector PIAs "**almost non-existent**" (fear publication chills honest self-criticism); no central registry, no duty to publish, no accountability for implementing mitigations; regulators lack resources and are weakened by defunding; in many jurisdictions PIAs only "**encouraged**" → depend on controller's goodwill. **Art 35 closed that gap** in the EU for high-risk processing.

**Discussion — arc:** pre-2012 few empirical studies, mostly government guidelines; **2012 first GDPR proposal introduced DPIA duties**; risk-based methods (RFID PIA, **PRIAM** — De & Le Métayer 2016); most studies post-2017. **22 of 30 primary studies (73.3%) cite GDPR as motivation** → results hard to transfer outside EU. GDPR answers several negatives: controller responsible, DPO monitors, DPIAs mandatory for high-risk and **before processing starts**.
**Four research gaps (Miles's typology):**
- **Evidence gap:** disagreement on effectiveness, whether PIAs should be mandatory, how to streamline while rigorous, whether real independence possible → "**a one-size-fits-all PIA approach is unlikely**".
- **Methodological gap:** mostly case studies/expert opinion (low in evidence hierarchy vs controlled studies, cohort studies, RCTs).
- **Empirical gap:** many proposed methods (like privacy engineering generally) **never evaluated in real practice**.
- **Population gap:** over-reliance on **WEIRD** (Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, Democratic); only **6 studies (15%)** from elsewhere (Brazil, India, Indonesia). **13 book chapters lack formal peer review → only 32 studies peer-reviewed.**
**Two arguments for practitioners/policymakers:** (1) **A distorted PIA is itself a risk** — misleading PIA leaves risks undetected → unsuitable measures, unlawful processing, later fines; some PIAs commissioned for marketing/"**fig leaf**"; teams "professionally blinkered"; **checklist approaches (UK ICO, CNIL) tempt box-ticking** (Bieker et al.). (2) PIAs should evolve into **Fundamental Rights Impact Assessments (FRIAs)** — risks extend beyond personal data to fairness, society, planet; **DSA Art 34** already requires systemic-risk assessment (civic discourse, elections, minors); **AI Act** (Parliament's approach) requires deployers to do FRIAs (marginalised groups, environment) → PIAs/DPIAs become pieces of a holistic FRIA integrated into risk management. Conclusion: PIAs must become **evidence-based** — one of the most promising paths in **Empirical Privacy Engineering** (empirical methods to evaluate privacy engineering); major frameworks need rigorous independent evaluation; **Human Rights Impact Assessments** even less understood.

**Assessment & links:** **Strength:** first systematic map of PIA *practice*; includes experience papers (practitioner voices); balanced about weak evidence. **Weaknesses:** relies on **lower-level evidence** (opinion papers, book chapters); **11 studies from one 2012 book**; **prevalence counts ≠ importance**; mostly European/British; computing databases may miss the legal literature.
**Debate: DPIAs — real protection or box-ticking?** *For:* accountability, early privacy by design, risk ownership, transparency if published, Arts 35–36 mandatory with clear roles. *Against:* perfunctory, late, conflicted, checklists, unpublished, poorly supervised. *Remedies:* independence + sign-off, publication + central registries, stakeholder consultation, evaluation research, move to FRIAs. **Wider theme: symbolic compliance** — same critique made of **consent banners (Santos)** and **unread privacy policies (McDonald & Cranor)**.
**Links:** scoping review R5 recommends researchers use DPIAs — yet R9 shows DPIAs themselves poorly evidenced. **DPC guide:** processors must **assist with DPIAs under Art 28(3)(f)**; confusion over processor vs joint controller links to Art 28. **Pilot's risk analysis = policy-level cousin of PRAs; Le Métayer co-authored PRIAM.** **Companion Wairimu et al. (R10)**: same conclusion — weak evidence, need empirical privacy engineering.

## R10. WAIRIMU, IWAYA, FRITSCH & LINDSKOG (2024) — "On the Evaluation of PIA and PRA Methodologies: A Systematic Literature Review"
**Background:** privacy by design is now law: by **2023, 162 countries had privacy laws** (Greenleaf); **GDPR Art 25** (data protection by design and by default) and **Art 35** (DPIA for high-risk processing). Same Karlstad group as R9; use Wright's definition of PIA and "PIA" to cover DPIA. **PIA** from anglophone countries (US, NZ, Australia, Canada) = process covering many aspects of privacy; **DPIA** = GDPR legal requirement; WP29 pointed to CNIL, RFID PIA, UK PIA as DPIA examples.
**Problem:** persistent criticism — little **rigorous evaluation**; overly complicated and time-consuming; steps generic/abstract; deciding level of risk vague, depends on assessor skill; shortage of **historic data**. Same problems affect the two techniques inside a PIA:
- **Privacy Risk Assessment (PRA)** = analyses/evaluates risks and estimates impact. Leading example: **PRIAM** (De & Le Métayer).
- **Privacy Threat Modelling (PTM)** = identifies/lists threats and controls. Leading example: **LINDDUN**.
- **Hybrids** covering security and privacy: **SPIA** (security and privacy impact assessment), **PSTM** (privacy and security threat modelling).
**Generalised PIA process (5 steps):** (1) **threshold analysis** (is high risk likely → is full PIA needed?); (2) system description, usually with **data flow diagrams (DFDs)**; (3) **PRA** (core of the PIA, optionally supported by threat modelling); (4) risks prioritised and mitigated with controls; (5) documented in a report kept as a **living document**.
**Gap in earlier reviews:** Georgiadis & Poels (13 PIA methods for big data, mostly DPA documents); Xiong & Lagerström and Tuma et al. (threat modelling; mix security & privacy; note "the immaturity of empirical research in the software engineering community"). **None surveyed scientific PIA/PRA/PTM methods; none distinguished validated from evaluated; none appraised study quality.** **RQ1:** what validated/evaluated PIA and PRA techniques exist in the scientific literature? **RQ2:** how and to what extent validated/evaluated?

**Method:** **systematic literature review (SLR)**, **Kitchenham** guidelines (planning, conducting, reporting), PRISMA-P protocol in Git repo + replication package. Searched **Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, ACM DL** with 7 terms ("privacy risk assess*", "privacy impact assess*", "data protection impact assess*", "privacy threat model*"; IEEE's 8-wildcard limit forced splitting). **Google Scholar excluded** (no incremental query optimisation, Boolean search, bulk export; results can't be reproduced). **991 results → 309 duplicates removed → 682 screened (double-blind, Rayyan, third resolves) → 98 full texts → 34 → +5 snowballed = 39 studies.** Excluded: not English; only listed privacy risks; analysed security alone; low quality (no research question/method); lacked ***a priori*** risk analysis (at design stage); PIA studies that didn't analyse the method itself. Extracted: method name/scope; qualitative vs quantitative risk analysis; how validated/evaluated and extent (e.g. # surveys/interviews); whether it assessed **privacy harms**; conclusions.
**Studies:** span **2004–2022**, peak **2018–2022** (GDPR). **69% conference papers**; Springer **41.02%**. **Solution proposals most common; evaluation research least; 26% tested methods proposed by others.** By category: **16 PIA, 9 PRA, 11 PTM, 2 PSTM, 1 SPIA.** Threat modelling included because it also identifies privacy risks at design stage, uses DFDs, and LINDDUN is often compared with PIAs.

### "Validated" vs "evaluated" — THE central distinction (from Wieringa et al.)
- **Validation** = assessing a method **without real-world use**: authors try it themselves on an **invented/hypothetical scenario**, experiment, simulation/prototype, or **paper comparison**. Shows coherent and applicable — **not that it works when real organisations use it**.
- **Evaluation** = analysing/applying the method **in real-world practice**: genuine **case study** inside a company, **action research** with practitioners, other empirical studies. = tested in the conditions it was designed for.
- Wieringa also recognises **solution proposals** (method presented without testing) and **philosophical papers**.
- **Examples:** two-layer DPIA of **Timón López et al.** tried only on invented use case → **validated**. **DPIA Data Wheel** (Henriksen-Bulmer et al.) used in a **3-month case study in a charity (40 charities, 29 staff trained)** → **evaluated**.
- **Sharp critique of "case study":** six method papers call their testing a "case study" but they are **illustrative scenarios built by authors**. **Yin:** case study = "an empirical inquiry that investigates a contemporary phenomenon… in depth and within its real-world context". By that definition these are **validation, not evaluation — better called "use cases"**. Matters because readers may assume method proven in practice when only shown on paper (results hard to transfer to industry).

### Findings — PIA methods (10; best-tested group)
**50% evaluated, 20% only validated**, the rest untested when proposed. Evaluated: **Ahmadian et al. model-based PIA** (3 industrial case studies in EU **VisiOn** project, compared with CNIL, UK PIA, BSI); **Oetzel & Spiekermann seven-step PIA** (system characterisation → privacy targets → protection demand → threats → controls → residual risk → report; evaluated via action research & workshops with IT experts); **Todde et al. DPIA for hospital information systems** (public healthcare enterprise); **DPIA Data Wheel** (based on **contextual integrity**); **Friedewald et al. five-phase method** (tested in **12 organisations**, start-ups to public administrations; extends Bieker et al.'s high-level DPIA operationalising Art 35 — not evaluated at first, later evaluated via Friedewald). **Vemou & Karyda six-step process and Ivanova's DPIA for AI systems:** neither validated nor evaluated.
**Compared with Art 35(7) GDPR minimum content:** (1) **systematic description of the processing and purposes**; (2) **necessity and proportionality**; (3) **risks to rights and freedoms of data subjects**; (4) **measures to address risks.** All ten assess risks (core of a PIA); **only half cover measures** (Vemou & Karyda argue suggested measures can mislead; authors reply sector-specific measures e.g. AI/data provenance are a useful knowledge base). Some miss elements (Ivanova treats description as out of scope; two omit necessity & proportionality). **Few PIA methods assess privacy harms** — though harm distinguishes privacy risk from security risk (**Recital 75**: physical or non-physical harm of varying likelihood and severity). **Oetzel & Spiekermann and Ahmadian assess harmful activities drawing on Solove's taxonomy; Friedewald brainstorms harms.** Sector-specific PIAs (healthcare, charities) supported by WP29 and **Art 35(1) + Recital 92** (single assessment for similar processing operations). **No common methodology → controllers choose one aligned with WP29 guidance.** The one **SPIA (Makri et al.)** adds security risks, organisational characteristics, metrics, but rests on OECD principles authors find too abstract; adds overhead; neither validated nor evaluated.

### Findings — PRA and threat modelling
- **9 PRA methods: more advanced on harm — 77.8% assess harms;** **PRIAM** = reference model: **7 components** (system, stakeholders, data, risk sources, feared events, privacy harms, privacy weaknesses); uses **harm trees** (trace how a feared event leads to harm); groups harms into **5 categories: physical, financial, societal, dignity, psychological**. Variants: Wairimu & Fritsch (patients after data breach; actual harms), Alshammari & Simpson (refined harm trees). Push to **reduce subjectivity**: qualitative = criticised as unstructured; semi-quantitative scoring = subjective; quantitative (**Wagner & Boiten; Hart et al. fuzzy set theory; Cronk & Shapiro probabilistic FAIR-P**) claim objectivity but **untested**. **88.9% of PRAs only validated; sole evaluated = Hong et al. ubiquitous computing method** (lo-fi prototypes, interviews with 20 people, test with 3 users). **PRAs more mature on harm than PIAs but barely tested in practice.**
- **Threat modelling — LINDDUN** (privacy counterpart of **STRIDE**: Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, Elevation of privilege). LINDDUN = acronym of privacy threat categories; protects properties: **unlinkability, anonymity & pseudonymity, plausible deniability, undetectability, confidentiality, content awareness, policy & consent compliance**. Provides measures but **no risk evaluation** (critics fault it; authors say unfair as risk assessment is outside threat modelling scope; Wuyts et al. suggest adding risk quantification of QTMM). **Most evolved:** now **LINDDUN GO** (novices), **LINDDUN PRO** (experts), forthcoming **LINDDUN MAESTRO** (model-driven). **P-STORE** evaluated via usability survey of **59 requirements engineers**. Hybrids: **QTMM** combines STRIDE with privacy goals, uses attack trees, evaluated; **Treacy et al.** STRIDE + LINDDUN for **Internet of Medical Things** — neither validated nor evaluated (promising but unproven).

### How testing was done & how good
Validation methods found: invented use cases, illustrative scenarios and examples, realistic scenario, comparative analysis, experiments, misnamed "case studies". Evaluation methods: genuine case studies, action research, empirical studies, classified with **Ivarsson's rubric** (contribution to real-world relevance). **Only 9 of 39 studies evaluated a method; 4 methods neither validated nor evaluated.** Validated methods mostly PRAs (8 of 9); PIAs had fewest merely-validated (2 of 10). **Quality (CEBMa):** all evaluations had focused question, suitable design, clear context — but most **weak on data collection and analysis** (Oetzel & Spiekermann simply say "interviews"); **only two studies described data collection; only the LINDDUN evaluation passed every quality question → model for future research.** Independent comparisons: one found **CNIL did better than ISO/IEC 29134**; another comparing guidance from ten jurisdictions rated **Ontario, Alberta, UK, Victoria best.** **CNIL and LINDDUN most analysed → most popular.**

### Conclusions/agenda/criticism
Most methods only validated with illustrative/hypothetical examples; empirical and practical gaps hinder industry uptake — **LINDDUN the exception**. Why evaluation is rare: needs **industry collaboration, ethics approval, NDAs**; realistic designs hard to build. Expect more **system-specific methods (esp. AI and LLMs)**. **Agenda (3 parts):** (1) evaluate validated methods (**PRIAM, PriS**), independently replicate evaluated ones (**LINDDUN, DPIA Data Wheel**), build quality requirements into designs; (2) **compare PRA methods** (never done), **integrate PRAs into PIAs, include actual privacy harms**; (3) explore/compare **hybrid** methods. **Threats to validity:** peer-reviewed only, no books/chapters; didn't record how many studies each exclusion criterion removed. **Further criticisms:** excluding book chapters omits much PIA-practice literature (R9 relied on it heavily); review ends 2022; validation/evaluation line can blur; quality checklists favour **well-reported** studies over **useful** ones.
**Links:** companion to R9 (R9 = how PIAs work in organisations; R10 = how scientific methods are tested; **both conclude evidence is weak → need empirical privacy engineering**). **Validation vs evaluation turned on other readings:** **Pilot** only validated (illustrative ANPR scenario) → not evaluated; **Najdi's conceptual design not even validated**; Pilot contrasts its automatic analysis with manual harm trees of De & Le Métayer's PRIAM. Scoping review R5 recommends PRA/DPIAs — "must now reckon with how little evidence shows these methods work in practice".

## R11. CUSTERS & VRABEC (2024) — "Tell me something new: data subject rights applied to inferred data and profiles"
*Computer Law & Security Review, 2024.*

**Problem — rights that tell people only what they already know.** Three kinds of data in the data economy: (1) data people **give**; (2) data **collected without awareness** (Google sued over tracking in **Incognito mode** → **$5 bn settlement 2023**; post-*Dobbs* fears about pharmacy data); (3) **new data analytics generates — inferences, categories, risk profiles = "ascribed personal data"** (inferred data + categories/profiles). Ascribed data is often **genuinely novel**; reveals how controller views/assesses person; real legal/practical effects → exactly what a data subject would most want to know.
**The dilemma:** GDPR rights clearly apply to data obtained **directly/indirectly** from people (mostly already known to them). Read by wording, most rights **barely reach inferences/profiles** the controller generates → exercising them "**won't tell them anything new**". If rights don't cover ascribed data → uninformative; if they do → hard to delimit; clash with **trade secrets, freedom to conduct a business, privacy of other data subjects**. Tech companies favour narrow readings (**TechNet** lobbied against broad access right in **Oregon**); **California, Colorado, Washington** state laws explicitly include inferences in "personal information".
**Is ascribed data personal data?** Usually yes when ascribed to an **identified/identifiable person**. A **group profile** ("beer buyers buy diapers", "55% of teenagers are gamers") **isn't personal data until applied to an individual** ("the probability that John, a teenager, is a gamer is 55%").
**Method:** four classic EU interpretation methods — **grammatical** (text), **systematic** (context), **teleological** (purpose), **historical**. Narrow grammatical reading covers only "**lower-order**" data (needs no analytics); broad teleological reading also covers "**higher-order**" data (inferences, predictions, risk profiles). **EDPB and CJEU appear to support narrow readings in some cases.** Paper offers (1) a **taxonomy** of personal data, (2) a systematic framework mapping each GDPR right (**Arts 12–22**) onto each data type.

**Taxonomy (4 types):**
1. **Directly obtained** = provided by data subject (address, DOB; social media, sensors, wearables, log-ins) — collected **from** the subject → **Art 13**.
2. **Indirectly obtained** = existing data from another source, typically a **data broker** (**Acxiom: files on 2.5 bn people, ~11,000 attributes; Experian**) — collected **on** the subject → **Art 14**.
3. **Inferred** = new data distilled from available data (age worked out from DOB; health, credit score, life expectancy). **Kosinski: Facebook likes reveal ethnicity, sexual orientation, religion, intelligence, substance use.** Generated by controller.
4. **Categories and profiles** = group labels (**Facebook's four user types: relationship builders, window shoppers, town criers, selfies**; risk groups); also generated by controller.
**Crucial:** the type depends on **how obtained, not what it says.** DOB given to A = directly obtained for A; shared with B = indirectly obtained for B; A/B computes age = **inferred**. Had the person stated her own age, it wouldn't be inferred. **Incorrect data is still personal data** (else rectification pointless: DB records John as female = personal data about John, can be rectified). Inferences are **probabilistic and often unverifiable** (predicted cancer risk).
**Profiling:** by **classification** (predefined classes) or **clustering** (groups emerge). **Distributive** profiles true of every member; **non-distributive** true of group but not each individual. **Art 4(4)** = automated processing evaluating personal aspects (captures inferences and profiles but **ignores non-automated profiling** — manual statistics/stereotyping). **Feedback loops:** broker data already enriched with inferences re-profiled → same input counted twice (income → credit score → insurance premium) → **self-fulfilling prophecies** (hot-spot policing — O'Neil: amplifies inequality). Being categorised → fewer offers, higher prices, exclusion, damaged reputation — yet people often **don't know the practice exists, how many categories, or which one they're in.** GDPR partial answers: **Arts 13(2)(f), 14(2)(g), 15(1)(h)** "meaningful information about the logic involved" (basis of contested "**right to explanation**" Wachter et al. argue doesn't exist); **Art 21** objection to profiling. **Profiles that aren't personal data fall outside GDPR** → controllers prefer **de-identified data**; **group privacy** concerns remain.

**Right-by-right:**
- **Information (Arts 12–14)** = "**primus inter pares**" — cornerstone of transparency; without it no other right exercisable. Procedural info (controller identity, DPO, purposes, legal basis, recipients, storage). **Art 13 can't apply to inferred data/profiles** (subject cannot provide what she doesn't have). **Art 14 may apply** if inferences treated as "self-obtained" by controller or obtained from another controller. Only **two hooks:** (1) **WP29 transparency guidelines**: where purpose involves creating inferred data, **purpose and categories of inferred data must be communicated**; (2) **duty to explain logic of profiling (Arts 13(2)(f), 14(2)(g))**. Both give **clues, not the profile**. CJEU in ***SCHUFA*** said in passing rights should be read in light of purpose of Art 22 (protect against risks of automated processing incl. profiling). **Verdict: ambiguous for inferred data (possible via Art 14), only partial for profiles.**
- **Access (Art 15)** = **substantive**: tells what data is processed, confirmation, **copy (Art 15(3))**. Plainly covers directly/indirectly obtained, incl. "**shadow profiles**" of non-Facebook members built from data collected through like plug-ins. ***Österreichische Post* (C-154/21):** access includes **identity of specific recipients** on request. **Inferred data in scope** — **Recital 63** mentions medical records incl. diagnoses & physicians' assessments; EDPB access guidelines agree. **Two limits:** (1) **case law:** AG Sharpston in ***YS and Others***: "only information relating to facts about an individual can be personal data… a legal analysis is not"; ***Nowak***: examiner's notes are personal data but **exam questions are not** → applied to analytics: inputs and inferences personal data, **model not** → can't be accessed (authors respond cases non-digital, outdated; pending **Austrian reference C-203/22** may clarify). (2) **Exceptions:** **Art 15(4)** copy must not adversely affect others; **Recital 63** protects **trade secrets, IP, software copyright**; reverse-engineering risk; practical burden; **balancing largely at controller's discretion**, almost no case law beyond a **2014 BGH** and **2018 Dutch Supreme Court** decision. AG Opinion in *SCHUFA*: trade secrets may justify not disclosing the algorithm, **never an absolute refusal**; EDPB/WP29: let people check profile automatically. **Art 15(1)(h)** covers **logic**, not the actual profile or category; "who else is in my category?" can't be answered (involves others' data). **Access covers inferred data and profiles only partially.**
- **Rectification (Art 16)** — correct inaccurate/complete incomplete (supplementary statement), alongside accuracy duty **Art 5(1)(d)**. Factual data easy; subjective data → subject's word prevails. ***TransUnion v Ramirez* (2021, US):** wrongly flagged on terrorist (**OFAC**) list, discovered only when buying a car. Inferred data harder: **error margins** — 95%-accurate inference may be factually wrong about you yet statistically right; unclear which prevails; supplementary statement doesn't help (only incomplete, not inaccurate). Some predictions (**75% cancer risk, credit score**) **unverifiable** even later; verifying how inferred needs others' data + controller's tools (conflicts with others' rights); controllers may "**know the data subject better than she knows herself**". CJEU in *Nowak* and *YS* did **not** extend rectification to analytics tools/logic; opinions/assessments challengeable only where a procedure to contest exists; supplementary statements sit in separate systems not used in analytics (Art 16 doesn't require they be). **Rectification covers neither inferred data nor profiles; for unverifiable data erasure may offer more.**
- **Erasure (Art 17)** — e.g. consent withdrawn, subject's interest outweighs controller's legitimate interest; **does cover inferences**. **Four problems:** (1) inferred data seems "**owned**" by controller (tied to controller's model) → dilutes subject's interest; (2) where data shared, controller need only inform recipients (**Art 17 + Art 19**), subject to disproportionate-effort exception; **Art 11(2)** imposes no duty to re-identify de-identified data; (3) **identifiability gap** — de-identified models/profiles fall outside the law even if discriminatory; one person's erasure barely changes a trained model → real change needs **collective action**; (4) **deleted data can simply be re-inferred** (central government DB re-populated a municipality's deleted data every night — "technology does not keep up with the law"). ML best practice = "**exact erasure**" (delete from model and training data). **Erasure covers inferred data but not profiles.**
- **Restriction (Art 18)** — marking data to limit future processing (Art 4(3), Recital 67). Grounds: accuracy contested; processing unlawful; controller no longer needs data but subject does; objection under Art 21 pending. Contested accuracy unusable for inferences (accuracy can't be assessed); unlawful/obsolete → erasure usually preferred. **Redundant and underused.** Practical use for inferences: together with **objection (Art 21)**, esp. marketing → covers inferred data and profiles **only via Art 18(1)(d)**.
- **Portability (Art 20)** — receive data "**provided**" in reusable format. Includes name/email/address and **observed** data (location, logs) but **excludes derived data from analysis (e.g. smart-meter profiles)** — generated by controller not provided. **Reputation scores = grey zone** (Airbnb reviews moved to Couchsurfing?; Commission criticised WP29 as too data-subject-centric). **Solove (sceptic):** portability assumes healthy privacy market; authors: also serves transparency and fairness → excluding inferences concerning. **Probably doesn't cover inferred data; doesn't cover profiles.**
- **Objection (Art 21) & automated decisions (Art 22):** the rights that **clearly reach inferred data and profiles.** Art 21: object to processing based on public interest/legitimate interests; **absolute for direct marketing incl. profiling**; **Art 21(5)** objection by automated means (e.g. Google's personalised-ads switch). Outside marketing, controller can override with **compelling legitimate grounds** → authors suggest broadening absolute right to cover more processing of inferred data (WP29 Opinion 06/2014 favoured wider opt-outs; footnote: CJEU ***Meta Platforms* (C-252/21)** — profiling for personalised advertising "cannot in principle rely on legitimate consent" — paper's wording). **Art 22**: right not to be subject to **solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects**; WP29 and CJEU in *SCHUFA* read as **prohibition** (protected by default); gives some control over inference; **Art 22(3)** some understanding. But **doesn't address systemic discrimination** (algorithms replicate/amplify prejudice); **Solove: automation isn't the key problem, inference is.** **Recital 71** urges appropriate statistical procedures, error correction, preventing discrimination; Wachter & Mittelstadt propose "**right to reasonable inferences**".

**Summary table:** **Directly/indirectly obtained:** every right applies. **Inferred:** information **ambiguous**; access **partial**; rectification & (probably) portability **no**; erasure, restriction (Art 18(1)(d)), objection, Art 22 **yes**. **Categories/profiles:** information & access **partial**; rectification, erasure, portability **no**; only restriction (18(1)(d)), objection and Art 22 **fully** reach them. **→ On a textual reading and in practice, rights fully cover provided data but reach inferences/profiles only through profiling-specific Arts 21–22 and "logic" duties (Arts 13(2)(f), 14(2)(g), 15(1)(h)).**

**Case for a broader reading:** controllers resist for **practical** (time, cost, extraction effort), **business** (trade secrets, IP, competitiveness), **legal** (others' rights) reasons. **Teleological argument points the other way:** rights exist to put people **in control**; including inferences shows what is done with their data and which decisions follow. **Stages-of-processing argument (original):** processing runs **input → throughput (analysis) → output (results) → outcome (decisions)**, yet rights mostly reach only **input**. Narrow reading gives rights "**little or no meaning**" for inferences ("it would not tell data subjects something new"). Real empowerment needs all four stages + ability to **act** (delete, correct, stop) — mislabelled "terrorist" example. Doesn't ignore controllers: **Art 23 restrictions** still apply; starting point should accommodate the vulnerable.
**Proposals:** textual reading doesn't deliver protection; broader reading should cover all stages. Objections answered: practical — "if the data can be extracted for decision-making, they can also be extracted for data subjects"; business — **aggregate/generalise** info; describe **processing and its meaning** rather than technical model details (more useful anyway); disclosure revealing disliked practices is no reason to refuse; legal (others' data) — **let DPAs act as trusted third parties** to scrutinise inferences/profiles; DPAs have powers/expertise but **can't invoke rights on behalf of data subjects** → propose **amending GDPR** to allow this, with subjects' consent.
**Strengths:** clear taxonomy; systematic right-by-right grid; original stages argument; practical proposals. **Weaknesses:** **purely doctrinal** — no empirical study of actual **DSARs** despite framing; relies on contested/pending case law; trade-secret balancing left vague; DPA-as-proxy depends on **under-resourced** authorities.
**Links:** **Najdi** lists 11 requirements (IR1–IR11), admits **IR10 (objection to profiling) can't be guaranteed once ML model leaves the enclave** — Custers & Vrabec explain legal side (models not personal data; one erasure barely affects model; data re-inferable). **Pałka's "inference economy"** (food/music → race/politics) = exactly inferred data; comprehensive policy would disclose purposes such as profiling (matches WP29 rule on disclosing categories of inferred data). ***Planet49*** = cookies tracking for interest-based ads = **input stage** of profiling pipeline; **Pilot** handles purposes like "profiling" at policy level but leaves inferences for future work. Like McDonald & Cranor: current transparency gives little actionable info — they focus on **cost**, C&V on **scope**. Sharpen critique of **rational user** (can't verify a 75% cancer risk). **Trust in DPAs as third parties contrasts with Dove & Chen's controller self-assessment backed by regulator and Iwaya's conflict-of-interest concerns.**

## R12. LINDGREN, KAREGAR, KANE & PETTERSSON (2021) — "An evaluation of three designs to engage users when providing their consent on smartphones"
*Karlstad University; Behaviour & Information Technology.*

**Background — identity providers and the consent gap:** users often register with a service provider (**SP**) via an **identity provider (IdP)** ("log in with Facebook") — convenient (fewer passwords, data auto-forwarded), but the IdP learns which services you use and when, and sees data in plaintext → social-network IdPs can build detailed profiles. Study grew from EU **Horizon 2020 CREDENTIAL** project: privacy-preserving cloud IdP that can't see data in plaintext (**proxy re-encryption**), delivered through **CREDENTIAL Wallet app**. Starting point: **technology alone not enough if users don't understand data flows.** Consent here = **permission (authorisation) dialogue** ("may the app share certain data?"). Prior work: dialogues offering no choice or only opt-out make it hard to notice/control data; users want to **select data themselves** rather than have it pre-selected.
**Legal hook — Art 4(11) GDPR:** consent must be specific, unambiguous, freely given by an **affirmative action**. GDPR accepts ticking a box or choosing settings as such an action but says **nothing about how effective** they are at producing genuinely **informed** consent → **gap between legal requirements and interface design.** Earlier (Karegar et al. 2018b): checkboxes didn't help users notice/remember details; **drag-and-drop improved recall vs opt-out/pre-selected Facebook-style dialogues (Karegar 2018a).** Nobody had compared drag-and-drop with other active opt-in designs.
**Theory — Sundar's (2007) model of interactivity effects:** source, message and **medium interactivity** (the interaction technique: how users access/select info). Different techniques change how users evaluate, engage and process content → cognition/attitudes. Sundar et al. 2014 on desktop websites (click-to-download, dragging, hovering, sliding, zooming, flipping): some techniques (e.g. sliding) improved recall. **Costs:** Wang, Grossklags & Xu 2013 — checkbox-enabled Facebook dialogues → users released less info, opted out more (attention not measured). **Bravo-Lillo 2013 "attractors"** in security warnings — inhibitive attractors (swiping over text, retyping) cut risky installations but took more time (swiping/typing slowest). **Assale 2019** — rich healthcare e-consent with emotional feedback: healthcare workers saw extra interaction as incompatible with workflow → **risk of over-burdening users.** Drag-and-drop idea goes back to **Pettersson et al. 2005 (PRIME)** (break habituation to "OK/Cancel" buttons) and **JITCTAs (Patrick & Kenny 2003, PISA)**. Gap: nobody compared usability of alternative active-engagement designs on **mobile**.

**Method:** **between-subjects**; **60 participants** randomly assigned to 3 groups of 20: **checkboxes (control, common practice), drag-and-drop (DAD), swiping.** Prototypes built in **Axure**, tested on **Samsung S6**. Each participant **role-played a persona** subscribing on a laptop to fictitious website **PhotoHex**, using CREDENTIAL Wallet as IdP: opened app on phone, "scanned" fingerprint (clicked icon), selected data on authorisation dialogue, accepted, saw confirmation on website. Website requested **3 mandatory items (full name, email, DOB)** and **2 optional (profile photo, personal interests)**. **Afterwards:** questionnaire covering demographics, **SUS (System Usability Scale)**, **IUIPC (Internet Users' Information Privacy Concerns: control, awareness, collection; 10 items, 7-point Likert, scored 10–70)**, and recall of data shared and purposes. Coffee coupon; Swedish Research Council principles; informed consent; persona = no real personal data. **Participants:** 18 women, 41 men, 1 undisclosed; mean age 25.8 (85% aged 19–29); all at university; education didn't correlate with performance. **Mean IUIPC 55.78 (min 34, max 70)** → fairly privacy-concerned; group means: swiping 51.55, DAD 56.40, checkbox 59.40.
**Usability = ISO 9241-11:2018 (3 components):** **Effectiveness** (accuracy & completeness → task completion + recall); **Efficiency** (resources relative to results → **time from typing credentials to seeing confirmation**); **Satisfaction** (**SUS**: ten 5-point items, 0–100; **<50 unacceptable, >50 OK, >70 acceptable, 85 excellent** — Bangor et al.; new authentication systems should reach ≥70 — Ruoti et al.). **Awareness metrics** (Ronen et al.; Karegar et al.): participants saw list of **14 data types**, marked each shared / not shared / not sure. **Precision** = correctly marked shared/not shared ÷ all marked shared/not shared; **Recall** = correctly marked shared ÷ number actually shared; **Uncertainty** = share of "not sure".
**3 designs** — shared card-based layout showing SP identity, mandatory data with general purpose, optional data with general purpose; **sticky footer** (Cancel, Accept, number selected); each item an icon with label (chosen via mini-survey); interactive tutorials via Help (?) icon. Differed only in action: **(1) drag-and-drop** — drag each icon into its own drop zone, undo available; **(2) swiping** — swipe each item left→right, guided by pointing-hand emoji + arrow (**signifier** = visual cue telling users what to do), clear before/after state; **(3) checkbox** — common practice, control.
**Results:** all 60 completed. **Time: checkbox fastest 93 s; DAD 129 s; swiping 132 s** (DAD +36 s). Ranges 43–275 (checkbox), 51–592 (DAD), 42–284 (swiping). **Satisfaction (SUS):** all "good"; **DAD highest 81.00**, checkbox 79.25, swiping 72.63. **Recall: swiping 0.93, DAD 0.92 vs checkbox 0.84 — but with only 20 per group, NOT statistically significant.** **Precision similar** (0.85 swiping, 0.88 DAD, 0.89 checkbox); **uncertainty lowest for checkbox (0.10 vs 0.15 DAD, 0.20 swiping).** Time did not significantly correlate with recall. **Two data types excluded** because they exposed **wrong mental models:** many believed their **fingerprint** was sent to IdP and SP; many said they shared DOB but not **age** (implicitly shared). **≤7 participants could recall processing purposes** → users focus on data, not purposes/conditions. **Observed design problems:** 3 DAD users first mistook drop zones for checkboxes; **11 of 20 swipers briefly confused; 4 swiped wrong direction**; only **3 opened tutorial**; signifiers for mandatory data weak in all three; some tried to skip mandatory items without noticing **Continue** disabled (the 592-s DAD user missed that DOB was required). **Correlations:** gender & education unrelated to usability/awareness; age correlated with time (**0.361, p ≤ .01**; under-30s finished in <100 s); **IUIPC negatively correlated with uncertainty (−0.256, p ≤ .05)** → more privacy-concerned = more certain about what shared.
**What it means:** earlier work used a confirmation screen to slow users (Karegar 2018b) — better to use that time actively. Better recall likely from **avoiding automated behaviour** (habituation to checkboxes used for T&Cs everywhere) — but study didn't test whether new designs resist habituation themselves. Need stronger signifiers for mandatory vs optional, error messages, **tutorials shown by default the first time a new gesture is used**; unclear whether effectiveness lasts once users get used to them.
**Legal implication (heart of paper):** **active engagement can itself be the affirmative action Art 4(11) requires; the most efficient method is not the most effective or satisfying.** If a design doesn't produce real awareness, SPs **can't rely on the consents they collect** → fines and loss of trust. Takes on Bauer et al., Egelman & Karegar (content of consent forms barely affects understanding): authors say **lack of attention** → users guess from preconceptions; if attention improves, content matters more. **Conclusion:** checkboxes speedy but don't engage like DAD and swiping; newer methods positively affect satisfaction; design patterns notably affect perceived usability. Future: whether more time = more attention (eye-tracking, imprecise); informed consent also needs awareness of **purposes, retention, revocation (Art 13)** — open whether users attend to those if engaged only in selecting data; "not sure" answers may signal **distrust of IdP/SP**: "Until we can find ways to differentiate… the legal requirements of informed consent will be under the control of the service providers."
**Strengths:** directly tests what an "affirmative action" should look like using standard metrics (ISO 9241-11, SUS, IUIPC); honest about non-significant results. **Limitations (some admitted):** persona = no real data at stake (reduced attention); **student participants**, not representative; recall can be biased by preconceptions (triangulate with eye-tracking); **small samples**, recall difference **not significant**; **recall ≠ understanding**; covers only **selection of data**, not purposes (almost nobody remembered).
**Links:** answers question *Planet49* leaves open — Court said active choice needed and ticking an unticked box suffices, but **which active behaviour actually yields informed consent?** Ticking a box is lawful but may be the **least engaging** option. Complements Santos's heuristics with empirical data; wrong fingerprint mental models echo Santos's cookie misconceptions. **Friction debate:** more engagement costs time — DAD +36 s vs checkboxes, just as Kay & Terry's textured agreements raised reading time ~7 s → ~37 s. Gains real but modest; counterpoints: **McDonald & Cranor** (time is the cost), **Assale** (hospital workflow), **habituation**, **Santos** (design power can become manipulation). **Broader lesson: the legal test checks the form of the action; meaningful consent needs attention-supporting design.**
