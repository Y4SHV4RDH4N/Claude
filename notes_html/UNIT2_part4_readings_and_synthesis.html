UNIT 2 — PART 4: R13 (Dove & Chen) · R14 (Medzini & Epstein) · R15 (Kay & Terry) · SYNTHESIS & EXAM TOOLKIT

R13. DOVE & CHEN (2020) — "Should consent for data processing be privileged in health research? A comparative legal analysis"

The problem: a research privilege, but divided laws on consent

  • Recital 159 GDPR defines scientific research broadly: technological development, fundamental research, applied research, privately funded research, public health studies. With safeguards and a basis in EU/Member State law, researchers enjoy a research privilege = lighter-touch regime: may store data for long, refuse to delete data even after a participant withdraws consent, reuse data across projects.
  • Rationale (twofold): protect individuals' rights and enable medical innovation (treating research like banking/marketing would slow breakthroughs).
  • Yet national laws diverge sharply on one question: must consent be the lawful basis for processing research data?
  • Authors trace the divergence to a common misperception + a contested normative link:
    • Misperception: consent is legally required to process personal data at all — in fact it's one of several lawful bases.
    • Normative link: some draw a line between consent as a research-ethics principle (grounded in autonomy; legally required for e.g. clinical trials) and consent as a lawful basis under data protection law. Authors' counter-argument (runs through whole paper): the two should NOT be conflated — for ethical, legal and methodological reasons; for public bodies consent may not even be a proper basis; consent is context-dependent not absolute.
  • Questions: is the divergence harmful to international research collaboration? Could it encourage regulatory arbitrage (research moving to "friendlier" jurisdictions)? Should researchers choose (explicit) consent or another basis? What role should public interest play?
  • Scope limits: adults with capacity; data collected for future research use; not secondary use of existing data, adults lacking capacity, or children.
  • Compares 4 regimes: GDPR; South Africa's POPIA 2013; UK Data Protection Act 2018 (DPA 2018); Ireland's Health Research Regulations 2018.

GDPR: a framework with no bias toward consent

  • Roles in research: controllers = companies, researchers, universities; processors = cloud providers, research collaborators; data subjects = participants.
  • Art 6 lists six lawful bases; consent (Art 6(1)(a), defined in Art 4(11)) is only one. Recital 50: further processing for scientific research is compatible with original purpose → no new lawful basis if same controller (fits purpose limitation Art 5(1)(b)). Two particularly useful bases: legitimate interests (mainly commercial research) and public task (needs basis in EU/Member State law). UK Health Research Authority (HRA) recommends researchers use bases other than consent.
  • Health and genetic data = special category data (Art 9) — processing generally prohibited unless one of ten exceptions in Art 9(2) applies → research must satisfy two conditions at once: an Art 6 lawful basis and an Art 9(2) exception. Key exception: Art 9(2)(j) — scientific research based on law and subject to safeguards in Art 89(1) → lets researchers avoid the explicit consent route Art 9(2)(a). (Pormeister argues legislators probably didn't intend this to apply to genetic data.)
  • Consent is a poor fit for much research: requirements (esp. freely given) so strict that for clinical trials consent may not be the most appropriate basis — EDPB Opinion 3/2019. Researchers gravitate to Arts 9(2)(j) and 89(1). GDPR on its own shows no bias toward consent. But Art 89(1) lets Member States build such a bias into national law → source of divergence.

The middle of the spectrum: South Africa and the UK

South Africa — POPIA 2013. Information Regulator provisions in force 2014; main body not yet in force when written. Influenced by early GDPR drafts; complements health research ethics guidelines (fit with broad consent — consent for a wide range of future research — questioned). s 26 "special personal information": religious/philosophical beliefs, race/ethnic origin, trade union membership, political persuasion, health or sex life, biometrics, criminal behaviour → prohibited unless exemption. s 27 lists five exemptions: (1) data subject consented; (2) establishment, exercise or defence of a legal right/obligation; (3) obligation of international public law; (4) historical, statistical or research purposes; (5) information deliberately made public by data subject.

  • Research exemption s 27(1)(d) is conditional: applies only if either (a) purpose serves a public interest and processing is necessary for it, or (b) it's impossible or would involve disproportionate effort to ask for consent — plus guarantees that privacy not disproportionately affected. (Paper at one point says "Section 27(7)(d)" — slip for s 27(1)(d).) → "two-track system": public-interest research may rely on consent or exemption; research not in public interest must prioritise consent, may use exemption only if consent impractical. Consent favoured but not mandated.
  • Legislative history (contested balance): SA Law Reform Commission 2005 Discussion Paper proposed narrow exemption with four cumulative conditions (public interest, necessity, impracticability of obtaining explicit consent, safeguards). 2009 Final Report removed the exemption (citing World Medical Association, BMA, Canadian Medical Association on importance of consent); Government Bill 2009 followed. National Assembly committee later re-introduced it in watered-down form — conditions now alternative not cumulative; safeguards always required.

UK — DPA 2018. Gives effect to GDPR + implements Law Enforcement Directive. Schedule 1, para 4: research processing of sensitive data allowed only if (i) necessary for archiving, scientific/historical research or statistics; (ii) carried out per Art 89(1) as supplemented by s 19; (iii) in the public interest (third condition is new — GDPR requires public interest only for archiving, DPA 2018 for all three purposes). Act doesn't define public interest in research; ICO: exemption doesn't cover commercial research such as market research or customer satisfaction surveys. "Substantial public interest" is a separate basis (Art 9(2)(g), s 10(1)(b)).

  • Also two-track but stricter than POPIA: for research not in public interest there's no impracticability escape route → consent becomes privileged/only basis. s 19(3) requires ethics approval for research that leads to measures/decisions about a particular data subject.
  • Data protection isn't the only UK law: common-law duty of confidentiality (important but not absolute). England & Wales: Reg 5 Health Service (Control of Patient Information) Regulations 2002 and s 251 NHS Act 2006 (formerly s 60 Health and Social Care Act 2001) allow NHS patient data use without consent and without breaching confidence; governed by HRA under Care Act 2014 via NHS research ethics committees and the Confidentiality Advisory Group. Scotland: Public Benefit and Privacy Panel; Northern Ireland: Medical Directors of Health and Social Care Trusts decide case-by-case. Digital Economy Act 2017 (Part 5, ch 5, s 64) lets public authorities disclose personal info for research without consent. Crucially these set aside duty of confidentiality, not data protection — a lawful basis under GDPR/DPA 2018 still needed, and it isn't consent. Laws complement each other; DPA 2018 = baseline absent a lex specialis.

Ireland: strong presumption of consent

  • Health Research Regulations 2018, made under s 36 Irish DPA 2018, in force August 2018. Six elements: (1) mandatory suitable and specific measures (Reg 3(1)); (2) define health research (Reg 3(2)) through five broad types — normal/abnormal functioning; innovative diagnosis/treatment/prevention; improving diagnosis/treatment/rehabilitation/palliation/quality of life; health system efficiency; population health and its social/cultural/environmental/occupational/economic determinants; (3) consent declarations for new research (Reg 5); (4) transitional arrangements (Reg 6); (5) committee with right of appeal (Regs 7–13); (6) misc (Regs 14–16).
  • Heart: Reg 3(1)(e) requires explicit consent before the research begins, for specified health research, either in a particular area or more generally in that area/related one → works as a rebuttable presumption. Rebutted via Reg 5(1) carve-out: researcher may apply to the Health Research Consent Declaration Committee (HRCDC) for a declaration that the public interest in the research significantly outweighs the public interest in requiring explicit consent. Before applying: DPIA under Art 35(1) + research ethics approval. Application must set out: lawful basis and Art 9(2) condition; controllers and joint-controller responsibilities (Art 26); proof personal rather than anonymised data is necessary; no damage/distress; data minimised; no disclosure without law/consent; safeguards; DPO appointed; ethics approval; DPIA results (esp. on data linkage) and consultations with data subjects; reasons why consent will not be sought.
  • "May apply" sounds voluntary but in practice is the only way around explicit consent. Critics (Clarke et al.): harms retrospective chart reviews, biobanks, research involving people lacking capacity. HRCDC describes explicit consent as "the default position" and about "empowering the patient"; aims for "Transparency, Confidence and Trust".

The spectrum (side by side)

Regime Mechanism Consent privileged?
GDPR default Exemption from consent under Art 9(2)(j) + Art 89(1) safeguards; consent = one option Not at all — researchers have almost no incentive to choose it
POPIA / DPA 2018 Exemption for public interest or impracticability (POPIA); public interest (DPA 2018); controller makes the assessment Moderately
Ireland Requires significant public interest + consent-waiver declaration from independent third party Strongly

Authors accept simplification: Art 89(1) safeguards specified differently — more detailed in Ireland and UK than POPIA's "sufficient guarantees"; UK adds ethics approval (s 19(3)).

Core argument: the "consent misconception"

  • Research-ethics (informed) consent = powerful ethical-legal norm resting on respect, autonomy, dignity, bodily integrity. Art 7 ICCPR: "no one shall be subjected without his free consent to medical or scientific experimentation"; consent forms also serve an evidentiary function.
  • Data-processing consent = simply one lawful basis; not necessarily a legal or ethical obligation.
  • "Consent misconception" = assuming that because consent is the key norm in research ethics, it must also be the data-protection basis. Participants especially think that by consenting to take part they're consenting to data processing.
  • Authorities: GMC — implied consent works for confidentiality but unlikely enough under Art 6(1)(a) or to count as explicit consent under Art 9(2)(a) → doctors may rely on different justifications under the law of confidence and under GDPR. EDPB Opinion 3/2019 (Clinical Trials Regulation 536/2014): informed consent under CTR must not be confused with GDPR consent; CTR consent protects dignity and integrity under Arts 1 and 3 of the Charter (CFR), not data protection. EDPB also points to power imbalance in research (participants may be ill, disadvantaged, institutionally dependent) → consent often not freely given → other lawful bases should be used in most cases.
  • Two further problems if consent mandated: (1) definitions of valid consent differ between data protection and ethics, and across countries (what counts as "informed"; how "broad" broad consent can be) → harms international collaboration; (2) withdrawal (Art 7(3)) removes the lawful basis → complicates research repositories, whereas withdrawal in research ethics usually doesn't undo past research. Form design: Art 7(2) requires consent request clearly distinguishable from other matters → information sheets must separate consent to participation from consent to processing and name all parties relying on consent.

Normative position: "South Africa+"

  • Data-processing consent is still valuable — "manifestly stronger legal form" for autonomy over one's data and participation; gives control over body/bodily integrity (data is part of it); shows respect; creates communicative bond between controller and data subject. Consent should be privileged, but "in a moderate and rebuttable form".
  • Criticism of both ends: GDPR default favours researchers too much (they'll use other bases even when consent easy; broad research definition risks abuse by private entities; if consent isn't onerous, is ethically appropriate and methodologically sound, researchers should get it — esp. privately commissioned research without clear public interest). Ireland privileges consent too much — disproportionate, burdensome, dampens research, competitiveness, patient access to innovation. Authors prefer self-assessment with accountability to a regulator over third-party bureaucracy; reject Irish premises: consent isn't the only way to empower patients, research without consent isn't automatically unethical, and access rights don't depend on consent being the lawful basis.
  • UK approach better (controller decides public interest; ICO can intervene) = "co-regulation" — presumed trust rather than command-and-control (though public interest not always easy to show). Preferred model close to POPIA: consent privileged unless either (a) public interest (determined by controller) + necessity, or (b) consent impossible/disproportionate effort. Always guarantees against disproportionate privacy impact; onus on researcher to make an honest, informed determination, accountable to a regulator.
  • "South Africa+" = POPIA-style core + GDPR-style procedural/substantive safeguards: anonymisation/pseudonymisation encouraged; DPO required where core activities involve large-scale genomic/health data; DPIA for new technologies/high-risk processing; controllers transparent about why consent impracticable or public interest overrides; other duties (confidence, research ethics) kept distinct from data protection for conceptual clarity.

Conclusions, criticism, links

  • Conclusion: research-ethics consent and data-processing consent must be distinguished to avoid the consent misconception so participants are "under no illusions" about what they consent to. Data protection consent is "less powerful but should be privileged as well — to a degree", because it "symbolizes respect" and puts data subject on a "more symmetrical informational and communicative plane" with controller. GDPR and Ireland "pull too far on opposite ends": one risks infringing rights and lets researchers dodge consent, the other overly burdensome. Context should be assessed by the controller/researcher rather than a third party.
  • Strengths: clear comparative spectrum; useful concept of the consent misconception; balanced proposal. Weaknesses: model relies on controller self-assessment = the same trust in controllers that Najdi doubts; same conflict-of-interest problem as PIAs (Iwaya); "public interest" vague and self-declared; doesn't address secondary use, children, adults lacking capacity; POPIA not yet in force when written.
  • Links: counterweight to consent-centred readings (Planet49, Santos, Pilot, Lindegren) — consent not always the right basis. Power imbalance that stops consent being "freely given" mirrors Art 7(4) and the coupling issue Planet49 left open. Irish DPIAs connect to impact-assessment readings (R9/R10); withdrawal/erasure tensions connect to Custers & Vrabec and Najdi. Who should decide? Authors' controller self-assessment + regulator vs third-party committee (HRCDC) vs Custers & Vrabec's DPAs as trusted third parties. Kay & Terry link back: research-software example + ethics board feedback concern research-ethics consent.

R14. MEDZINI & EPSTEIN — "Privacy Governance from a Polycentric Perspective" (book chapter)

Background: from regulation to regulatory governance

  • Claim: privacy is not governed by the state alone — by many interdependent actors at many levels, with overlapping mandates and no final arbiter. In the authors' vocabulary privacy governance is polycentric (many centres of decision-making rather than one). Best seen by looking at regulatory intermediaries (actors between rule-makers and rule-takers).
  • Two contrasting case studies: formal, legal institutionalisation of the Data Protection Officer (DPO) in the EU, and informal governance of remote-teaching technology by educators in early COVID-19. Both display transscalarity and transsectorality, diffusion of knowledge and authority, fluidity.
  • Regulation (classic) = state "command and control" (state writes rules, enforces on regulated). Regulatory governance = plural forms of governing: government, industry, civil society and public share responsibility (Levi-Faur). Related scholarship: "regulatory state" (Majone 1994), "decentring regulation" (Black 2001), "post-regulatory state" (Scott 2004), "regulatory capitalism" (Levi-Faur 2005), new tools of public action (Salamon), "smart regulation" (Gunningham), audits (Power's The Audit Society 1999), "regulatory intermediation" (Abbott et al. 2017). Common thread: non-state and transnational actors have real regulatory impact at supranational, national, local levels; they're interconnected, interdependent and also compete for legitimacy, authority and power.

The polycentric framework (Scholte 2017 — first to apply it explicitly)

Seven features: transscalar (local/national/international at once); transsectoral (government, business, civil society); diffused (authority spread out); fluid (arrangements keep changing); overlapping mandates; ambiguous hierarchies; no final arbiter (several bodies claim responsibility; unclear who outranks whom; no one has last word).

  • Rests on Black's (2001) fragmentation of knowledge and power: no single actor has all knowledge for complex, dynamic "wicked" problems; power distributed so no actor controls all critical resources → breaks traditional public/private dichotomy; draws attention to how power and everyday mundane practices are recalibrated.
  • Two ways to study: institutional view (formal organisations, explicit rules, authority relations) and non-institutional (informal norms, practices, techniques). Together reveal "ordered chaos" (Koinova et al. 2021) — order without a central authority.
  • Actor categories: rule-makers and rule-takers (separate = second-party regulation, e.g. state regulating companies; same = first-party self-regulation — Black 1996); rule-beneficiaries (people whose interests rules protect — citizens, consumers, users; includes indirect beneficiaries like captured interest groups; consumers benefit from food-safety rules without being rule-takers); data subjects = "empowered beneficiaries" (rights to access, rectify, erase, restrict, port) — but "being empowered… does not mean these actors act upon their powers"; third-party regulation by regulatory intermediaries = individuals/organisations, formal or informal, with unique capacities, resources, expertise, responsibilities that rule-makers/rule-takers lack — auditors classic example (Power: firms publish own financial reports but need auditors to assure outsiders reliable). Focusing on intermediaries = a way to operationalise polycentrism.
  • Informational power (Braman 2009) underlies all other forms of power (instrumental, structural, symbolic); governing information = "decision-making with constitutive effects" — shapes the world it governs; happens via formal regulation and technology design; formally and informally.

Privacy governance and the research question

  • Polycentric thinking already runs through information policy: from 1980s liberalisation/privatisation of telecoms moved expertise outside the state, created strong private/transnational actors; internet brought informal regulation through design; WGIG 2005 defined internet governance as "the development and application by governments, the private sector and civil society, in their respective roles, of shared principles, norms, rules, decision-making procedures, and programmes that shape the evolution and use of the Internet" → debates on multistakeholderism, "ecosystem" (Hofmann), "networked governance" (Mueller), "co-regulation" (Marsden). Similar in cybersecurity governance (Mueller 2017) and platform governance (Facebook, Amazon, Apple, Microsoft, Google navigate many legal systems, lead standardisation, self-regulate → governance by and of platforms).
  • Privacy scholarship works in separate strands: legal (rights-based; informational self-determination — backbone of EU data protection, also in US law); regulatory (power of independent DPAs; limits of private self-regulation; strength of US FTC); economic (privacy calculus — Acquisti et al.); behavioural (privacy literacy as basis for individual agency). Missing: map of "loci of control" (where control over personal data actually sits).
  • Earlier implicitly polycentric work: Bennett & Raab (2003) — four groups of privacy instruments (transnational, legal, self-regulatory, technological); laws not dominant, regulators not necessarily pivotal; privacy governance = "a complex phenomenon that involves a plurality of actors and a range of methods of operation and coordination". Mayer-Schönberger (2010) criticised reliance on individuals to enforce own rights → learn from Europe's privacy professionals as information governance intermediaries. Bamberger & Mulligan, Privacy on the Ground (2015) (five countries): ambiguous legal requirements, activist regulators, stakeholder scrutiny (esp. US, Germany) empowered privacy professionals inside companies. Engineers have narrow perceptions of privacy (Waldman 2018; Ribak 2019).
  • Critics: Koinova et al. — polycentrism neglects structural context in which authority is assigned; Julie Cohen (2012) — US "new privacy governance" ill-equipped for surveillance capitalism, rooted in ideology that "systematically downplays the need to hold market actors accountable" and relies too heavily on private regulation and notice and choice; Bennett & Raab (2020) — governance may be reverting to a simple two-party relationship between regulator and regulated organisation. Research question (paraphrasing van Eeten & Mueller 2013): "where is the governance in privacy governance and why does it matter?"

Case one: the DPO in the EU (formal intermediary)

DPOs = information governance intermediaries placed inside organisations: interpret law, manage risks, strengthen accountability of rule-takers. Three milestones (a polycentric institution built slowly and through political struggle):

  1. German BDSG 1977: independent internal compliance officers (betriebliche Datenschutzbeauftragte) mandatory in organisations above a certain size; enforcement left to the Länder supervisory authorities. Professional DPO associations followed: Germany 1977, Austria 1983, UK 1993 (where the role had no legal recognition).
  2. 1990s Data Protection Directive (95/46): DPO as a political compromise in the fight against "data havens". French-led coalition wanted power concentrated in DPAs (extensive notification/registration of processing); German delegation wanted a diffused approach (organisations appoint own internal supervisor, or DPAs delegate tasks to DPOs). Council Presidency brokered: organisation exempt from notifying DPA if it appointed and registered a DPO. In practice weak at national level — DPOs confined to national law, kept public registries, notified DPAs of risks; Member States had wide discretion; Belgium and UK legislated but never issued implementing decrees; most DPAs treated DPOs as their "eyes and ears" or a sanction tool (could strike officers from registries) → polycentric recognition of DPOs stunted.
  3. GDPR (negotiated after Lisbon Treaty): Commission chose a directly applicable regulation; proposed mandatory DPOs alongside simpler notification, more managerial responsibility, self-regulatory mechanisms. Council (via DAPIX working party) resisted: questioned mandatory DPOs / more granular / which entitlements; opposed ~50 powers to adopt delegated and implementing acts. Parliament sided with Commission on DPOs, with Council against the delegated powers. Compromise December 2015; GDPR adopted April 2016. Result — much less Member State discretion: GDPR requires a DPO (internal or contracted externally) for set categories of organisation; Member States can add categories but not subtract. Tasks: monitor compliance; advise managers and staff; indicate TOMs needed; extra tasks allowed if no conflict of interest. DPO must be proficient and is protected from dismissal or penalty. No formal degree or certification required (DPAs such as France's CNIL accredit training courses but cannot make certification mandatory). Assessment: diffused and polycentric but not polycentric in the ideal sense (Scholte) because hierarchy remains: DPAs arbitrate non-standard DPO decisions; courts are final arbiter in a crisis. Meanwhile DPOs depend on non-state actors (professional networks) for knowledge, credibility, proficiency, defence of professional interests.

Case two: educators & remote teaching in COVID-19 (informal intermediary)

  • Spring 2020: >1.2 billion pupils and >200 million university students lost in-person classes; rapid tech adoption exposed normally hidden governance — "crises reveal implicit governance structures". (Evidence mostly from WEIRD countries.)
  • Concerns: Institutional — schools lost control over student data to commercial platforms → power shifted from state to platform providers. Social/pedagogical — home and professional life collapsed into each other → "privacy boundary turbulence" (disruption of usual lines between private and public spheres). Role of street-level bureaucrats (front-line officials — here teachers — who apply policy in daily practice) and epistemic communities (networks sharing expertise) became crucial.
  • Responses shaped by path dependency and national culture: Israel — teachers, pupils, parents already used WhatsApp with little attention to privacy → main teaching channel. Germany — higher privacy sensitivity; teachers sometimes had only pupils' postal addresses; using WhatsApp seen as an "act of rebellion". Institutions chose different platforms: closed commercial (Blackboard, Canvas, Google Classroom) accepting/ignoring privacy risks vs self-hosted open-source (Moodle); later Zoom, Webex, Teams, BigBlueButton provided centrally. US: Data Protection Addenda agreed at level of each institution substituted for central regulation. Estonia started with GDPR-friendlier BigBlueButton, yet Zoom came to dominate.
  • Decisive factor = agency of individual teachers: learned tools from personal/professional networks, not formal sources → "hyperlocal, authentic expertise"; sometimes required extra tools exposing students to third-party data collection; could enforce or circumvent official policy (e.g. whether students had to keep cameras on in Israeli schools). → knowledge, expertise, agency, resources and authority distributed across many actors; reveals "informal institutionalisation" (individual agency + social structures + socio-technical systems = lasting arrangements); crisis decisions create path dependency.

What the two cases show — 3 polycentric markers

  1. Transscalarity & transsectorality: DPOs (formal delegated authority) and educators (choose among commercial products; develop norms-in-practice on surveillance and video) act outside government but in the "shadow of hierarchy" of regulators and technology designers (discretion while knowing more powerful actors set the frame and could intervene). Interpretive flexibility (room to read rules/tools their own way) creates tension with top-down order. Other actors (engineers, certification and monitoring bodies) also belong.
  2. Diffusion of knowledge & authority: both rely on epistemic communities and professional networks. DPOs must demonstrate qualities through associations, certifications, academia; some associations also act as interest groups. Educators rely on peers and respond to parent pressure. A new "network effect": epistemic networks compensate for literacy deficits, information overload, missing authority. Call for research on isomorphism of networks (tendency to become alike) and their politicisation.
  3. Dynamism & fluidity: DPO role formalised over decades alongside technology change; COVID-19 compressed change (Israeli universities now accept recording of lectures — a new norm that outlasted the emergency). Research must be longitudinal and study crises/disasters. Authors call two cases "tip of the iceberg" and "proof of concept". Future: governance of and by algorithms, mapping networks that produce norms/language/practice, systematic comparative analysis across cultures/systems.

Criticisms & why it matters

Strength: reframes who protects privacy — not law vs individual but a landscape of rule-makers, rule-takers, rule-beneficiaries and intermediaries; supplies useful vocabulary (Scholte's seven features); historically grounded account of the DPO (BDSG 1977 → Directive compromise → GDPR). Weaknesses: rests on two illustrative cases not systematic study; COVID case relies on emergent evidence from WEIRD countries; authors admit hierarchy persists (DPAs and courts have final say) — sits uneasily with "no final arbiter"; doesn't fully answer critics (Cohen: fails to hold market actors accountable; Bennett & Raab: return to two-party regulation; polycentrism ignores structural context in which authority is assigned). Links: adds intermediaries (DPOs, educators, professional networks) to the law vs design vs technology debate. Intermediaries appear throughout: DPOs required under DPC guide and Ireland's Health Research Regulations; Iwaya discusses DPO roles and warns of PIA consultant conflicts of interest; consent management platforms (Santos); LLM readers and consumer associations (Pałka); the federation that brought Planet49; Custers & Vrabec's DPAs as trusted third parties; Dove & Chen's HRCDC; processors under Art 28. "Empowered beneficiaries often don't act on their rights" links to the privacy paradox, to McDonald & Cranor (time to read notices is itself a cost), to Lindegren's wrong mental models, and to Kay & Terry's "all EULAs are basically the same". Chapter's answer to its own question: the governance in privacy governance lies not only in statutes and regulators but in the many intermediaries who interpret, apply and sometimes bend the rules in practice.

R15. KAY & TERRY (2010) — "Textured Agreements: Re-envisioning Electronic Consent"

The problem: agreements nobody reads

  • Fewer than 2% of people read license agreements when installing software. Matters because EULAs (end-user license agreements) can contain terms of direct concern: data collection, installation of extra software (DRM, ad-serving) affecting privacy, security, user experience. People skip because reading doesn't serve their immediate goal (use the software). "Software agreements" = privacy policies, EULAs, consent agreements for research software → paper speaks directly to electronic consent.
  • Who wants better agreements: proprietary firms building trust (Aviary puts a summary in a side column); free/open-source producers explaining philosophy (Free Software Foundation's GNU GPL); researchers distributing software (ingimp project) with an ethical duty of informed consent (without better agreement participants may miss risks like sensitive data being made public); governments considering legislation should base it on demonstrably better processes. Aim: find a way of presenting agreements that people actually read.

Earlier solutions and their limits

Approach Limit
Single-screen summaries / layered notices (Good et al.; from medical consent forms and financial privacy notices) — summary layer in front of full text; cut spyware installs Summaries omit terms and may reduce reading of the full agreement
Privacy "nutrition labels" (Kelley et al. 2009, tested on P3P sites) — data uses and opt-ins as a table Unclear whether they generalise to other agreement types
JITCTAs (Patrick & Kenny) — small agreements at the moment data is collected Only work if agreement can be split into context-specific pieces without overloading users
Polymorphic dialogs (Brustoloni et al.) — reorder buttons/temporarily disable them so users can't follow learned click paths Designed for short warnings, not long dense texts
Enhanced text in medical consent (Campbell et al.) — better headers/pictures helped low-literacy readers; video did worse Subjects were told to read
  • Most current solutions remain plain text with minimal typography → opportunity to apply visual design. (Footnote: layered notices could be multiple progressive layers, but untested.)

Designing a textured agreement

  • Came from a formative evaluation (early exploratory study) with 21 subjects; dozens of low-fidelity prototypes (paper and computer mock-ups with supplementary videos, illustrations, enhanced text, inspired by advertising, technical communication, comics). Prototypes shown inside mock installers using deception (participants told they were there to "evaluate usability"); order varied. Key insight: videos and separate illustrations caused confusion or disinterest; visually redesigned text worked best. Users felt company had made "an explicit effort to communicate".
  • Design theory drawn on: Tufte — "separation" and "layering"; Kress & van Leeuwen — "salience", "framing", hierarchy of information, composition enabling non-linear reading; Wright (1977) — headings make skimming easier and help readers integrate information; design influences pacing (speed/rhythm of reading).
  • Textured agreement = agreement visually redesigned using typography, factoids, vignettes and iconic symbols, without changing the underlying text. Two high-level goals:
    1. Clear information hierarchy — give visual weight to what matters from the user's perspective (e.g. automatic data collection) rather than standard liability clauses. Counters impression agreements are irrelevant, unskimmable, long and hard; readers can enter at a high level (headers, factoids) then drop to detail nearby → "skimmability" lowers barrier to reading further.
    2. Capture and retain attention through visual variety. Overuse kills the effect: users read only 3–4 warning boxes per document → progressive exposure to new elements and balanced techniques across the document. Hierarchy + variety create accessible pace; plain text signals "long and arduous".
  • Four techniques: (1) Typographic manipulation (bold key terms, headers, bullets = first level of hierarchy); (2) Factoids (replace pull-quotes, impractical for dry legal text; written for the purpose; either show consequences of terms (e.g. most popular OS among users — hints OS is being collected) or humorous hooks like "98% of users don't read consent agreements" which acknowledge tedium and signal this agreement is different); (3) Vignettes (comic-like mini-narratives of the user using the software, e.g. a user clicking the contrast tool with caption "we're collecting COMMAND NAMES!" — creates personal relevance); (4) Iconic symbols (warning icons, coloured boxes marking sensitive terms; used sparingly to keep "arousal strength" — Wogalter & Laughery). Factoids kept germane (relevant) + add a human touch. No single template — a set of strategies; templates published online.

Experiment 1 — does texture capture attention?

  • Between-subjects (each participant saw one version); used deception (participants didn't know agreements were being studied). Five conditions: control (plain text); minimal (pretty but didn't reinforce content — tests whether any effect is mere novelty); moderate (balanced: scholarly heading font, warning boxes for 3 clauses, relevant factoids); heavy (as many techniques as possible); summary (one-page summary in Good et al. style before installer).
  • Task: "you got a digital camera" — download, install and use three image apps ("Photo Time", "Photo Job", "Photo Desk") from a mock web page to choose best for rotating images. Agreements derived from real ones; each stated data would be collected, with varying details. Verbal consent first (written consent primed attention in formative tests); debrief and paper consent after. Order counterbalanced → 5 × 6 design. Windows XP in VMware, internet disabled, snapshots; custom Java installer mimicking Windows logged screen times, scrolling, cancellations. Measures: first-run time on installer screens; maximum scroll into the agreement. 90 recruited, 6 dropped → 84 (17 per condition, 16 in heavy); 43 female, 41 male, age 17–47 (mean 24); expertise 3.4/5; $10 coffee gift certificate; 3 outliers (>3 SD) dropped.
  • Results: mean first-run time on agreement screen: heavy 39.8 s (SD 39); moderate 35.6 s (SD 39.2); summary 10.3 s; control 7.1 s. ANOVA significant: F(4,76) = 5.65, p < 0.01. Tukey: heavy > control (p < 0.01); heavy > summary (p = 0.026); moderate > control (p = 0.026); trend moderate > summary (p = 0.063). Minimal did NOT significantly improve on control → effect of texturing isn't just novelty. Scrolling differed significantly (F = 3.96, p = 0.014): heavy beat control and summary. Self-reported reading higher for moderate than control (p < 0.05); reading habits (never noticed/never/rarely/often/always) significant factor. In summary condition many participants cancelled at the summary screen (as in Good et al.) → summaries do communicate. Total pre-install time only a trend (p = 0.089); scrolling across all runs significant (moderate and heavy beat summary). Visual appeal: heavy and moderate rated higher than control and summary; minimal higher than summary.
  • Comments: heavy participant: "It got me to read them… I NEVER read them. Big letters, organized points, and cartoons help." Moderate: headings "placed similar to a newspaper". But heavy was also "somewhat obnoxious" and "over-the-top" → moderate may strike the better balance.

Experiment 2 — does texture improve retention?

  • Two conditions: control (plain text from Exp 1) vs refined textured design (more vignettes/sensationalism than moderate but more judicious than heavy). One app, "Program A"; participants stopped right after agreement screen (no controls to skip ahead); then 8-question open quiz ("According to Program A's license agreement…": which image characteristics are recorded, whom to contact); each question 2 pts (max 16; 2 complete, 1 partial). 28 participants (17 female, 11 male, 19–31, native English).
  • Results: textured spent 53.6 s vs control 19.7 s (F(1,26) = 7.54, p < 0.05); scrolled to max 71.4% vs 47.4% of document (p < 0.05). Median quiz score 4 (IQR 5.74) textured vs 0 (IQR 4) control; ANOVA on ranks p = 0.052 — borderline, not conventionally significant. Quiz score strongly correlated with reading time (r = 0.83, p < 0.0001) → extra time translated into knowledge. Textured users read/skimmed whole document more slowly; control users jumped to the end. Comments: control — "All of the EULAs are basically the same"; textured — "I read more due to the diagram stating that 98% of the users don't read the License Agreement" (hooks pulled readers in).

Conclusions

  • Across both experiments texturing raised attention substantially: time on agreement rose from ~7 s to ~37 s (36–40 s depending on design) in Exp 1; +34 s (20 → 54 s) with better recall in Exp 2. Only a subset of techniques used → room for improvement. Works on the agreement itself, not on an auxiliary document.
  • Summaries — more cautious verdict: they are read (hold attention 18 s longer than welcome screens, p < 0.0001) but reduce reading of the full agreement. Echoes McDonald et al. (2009) — users less accurate with summaries when answers weren't in the summary layer. "Summaries… do so at the cost of reading the full agreement." → caution about partitioning consent (splitting into separate phases).
  • Ethics board feedback very positive (getting participants to read consent forms is a known struggle) but noted heavy designs may be hard for seniors. Future work: long-term habituation/desensitisation, other demographics (seniors), real-world agreements, templates for easier adoption.

Strengths, criticisms, connections

Strength: changes only the form of the agreement, not the legal text → compatible with existing law; realistic deception design; combines behavioural measures (time, scrolling) with a comprehension measure. Weaknesses: small samples (Exp 2 only 28; quiz result p = 0.052); student participants; saw agreements only once (habituation untested); even improved comprehension is low (median 4/16); "sensationalism" and humour could become manipulative — the same design power used for dark patterns (Santos); someone must curate what counts as "important" → choice left to the designer. Debates:

  • Form vs content: Kay & Terry fix the form; Pałka says content of vague policies is the real problem → texturing a vague policy just highlights its vagueness.
  • Reading cost: Kay & Terry make people read more (time goes up) vs McDonald & Cranor (time is the cost; 5× increase in reading time per agreement multiplies the national bill) → trade-off between engagement and burden, confirmed by Lindegren (more engaging designs take longer): drag-and-drop +36 s vs checkboxes; textured agreements ~+30 s vs plain text.
  • Summaries & layered notices (scoping review, McDonald & Cranor recommend) — K&T show summaries displace full reading → caution for Santos's dashboards/icons and nutrition labels. Their answer to comprehensiveness vs comprehensibility = fix the form of the full text rather than shorten it.
  • Meaning of "clear affirmative action" (Art 4(11), Recital 32, Planet49): ticking an unticked box is enough in law, yet plain-text agreements get only ~7 s of attention → legal test checks form of the action; meaningful consent needs attention-supporting design. With Lindegren, shows design can raise attention/recall, but gains modest; control participant's "all EULAs are basically the same" shows users assume agreements are interchangeable. Dove & Chen: sometimes consent isn't even the right legal basis. Ingimp example + ethics board feedback link to Dove & Chen's research-ethics consent.

2. The master debate: "Can notice-and-consent be saved?" — arguments for a 25-mark answer

Thesis: Notice-and-consent survives only if (a) the cost of self-management is reduced or shifted to collective bodies, AND (b) controllers are technically/legally constrained after consent; law, design and technology are complementary, with Art. 25 GDPR (data protection by design & default) as the bridge.

6. Common traps

  • Planet49 did not rule on bundling (Art 7(4)); it required info on "consequences of any consent he or she might give" — not "of refusing".
  • Santos et al. used 3 of Nielsen's 10 heuristics; no new user testing; DPA decisions on colours conflict (Danish/Belgian 113/2024/Austrian vs Belgian 161/2023 and LG Passau).
  • Pałka: GPT-4 never hallucinated on mock policy, but CoT prompt made it worse; a typo labels the consent question "Q6" (actually Q4).
  • McDonald & Cranor: 244 h in results vs 201 h in conclusion; skimming figure = first-question time (6.3 min point).
  • Najdi: framework claims "fully supported" without implementation, while holding others to implementation/evaluation standard (asymmetry).
  • Iwaya: prevalence ≠ importance; 11 studies from one book.
  • Wairimu: "case study" often = use case (validation not evaluation); Wairimu = 39 studies, Iwaya = 45.
  • C&V: type depends on how obtained, not content; incorrect data is still personal data; group profile isn't personal data until applied to an individual.
  • Lindegren: recall difference not significant; DAD took longer but had highest SUS.
  • Dove & Chen: POPIA s 27(1)(d) (paper's "27(7)(d)" is a slip); POPIA not yet fully in force when written.
  • Kay & Terry: Exp 2 result p = 0.052 (borderline); heavy design also "obnoxious"; summaries displace full reading.
  • Medzini & Epstein: DPO system is polycentric but not ideal-type (hierarchy remains: DPAs/courts).
