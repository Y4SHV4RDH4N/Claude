1. WARREN & BRANDEIS, "The Right to Privacy" (1890)

4 Harv. L. Rev. 193 (15 Dec 1890). Epigraph: Willes J. in Millar v Taylor → judges can recognise new rights without legislation.

Bottom line: common law already has a right "to be let alone" (Cooley's phrase), protected "in disguise" via property/contract/confidence. True basis = "inviolate personality", not property. New tech (instantaneous photos) + gossip press → recognise as tort. Limited by public interest, privilege, consent. Remedies: damages, limited injunction; criminal law needs legislation.

Context: Kodak/instant photos (surreptitious); "what is whispered in the closet shall be proclaimed from the house-tops"; yellow press; E.L. Godkin; NY portraits case (fn 13). Backstory (Warren annoyed at press; "daughter's wedding" story doubtful†). ⚠ Roessler wrongly calls them "Justices" — in 1890 they were Boston law partners; Brandeis joined SC in 1916.

Argument step by step

  1. Common law grows: physical (trespass) → "spiritual nature, feelings, intellect"; life → "right to enjoy life, the right to be let alone"; property → "every form of possession, intangible". Judges protect emotions "without the interposition of the legislature."
  2. The need: photos + newspapers "invaded the sacred precincts of private and domestic life"; gossip has become a trade; mental harm > physical. Social harm: "Triviality destroys at once robustness of thought and delicacy of feeling."
  3. Why defamation doesn't fit: defamation = injury to reputation (external); no remedy for hurt feelings (damnum absque injuria).
  4. IP is really privacy: each person decides "to what extent his thoughts, sentiments, emotions shall be communicated to others"; lost only on publication by author; independent of method/value (casual letter/diary = "most valuable poem").
  5. "Property" can't explain cases: diary (protected = "not the intellectual product, but the domestic occurrence"); Prince Albert v Strange (1849) (royal etchings; even a catalogue restrained; Cottenham: "privacy is the right invaded"); Wyatt v Wilson (1820); gems-catalogue example.
  6. CONCLUSION KEY QUOTE: "not the principle of private property, but that of an inviolate personality."
  7. All forms of expression: "writing, conduct, conversation, attitudes, or facial expression"; labour/deliberateness/value/intent-to-publish all abandoned.
  8. Contract & confidence inadequate: | Case | Facts | Basis | |---|---|---| | Abernethy v Hutchinson 1825 | Surgeon's lectures in The Lancet | Breach of confidence | | Prince Albert v Strange 1849 | Royal etchings | Property + trust/confidence/contract | | Tuck v Priester 1887 | Extra copies of picture | Breach of contract | | Pollard v Photographic Co 1888 | Photographer sold lady's photo | Implied contract + confidence; "taken on the sly"? | | Yovatt v Winyard 1820 | Employee copied recipes | Breach of trust; no distinction from stranger | Tech point: photography once needed "sitting" → contract/trust; surreptitious photography → "contract and trust are inadequate" → "law of tort." Stranger problem → rights "as against the world" (in rem).

6 limitations | # | Limitation | Detail | |---|---|---| | 1 | Public/general interest | "modest and retiring individual" vs "would-be congressman"; no legitimate connection to fitness for public office | | 2 | Privileged communications | courts, legislatures, reports | | 3 | Oral publication | probably no redress w/o special damage | | 4 | Ceases on publication by/with consent | private/restricted circulation ≠ publication (early contextual integrity) | | 5 | Truth no defence | injury to privacy not character | | 6 | Absence of malice no defence | like trespass | Remedies: damages (all cases); injunction (limited); criminal needs legislation. Closing: "front entrance… back door to idle or prurient curiosity."

Key concepts: right to be let alone (Cooley) · inviolate personality · damnum absque injuria · rights in rem · control over communication (→ Westin). Legacy†: Roberson (NY 1902, rejected; led to Civil Rights Law §§50–51) · Pavesich (Ga 1905, first to recognise) · Prosser 1960: intrusion, public disclosure, false light, appropriation (§652) · Brandeis' Olmstead dissent → Katz · India: Puttaswamy; R. Rajagopal 1994. Critiques: vague/broad · too narrow (press/disclosure; ignores aggregation, secondary use, surveillance) · free speech · elitist/class-based · gendered ("a woman's face") · individualistic (no structural regulation) · public/private dichotomy · only dignitary harm. Strengths: tech-sensitive; non-pecuniary harm; anti-commodification; social harm of gossip.

2. ROESSLER, "Three Dimensions of Privacy" (2018)

Handbook of Privacy Studies, pp. 137–141. Philosopher, Amsterdam. Bottom line: privacy = autonomy/freedom; decisional, informational, local — "dimensions, not realms or spaces". Social/democratic value: "Threats to privacy, therefore, are always also threats to democracy." Autonomy = "(part-)author of her own biography."

Decisional (privacy of actions): protects decisions about how to live; origin Roe v Wade 410 US 113 (1973) (Blackmun: privacy "broad enough to encompass a woman's decision whether or not to terminate her pregnancy"). Feminist theory: sexual self-determination; privacy of the body (Gatens); harassment; sexual orientation. Intimate decisions = "authentic identity". Extends to public space: others owe "restraint, inattention, reserve, and indifference" ("none of your business"), subject to renegotiation. India: Navtej Johar, X v Principal Secretary†. Caveat: Dobbs overruled Roe†; MacKinnon†.

Informational: from W&B; threat = Big Data/state surveillance; control over self-presentation; people regulate relationships and roles; "If everyone knew everything about everyone else, differentiated relations and self-presentations would no longer be possible, and nor would autonomy." BVerfG Census judgment 1983 (BVerfGE 65, 1 [43]) → informational self-determination. Relational privacy: Fried 1968, Rachels 1975. Snowden used PETs. Links: GDPR control model, Puttaswamy, Goffman†, Nissenbaum.

Local: home, "own four walls"; not natural but constructed from liberal idea of withdrawal. (a) solitude/being-for-oneself, body (Woolf, Orwell†); (b) family relationships ("take a break from roles"). Feminist critique: division of labour, domestic violence, "pre-political space". Response: privacy of home justified only insofar as it secures equal autonomy (Honneth, Rawls, Okin†).

Conclusion: Snowden → "constitutive relevance for democracy"; Simitis 1987; post-9/11; presumption of innocence endangered: mass surveillance → "every democratic subject into a potentially guilty object". Final line: "Democracy relies upon citizens who value their autonomy, both in public and in private."

Evaluation: + unifies; escapes public/private; takes feminism seriously; individual+social. − autonomy-centrism; control theory poor for Big Data (Nissenbaum: appropriate flows); overlaps with liberty (Thomson); factual slips (W&B "Justices", "constitutional"); Western liberal framing; thin on remedies.

3. SOLOVE, "A Taxonomy of Privacy" (2006), 154 U. Pa. L. Rev. 477

Bottom line: "Privacy is a concept in disarray." Map activities causing problems: 16 harms, 4 groups. Family resemblance (Wittgenstein). Law's secrecy paradigm → "no balancing ever takes place." Aim: identify problems so they can be balanced — not that privacy always wins.

Vagueness quotes: Borges; McCarthy; BeVier ("chameleon-like"); A. Miller; Gerety ("protean"); Scheppele; J.J. Thomson. Six unlike examples: rape victim's name (Florida Star), reporters in home (Dietemann), X-ray devices, thermal imaging (Kyllo), list of 5m elderly incontinent women, GeoCities sold data.

Why not Prosser? tort-only; outdated; catalogues existing law. Others: Westin (solitude/intimacy/anonymity/reserve), Gormley (areas of law), Kang (space/choice/info flow), DeCew.

Method (8 points): (1) privacy is social — "relief from a range of kinds of social friction"; (2) focus on activities (privacy equation = harmful activity + private matter; he covers only first half); (3) consent (but Cohen/Schwartz: boilerplate = "legal fiction"); (4) not normative about outcome; (5) family resemblance; (6) dignitary vs architectural harm — dignitary = injury to feelings/reputation (disclosure, exposure, distortion); architectural = structural risk "like pollution" (↑ risk of harm; upsets power balance → chilling effect, Laird v Tatum); (7) not merely individual — constitutive privacy (Dewey, Post "rules of civility", Cohen, Schwartz, Gavison); (8) model: collect → process → disseminate; invasions go toward individual.

THE TAXONOMY

Group Harm Definition
A. Collection 1 Surveillance watching/listening/recording
2 Interrogation pressuring to divulge
B. Processing 3 Aggregation combining data
4 Identification linking info to individuals
5 Insecurity carelessness protecting stored info
6 Secondary use new purpose without consent
7 Exclusion no notice/input on records
C. Dissemination 8 Breach of confidentiality breaking promise
9 Disclosure true info affecting judgment of character
10 Exposure nudity, grief, bodily functions
11 Increased accessibility amplifying access
12 Blackmail threat to disclose
13 Appropriation using identity for another's aims
14 Distortion false/misleading info
D. Invasion 15 Intrusion disturbs tranquility/solitude
16 Decisional interference govt interference in private decisions

Mnemonic: Collect (S,I) → Process AIISE → Disseminate B D E I B A D → Invade (I,D).

A. Collection

Surveillance: history (Peeping Tom; wiretap law: Olmstead 1928 → Katz 1967 → Title III 1968 → ECPA 1986). Harms: self-censorship/chilling; social control (CCTV "friendly eye in the sky"); Cohen: choices "toward the bland and mainstream"; Benn (covert = wrong; Solove rejects: "awareness" affects freedom); Panopticon (Bentham/Foucault); even covert = architectural (FBI & MLK). Law's failure = where not effects: protected private places (Kyllo, Karo, Hamberger) vs public places not (Florida v Riley, Dow, US v Knotts) = secrecy paradigm; partial exception Nader v GM. Westin; Douglas J.: "Monitoring… kills free discourse." Interrogation: pressure; 5th Am.; census outcry; harms: coercion, chills association (HUAC, Barenblatt), distortion (Brooks, Dostoevsky); unlike surveillance occurs with awareness. Protections unsystematic (Shelton, Baird, ADA, evidentiary privileges); 5th narrow (use immunity; Ullmann; no parent–child privilege — Lewinsky).

B. Processing

Aggregation: "the whole becomes greater than the parts" (portrait); 1965 Federal Data Center/1974 FEDNET scrapped. Harms: upsets expectations (dignitary); dossier / "digital person" (architectural); distortion (reductive; → profiling/ADM). Law: Reporters Committee 1989 (rap sheets; practical obscurity); but Megan's Law cases (Russell, Paul P.) = no justification offered, "no balancing took place". Identification: HEW 1973 (SUI/SSN). Benefits: fraud ↓, accountability. Not inherently demeaning; harms: "informational baggage" (B v France; Gattaca), expressive identification (stigma), ↑ govt power (Soviet passports), destroys anonymity (Publius; Talley; McIntyre), Hiibel (Stevens: "A name can provide the key"). Harm = linking marker to data stream. India: Aadhaar. Insecurity: identity theft (≈2 yrs/200 hrs); law: Whalen v Roe, FIPs, statutes, liquidated damages (but Doe v Chao); courts reluctant (Earls). "Insecurity is the injury of being placed in a weakened state." Links: GDPR Art 32/82; DPDP s 8(5) ₹250 cr. Secondary use: 1977 computer matching; HEW FIPs → purpose specification. Harms: thwarts expectations; resembles breach of confidentiality; Schwartz "asymmetry of knowledge"; fear/powerlessness; architectural (data out of context). Links: GDPR Art 5(1)(b); DPDP s 6(1), s 7(b) maternity illustration. Exclusion: FIPs (no secret systems; access; correct) → transparency & participation. Architectural (↓ accountability); "powerlessness and frustration". Law: fiduciary analogy (Meinhard v Salmon; Moore); GLBA opt-out fails. Links: GDPR Arts 13–16; Srikrishna fiduciary; DPDP s 11(2).

C. Dissemination

Breach of confidentiality: McCormick v England; no "highly offensive" requirement (disclosure tort = content; breach = source/relationship); "victim has been betrayed"; Simonsen, Tarasoff. Third-party doctrine (Miller 1976, Smith 1979) rests on secrecy paradigm; England's breach of confidence (Campbell†); India: Carpenter†. Disclosure: §652D; critics Volokh (censorship), Posner (concealment); Solove's justifications (autonomy, democracy, security — Remsburg v Docusearch [Amy Boyer murdered], distorts judgment, "prisoner of recorded past" → RTBF). Secrecy-paradigm cases: Cefalu, Penwell, Sipple (outed), Duran; contrast Times Mirror. Strahilevitz: harm = "spreading information beyond expected boundaries". Exposure: DeMay v Roberts, NARA v Favish; deeply primordial attributes; not character; dignity "ability to transcend one's animal nature" (Elias, Miller); no separate tort; Graham (dress) protected, McNamara (soccer player) not. Increased accessibility: "difference in quantity becomes difference in quality"; Walls; Reporters Committee. Link: DPDP s 3(c)(ii) public-data exclusion; Google Spain†. Blackmail: history; theories table (Posner, Anderson & Block, Brown, Epstein, Gordon [Kantian], McAdams) vs Solove: POWER/domination ("not in the disclosure… but in the control"); analogy to assault; Hedda Gabler. Appropriation: Roberson (Abigail, 25,000 flour ads) → NY §§50–51; §652C; rationales dignity (Bloustein) vs property (Prosser/right of publicity); Solove: freedom & self-presentation — Pavesich ("a slave"), Gray J. "unwanted notoriety", "authorship of her self-narrative", better word "exploitation". Links: deepfakes; Bachchan/Kapoor†. Distortion: defamation, false light (§652E), accuracy rights (FIPs, OECD, EU Directive Art 6). Reputation = "currency through which we interact" (Post); Iago in Othello.

D. Invasion

Intrusion: castle (Semayne), Cooley 1868, §652B; Galella v Onassis; spam/telemarketing; solitude critique (Aristotle) vs reply (Arendt, Thoreau, Montaigne); personal space in public (Altman, Goffman "territories of the self"; Shulman, Stressman, Sanders v ABC). Decisional interference: Griswold, Eisenstadt, Roe, Pierce; objection (Tribe/Henkin: autonomy); Solove: linked to disclosure (Whalen), insecurity, exposure, intrusion (Botsford, Stanley, Lawrence: "Freedom extends beyond spatial bounds"), blackmail. India: Puttaswamy, Navtej Johar, Suchita Srivastava†.

Conclusion: Prosser "far too narrow… out-of-date"; no common denominator; privacy problems like pollution (by-products); courts' pitfalls (public domain, public monitoring, public place, no intimate details, no new data); regulate activities of people/businesses/govt not technology. Evaluation: + pragmatic, exposes secrecy paradigm, architectural harm fits GDPR, maps onto DP law (collection=minimisation; aggregation/ID=profiling; insecurity=security+breach; secondary use=purpose limitation; exclusion=transparency/access; distortion=accuracy; disclosure=RTBF). − no unifying theory; over-inclusive; "private matters" under-theorised; US-centric; consent; overlaps; dated; individual-harm framing.

4. Comparison, debates, exam skeletons

Mapping: Roessler decisional ↔︎ Solove decisional interference; informational ↔︎ collection/processing/dissemination; local ↔︎ intrusion/surveillance in private places; democracy ↔︎ architectural harm/constitutive privacy. Debates: (1) essence — unitary (W&B, Westin, Gavison, Roessler) vs reductionist (Thomson; Tribe/Henkin) vs pluralist (Solove) → combine Solove (what) + Roessler (why) + proportionality; (2) public/private; (3) privacy vs free speech; (4) dignity vs property vs liberty (Whitman's two cultures†); (5) individual vs social; (6) feminist critiques; (7) technology & law (AI lessons). Applying Solove (technique): Collection? Processing? Dissemination? Invasion? For each: dignitary/architectural? secrecy paradigm? balance. Worked example: Indian welfare app links Aadhaar to health/benefits data, shares with police → identification, aggregation, secondary use (DPDP s 7(b)–(c)), exclusion (s 11(2)), insecurity, decisional interference (chilling, Whalen), architectural → Roessler → Puttaswamy proportionality. Likely Qs: (1) "Concept in disarray" · (2) W&B inviolate personality vs property · (3) Solove taxonomy useful without theory? · (4) secrecy paradigm · (5) Roessler autonomy · (6) surveillance harmful even if covert/public? · (7) disclosure vs exposure vs breach · (8) value beyond individual. Traps: W&B not judges; "let alone" is Cooley's; Prosser made 4 torts; Solove = activities not "private things"; Solove rejects Benn; identification not inherently demeaning; exposure ≠ disclosure; blackmail = power; appropriation = freedom/self-presentation ("exploitation").
