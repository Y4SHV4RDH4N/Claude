0. THE BIG PICTURE (read this first)

  • Why roles matter: the GDPR allocates responsibility by role. Whether an entity is a controller, joint controller, processor, recipient or third party decides who must comply, who can be fined, and against whom data subjects can exercise rights.
  • Accountability anchor: Art 5(2) (controller responsible for, and must be able to demonstrate, compliance with Art 5(1) principles) → Art 24 (appropriate TOMs) → Art 28 (rules on engaging processors).
  • Four framing points (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020):
    1. Concepts and criteria have not changed since Directive 95/46/EC → CJEU cases under the Directive still apply.
    2. Concepts are functional: roles follow what parties actually do, not contractual labels. Allocation "should stem from an analysis of the factual elements… and as such is not negotiable". AG Mengozzi (Jehovah's Witnesses): "excessive formalism would make it easy to circumvent" the rules.
    3. Concepts are autonomous (EU data protection law, not IP/competition law).
    4. "Controller" read "in a sufficiently broad way, favouring as much as possible effective and complete protection of data subjects" (avoid gaps/circumvention) "while at the same time not diminishing the role of the processor" (Google Spain C-131/12; Wirtschaftsakademie C-210/16; Fashion ID C-40/17).

  • Master theme tying them: the law puts ultimate responsibility on the controller, yet many controllers (esp. SMEs, public bodies) face take-it-or-leave-it contracts and opaque chains. EDPB answers with legal duties (verify, know the chain, no excuse for "practical difficulties"); C4 answers with technology (formalise contracts + monitor on blockchain); C5 shows how India concentrates everything on the fiduciary without the Art 28 machinery.

C1. EDPB GUIDELINES 07/2020 on the concepts of controller and processor in the GDPR

Status/timeline: adopted under Art 70(1)(e) GDPR; consultation draft 2 Sept 2020; final v2.0 on 7 July 2021; corrected v2.1 on 20 Sept 2022. Replaces Art 29 WP Opinion 1/2010 (WP169). Part I = concepts; Part II = consequences of each role; annex flowchart.

The controller — Art 4(7): five building blocks

Controller = "natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data"; where Union or Member State law determines purposes and means, that law may designate the controller or the criteria for choosing it.

  1. Type of entity — no limit (organisation, individual, group; e.g. the religious community together with its members in Jehovah's Witnesses). In practice the controller = the organisation, not its CEO, an employee, compliance officer or department. A marketing department running a campaign with considerable independence still acts for Company ABC (the controller). Employees' processing is presumed under the organisation's control, unless an employee unlawfully uses data for own purposes (e.g. setting up own company) → organisation must prevent via measures such as training. Employees = "persons acting under the authority of the controller" (Art 29), not controllers/processors. In company groups, each entity's role must be checked.
  2. "Determines" = influence through decision-making power. Questions: "why is this processing taking place?" and "who decided it should take place for a particular purpose?"
  3. "Alone or jointly with others" — several entities may control the same processing; a party can be controller without taking every decision.
  4. "Purposes and means" = the core. Purpose = "an anticipated outcome that is intended or that guides your planned actions" (the why); means = "how a result is obtained or an end is achieved" (the how). Controller must decide both; a processor can never determine the purpose.
  5. "Of the processing of personal data" — control may attach to a single operation or a set of operations, the whole processing or only one stage (Fashion ID). Chain operations that look disconnected at micro level, each with own purpose, may at macro level form "a set of operations" pursuing a joint purpose with jointly defined means → joint control. Actor can be controller even if it doesn't deliberately target personal data or wrongly thinks it processes none. Access to the data is not necessary (Wirtschaftsakademie): an entity that outsources processing but has determinative influence (e.g. adjusting parameters deciding whose data is processed) is a controller.

Sources of control; essential vs non-essential means

  • Legal provisions — explicit competence (law designates controller or criteria; "will be determinative") or implicit (law sets a task requiring processing / obliges a body to retain or provide data; the body charged = controller; e.g. municipality obliged by law to pay welfare benefits based on claimants' finances).
  • Factual influence — judged on all circumstances; same entity can be controller for some operations, processor for others. Some roles are "naturally attached" to an activity via traditional roles or professional expertise: employer→employees, publisher→subscribers, association→members. Law firm representing Company ABC = controller (considerable independence on what info to use and how; processing "linked to the functional role" of a law firm). Email service provider = controller of traffic/billing data but not of message content where it only transmits messages (sender controls content).
  • Contracts help but aren't decisive: a contractual statement of roles can be followed if no reason to doubt it reflects reality, but "it is not possible either to become a controller or to escape controller obligations simply by shaping the contract in a certain way where the factual circumstances say something else". Calling someone a "subcontractor" doesn't make it a processor (WP29 SWIFT opinion, WP128). Even for a pre-defined service the controller must actively approve how processing is done; processor can't later change essential elements without approval. Cloud provider with standardised take-it-or-leave-it contract → Company X remains controller (it chose that provider for its own purposes); provider is processor insofar as it acts on customers' behalf/instructions.
  • KEY CONCEPT — essential vs non-essential means: | | Essential means | Non-essential means | |---|---|---| | Definition | "traditionally and inherently reserved to the controller" and "closely linked to the purpose and the scope" | "more practical aspects of implementation" | | Examples | type of personal data ("which data?"); duration/retention ("for how long?"); categories of recipients ("who shall have access?"); categories of data subjects ("whose data?") | choice of hardware or software, detailed security measures | | Who decides | Controller only (together with purpose decide whether processing is lawful, necessary, proportionate) | May be left to processor | Even then controller must stipulate elements in the contract (e.g. instruction to take all Art 32 measures + duties to assist), remains responsible under Art 24, and must be "fully informed about the means that are used". Advisable to document at least necessary TOMs in the contract.
  • Worked examples (know these):
    • Payroll administrator given clear instructions by Employer A (payments, retention, disclosures), choosing only software and internal access = processor. Bank B receiving data to make payments and independently deciding what to keep and for how long = controller → transfer = disclosure between two controllers.
    • Accounting firm C auditing A's books without detailed instructions, deciding purpose/data/retention/technical means under auditing laws = controller; but if law imposed no specific obligations and A gave very detailed instructions, C would be processor (difference: core professional activity vs limited ancillary task).
    • Hosting provider storing encrypted data without knowing if it's personal = processor; so is a call centre using own software but whose security measures Company X approved.
    • Market researcher XYZ: ABC gives the questions and receives only statistics → ABC controller (without any access to data), XYZ processor; but if XYZ collected data independently with own methodology and produced statistics for ABC without instructions → XYZ sole controller, ABC has no role.

Joint controllers — Art 26(1)

"Where two or more controllers jointly determine the purposes and means of processing, they shall be joint controllers." Existed under the Directive; GDPR adds specific rules; CJEU clarified in Wirtschaftsakademie, Jehovah's Witnesses, Fashion ID. Assessment is factual not formal; "jointly" = "together with" or "not alone". Overarching criterion: joint participation of two or more entities in the determination of purposes AND means (must cover both).

  • Two forms of joint participation:
    1. Common decision — deciding together with a common intention.
    2. Converging decisions — decisions that "complement each other and are necessary for the processing to take place in such manner that they have a tangible impact on the determination of the purposes and means". Must concern purposes and means, not other commercial matters (all commercial arrangements involve converging decisions). Key test: whether the processing would not be possible without both parties' participation, in the sense that each party's processing is "inseparable, i.e. inextricably linked". A processor also participates but not for its own purposes.
  • Three refining principles: (1) Access to data not required (Jehovah's Witnesses: members took notes door-to-door; community was joint controller without access or written instructions because it organised/coordinated the activity and knew in general processing took place to spread the faith). (2) Joint control covers only the jointly determined operations; for earlier/later operations decided alone, an entity is sole controller (Fashion ID). (3) "Joint" does not mean "equal" — operators may be involved "at different stages" and "to different degrees" (Wirtschaftsakademie).
  • Jointly determined purposes may be identical, common, or closely linked/complementary (e.g. mutual benefit from same operation) provided each participates in determining purposes and means. Fashion ID: embedding Facebook's "Like" button served economic interests of both retailer and Facebook; Wirtschaftsakademie: fan-page statistics helped Facebook's advertising and the administrator's promotion. Mutual benefit is "not decisive" — "the mere existence of a mutual benefit (for ex. commercial)" doesn't create joint control; an entity with no purpose of its own that is "merely being paid for services rendered" is a processor.
  • Jointly determined means — each party needn't decide all means. Where one entity provides a platform/standardised tool, the entity that decides to use it for its own purpose also participates in determining the means: "the use of an already existing technical system does not exclude joint controllership when users … can decide on the processing". Fan-page admin defined parameters (target audience, objectives); Fashion ID, by embedding the button, "exerted a decisive influence" over collection and transmission to Facebook. A common system does not always produce joint control, esp. where processing is separable or the provider has no purpose of its own ("a mere commercial benefit … is not sufficient to qualify as a purpose").
  • Examples: Travel agency sending bookings to airline and hotels = three separate controllers; but if they build a common booking platform for package deals, agree essential means and market jointly → joint controllers for the platform and marketing, sole controllers otherwise. Co-branding companies jointly running a marketing event = joint controllers for the event. Clinical trial: investigator and sponsor who draft the protocol together = joint controllers; investigator who simply accepts sponsor's protocol = processor. "global matchz" headhunter: no formal joint decision, but X's decision to run the matching service and Y's decision to feed it CVs are complementary, inseparable, necessary converging decisions → joint controllers for finding candidates; X alone controls own database; Y alone controls hiring. Joint health study: party that merely ran the analysis for the others with no purpose of its own = processor even if it chose non-essential means.
  • NO joint control in 3 situations: (1) mere exchange of data between separate controllers (employer sending employee data to tax authorities under legal obligation); (2) shared infrastructure where each entity decides its own purposes (group companies sharing a database hosted by parent — parent = processor for storage — each deciding on its own data, access, retention); (3) chain of successive operations with independent purposes and means (authority compiling legally mandated statistics from data other bodies must send it = only controller of the statistics; senders responsible for accuracy).

The processor, third parties and recipients

  • Processor — Art 4(8): processes personal data "on behalf of the controller". Any actor (even an individual) can be one; processors can be held liable and fined. Two basic conditions:
    1. Separate entity — one group company can be processor for another, but a department cannot be processor for another department of the same entity; employees/temporary staff under controller's direct authority fall under Art 29.
    2. Processing on behalf of the controller = "serving someone else's interest"; recalls legal concept of delegation. Processor follows instructions at least on purpose and essential means; lawfulness (Art 6 / Art 9) derives from the controller's activity; processor may have some discretion over TOMs but may not process for its own purposes.
  • Processor going beyond instructions → Art 28(10): infringes GDPR and is treated as a controller for that processing. MarketinZ example: contract called it GoodProductZ's "data processor" for advertising; it used the customer database to develop its own business → became controller for that processing, an infringement.
  • Not every service provider is a processor — role stems "from its concrete activities in a specific context", not entity type. Taxi company whose platform lets businesses book airport taxis for employees = controller (designed the platform, decides categories and retention). General IT support provider with "systematic and inevitable" access = processor needing an Art 28 contract; IT consultant fixing a bug with purely incidental, very limited access = neither processor nor controller (customer relies on Art 32 measures). Standardised cloud service used by a municipality's schools = processor, but municipality must ensure its specific storage and deletion instructions are respected "regardless of what is generally offered in the standardized service", since the controller "must make the final decision to actively approve the way the processing is carried out, at least insofar as concerns the essential means."
  • Third party (Art 4(10)) = anyone other than data subject, controller, processor, and persons under their direct authority authorised to process (e.g. employees). Employee accessing data without authorisation for own purposes = third party (and controller if processing for own purposes). Another company in the same group = third party; so are cleaners; parent company requesting subsidiaries' employee data for group statistics = third party that then becomes controller of its own statistics.
  • Recipient (Art 4(9)) = anyone to whom data are disclosed, third party or not, except public authorities receiving data in a particular inquiry under the law (e.g. tax/customs — Recital 31). A processor receiving data is a recipient; so are the airlines/hotels/excursion providers to which travel agency ExploreMore sends data (all controllers).

Controller–processor relationship & the Art 28 contract

  • GDPR novelty: processors now have direct obligations — records (Art 30(2)), security (Art 32), DPO in some cases (Art 37), notifying controller of breaches without undue delay (Art 33(2)), Chapter V transfer rules and (in EDPB's reading) the Art 28(3) duties such as assistance.
  • Art 28(1) sufficient guarantees: controller may use only processors providing sufficient guarantees of appropriate TOMs and must assess them and be able to prove it took the GDPR's requirements into serious consideration. Processor demonstrates via documents (privacy policy, information security policy, external audit reports, certifications such as ISO 27000). Case-by-case risk assessment; no exhaustive list, though Recital 81 points to expert knowledge, reliability, resources, market reputation; adherence to approved code of conduct or certification (Art 28(5)) helps. Continuing obligation: "does not end at the moment where the controller and processor conclude a contract" → verify at appropriate intervals incl. audits/inspections.
  • A contract or other legal act is mandatory: in writing, incl. electronic form (Art 28(9)); non-written agreements insufficient however thorough; signatures recommended. Must bind the processor and set out the controller's obligations too; an "other legal act" (e.g. national law) must be supplemented if content missing. Absence of contract = infringement for which both controller and processor are responsible → SA may fine both; pre-GDPR contracts not updated to Art 28(3) are also an infringement. Yet the contract doesn't decide whether the relationship exists (factual analysis can set it aside; a relationship can exist without a contract, in breach of Art 28(3)). EDPB recommends gathering the Art 28 terms in one place (e.g. an annex).
  • Standard contractual clauses (SCCs) — parties may draft own terms or use SCCs in whole/part (Art 28(6)), adopted by the Commission (Art 28(7)) or by an SA through the consistency mechanism (Art 28(8)) (e.g. Danish SA's clauses in EDPB Opinion 14/2019). Not to be confused with Art 46(2) transfer SCCs. SCCs optional, but if used the clauses must stay the same (blanks may be filled; non-contradictory clauses added).
  • Power imbalance: contracts often drafted unilaterally by providers; SCCs "may contribute to rebalancing the negotiating positions"; provider drafting "is not in itself problematic". But "the imbalance in the contractual power of a small data controller with respect to big service providers should not be considered as a justification for the controller to accept clauses … which are not in compliance", nor can it "discharge the controller from its data protection obligations". A controller that freely accepts such terms "has also accepted full responsibility". Modifications must be directly notified to and approved by the controller; "the mere publication of these modifications on the processor's website is not compliant with Article 28".
  • Contract content: "should not merely restate the provisions of the GDPR"; must give "more specific, concrete information as to how the requirements will be met and which level of security is required"; "far from being a pro-forma exercise" (Recital 79 — clear allocation of responsibilities). Must describe: subject-matter ("video surveillance recordings of people entering and leaving a high-security facility"), duration, nature and purpose ("filming" and "archiving of images" to detect unlawful entry), type of personal data "in the most detailed manner as possible" (not merely "personal data pursuant to Art 4(1)" but e.g. "health records"), categories of data subjects, and the controller's obligations and rights (instructing, supervising, auditing).

EDPB recommends an annex with procedure/template for further instructions.   • Infringing instructions: Art 28(3) also requires the processor to immediately inform the controller if, in its opinion, an instruction infringes data protection law; controller then assesses. Contract should set consequences (termination if controller persists; suspension of the instruction until controller confirms, amends or withdraws).
  • Sub-processors (Art 28(2) and (4)): "chains of subcontracting are becoming increasingly complex" while controller "retains its pivotal role"; need prior specific or general written authorisation before any data entrusted. Processor must list intended sub-processors with location, tasks, proof of safeguards.
    • Specific authorisation: covers a named sub-processor for a specific activity and time; a request left unanswered within the timeframe counts as DENIED.
    • General authorisation: annexed list + selection criteria; processor informs controller "in due time" of changes so it can object; failure to object in time = authorisation. Key difference = meaning of the controller's silence. Changes must be actively flagged; merely giving access to a list updated from time to time is not enough.
    • Art 28(4): processor must impose "the same data protection obligations" on sub-processor by contract, including audit rights, since "the whole chain … needs to be regulated by written agreements". "Same" read functionally (substance must match, not words; obligations irrelevant to the sub-processor's task needn't be included). Initial processor remains fully liable to the controller for its sub-processors → should propose only those with sufficient guarantees.
  • Liability: Art 82(2) processor liable for damage only if it breached processor-specific obligations or acted outside/contrary to lawful instructions; Art 82(4) each party involved is jointly and severally liable for entire damage to ensure effective compensation, with recourse between them (Art 82(5)). Breaches of Arts 25–39 incl. Arts 26 and 28 can attract fines up to €10 million or 2% (Art 83(4)(a)).

The Art 26 arrangement between joint controllers

  • Joint controllers must determine their respective responsibilities "in a transparent manner" (Art 26(1)) — "who does what" — to avoid reduced protection or "a negative conflict of competence" where no one complies. One party's dominant influence "does not negate the joint controllership and cannot serve to exempt either party".
  • Art 26(1) mentions "in particular" data subject rights and Arts 13–14 information → list non-exhaustive. EDPB adds: Art 5 principles, legal basis, security, breach notification, DPIAs, use of processors, transfers, contact with data subjects and SAs. Each controller needs its own legal basis and must prevent incompatible further use (joint control doesn't authorise purposes beyond joint scope).
  • Allocation is flexible, need not be equal (Wirtschaftsakademie); should reflect who is competent and in a position to act, and be documented. But some duties cannot be divided: with shared tools both must ensure purpose limitation and security. GDPR doesn't specify legal form; EDPB recommends a binding contract or legal act (so one can seek the other's liability for its allocated part and both can demonstrate compliance), in clear and plain language.
  • Towards data subjects: arrangement must "duly" (accurately) reflect the parties' roles (Art 26(2)); its "essence" must be made available. EDPB: essence should cover at least all Arts 13–14 information, stating which joint controller is responsible for each element, and the contact point. Controllers choose means (e.g. privacy policy). Contact point recommended but not mandatory.
  • Crucially, data subjects are NOT bound by the arrangement and may exercise rights against each controller (Art 26(3)) — e.g. against the one in their Member State; requiring them to use the contact point would be an "excessive burden" → controllers should forward requests internally. SAs also not bound (by joint-controller qualification or contact point) and may approach any of them (Art 58).

The flowchart & debates

  • Flowchart logic: check legal appointment/legal task → does a party decide purposes, which data, which individuals, whether and to whom data are disclosed, how long kept? If so = controller; if it merely acts on another's instructions, deciding at most non-essential means = processor. In doubtful cases: benefit beyond payment for services or a relationship with the data subjects as employees/customers/members points to control; following documented instructions with no purpose of own points to processing. Last step: would processing be impossible without more than one party's decisive influence? → joint controllers for whole processing or particular stages.
  • Debates: (i) functional approach prevents circumvention but creates uncertainty — EDPB eases by following contracts that reflect reality. (ii) Expansion of joint control via the CJEU trilogy closes gaps but critics warn of over-inclusion, small parties bound by platform terms they can't influence, vague boundaries (AG Bobek in Fashion ID warned against making everyone responsible for everything†). Answers: "joint ≠ equal", control limited to particular phases, commercial benefit alone isn't enough. (iii) Power imbalance stance can be criticised for ignoring a cloud market where SMEs cannot negotiate (responses: SCCs, certification, collective procurement†).
  • India link: DPDP Act 2023 s 8(1) = fiduciary responsible "irrespective of any agreement to the contrary"; s 8(2) requires processors to act under "a valid contract" but prescribes no contract content, has no Art 26-style arrangement rules and no equivalent of Art 28(10) → lacks the machinery these Guidelines elaborate.

C2. CIMINA (2020) — "The data protection concepts of 'controller', 'processor' and 'joint controllership' under Regulation (EU) 2018/1725" (ERA Forum, Oct 2020)

Written by a Legal Officer at the EDPS (in a personal capacity). Summarises novelties of the EU institutions' data protection regulation for the three concepts, esp. joint controllership as interpreted by the CJEU.

Why it matters; the Regulation

  • EU institutions, bodies, offices and agencies (EUIs) are not governed by the GDPR but by its sister instrument Regulation (EU) 2018/1725 ("EUDPR"), which repealed Regulation 45/2001, supervised by the EDPS. Deliberately aligned with the GDPR: provisions mirror GDPR's but with different numbers.
  • Mapping (memorise): | Concept | EUDPR | GDPR | |---|---|---| | Controller definition | Art 3(8) | Art 4(7) | | Processor definition | Art 3(12) | Art 4(8) | | Accountability | Arts 4(2) and 26 | Arts 5(2), 24 | | Processors | Art 29 | Art 28 | | Joint controllers | Art 28 | Art 26 | | Data subject rights | Arts 17–24 | Chs III | | Information duties | Arts 15 and 16 | Arts 13–14 | | Compensation | Art 65 | Art 82 |
  • Because texts run in parallel, CJEU case law carries across to EUIs. Timing note: Cimina wrote before final EDPB Guidelines 07/2020; relies on the September 2020 consultation draft and EDPS Guidelines of 7 Nov 2019 on the same concepts.

The controller (Art 3(8)) — six elements

Controller = "the Union institution or body or the directorate-general or any other organisational entity which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data; where the purposes and means … are determined by a specific Union act, the controller or the specific criteria for its nomination can be provided for by Union law".

  1. Who can be a controller: includes a DG (department of Commission etc.) and "any other organisational entity" → a DG can be a controller in its own right. Differs from GDPR where *EDPB says a department can't be controller; the organisation is.
  2. "Alone or jointly with others" → opens door to joint control; CJEU confirms each actor has its own obligations.
  3. "Determines" = factual influence over a specific operation (Wirtschaftsakademie, Fashion ID, AG Bot and Bobek Opinions). Factual evaluation: reason for the processing, who initiated it, who benefits.
  4. "Purposes and means" = heart. Controller decides purpose as a matter of fact; "means" = TOMs used to carry it out. Following EDPB: essential means (type of data, retention, data subjects, who has access, recipients) = controller; non-essential means (software/hardware choice, detailed technical security measures) = may be decided by processor under general instructions.
    • ⚠ Wording tension to flag in an essay: Cimina writes "although purposes and means are linked, it is not necessary for an entity to equally determine both in order to be considered a controller." On its own this conflicts with the final EDPB Guidelines (controller must decide both purpose and means). Reconciliation: controller must always decide purpose + essential means, but need not decide every aspect of the means equally (non-essential means may be delegated). Her sentence permits delegating non-essential means; it doesn't make means optional.
  5. Control relates to "the processing" — Art 3(3): any operation or set of operations; control may cover the whole or part (decisive in Fashion ID).
  6. EUI-specific: where a Union act determines purposes and means, it may designate the controller; even without express designation, controller found through implicit competence (law assigns a task to an EUI that requires processing).
  • Art 26: controller bears main responsibility for compliance and duty to demonstrate it; towards data subjects (Arts 17–24) it is "the main point of reference". CJEU has broadened data subject protection by extensively expanding joint controllership, building on the broad reading of "controller" in Google Spain (C-131/12) — justified by need for effective and complete protection.
  • Liability — where Regulation departs most from GDPR: Art 65 gives anyone who suffers material or non-material damage a right to compensation from the EUI "subject to the conditions provided for in the Treaties". Unlike Art 82 GDPR, it is brief and silent on how liability is split between controller and processor → read with Art 340 TFEU (Union makes good damage caused by its institutions per general principles common to Member States' laws) and Art 268 TFEU (CJEU jurisdiction). No case law yet → allocation will be settled by the CJEU.

The processor (Art 3(12))

  • Defined exactly as Art 4(8) GDPR: processes personal data "on behalf of the controller".
  • Striking EUI point: DGs are listed as possible controllers but not as possible processors. Yet, as the EDPS observes, DGs often process data on behalf of other DGs as "support DGs" or business owners, in a role similar to a processor's, sometimes reinforced by Service Level Agreements (SLAs) → EDPS recommends internal arrangements identifying exact tasks and responsibilities of support DGs.
  • Essence of the role: processing on behalf of the controller, in its interest, following its instructions. Processor is not a mere tool — keeps "a considerable degree of autonomy" in deciding how to provide services and choosing non-essential means; may propose/advise but controller decides whether to accept without imposing every element. If a processor goes beyond its mandate and decides purpose and essential means → may become controller or joint controller depending on scope of deviation (e.g. whether it departed from instructions to comply with data protection principles). Reusing data for its own purposes = clear breach. GDPR reaches same result in Art 28(10).
  • Art 29(1): controller may use only processors offering sufficient guarantees of appropriate TOMs; accountability means it must assess and prove. Due diligence: check privacy/records-management/information-security policies, external audit reports, processor's reliability & expertise on breaches/security → only then contract; keep verifying compliance regularly. EDPS adds: processor should not outsource further without prior authorisation, keep controller informed of changes with a right to object, pass same contractual obligations to any subcontractor; a processor subject to GDPR → GDPR compliance is one element of sufficient guarantees. Processor must also assist with data subject rights and with Arts 33–41 (security, breach notification, DPIAs, confidentiality of electronic communications) → controller must set clear rules and precise instructions in the contract.
  • Processor liability "still more limited in scope" than controller's (Recitals 45 and 50 and Art 29 strengthen processors' responsibilities, but): controller may be liable for damage from an infringement or a breach of contract; processor liable when it acts outside the controller's mandate or breaches its own obligations under the Regulation. Can be entirely liable if it bears the main responsibility for the damage; if it followed strict instructions it is not liable. Towards data subjects, controller is the main responsible party; processor answers only if shown to have acted beyond mandate. Compare Art 82(2) GDPR (processor liable only for processor-specific breach/outside or contrary to lawful instructions) and Art 82(4) (joint and several liability for whole damage)†.

Joint controllership under Art 28

  • Not new (Reg 45/2001 and Directive 95/46 already referred to "alone or jointly"). Novelty = full dedicated provision, Art 28 (= Art 26 GDPR). Art 28(1): joint controllers exist "where two or more controllers or one or more controllers together with one or more controllers other than Union institutions and bodies jointly determine the purposes and means". Can link two EUIs, or an EUI and an outside actor (management portal provider; national public authority). Outside party is subject to the GDPR but Art 28 of the Regulation still applies in full.
  • Each joint controller must have the possibility or right to determine purpose and essential means — by agreement, or (following Fashion ID and EDPB) by converging on purpose and essential means where the processing "would not be feasible without the participation of both parties". Cimina: "in substance, a general level of complementarity and unity of purpose may already be enough to trigger joint controllership, if these elements are jointly determined." Access to data not needed (lack of access may affect a party's level of responsibility, not its role). Distinguishing joint from separate controllership (each pursuing own purposes) is difficult in practice; if parties neither jointly determine nor converge on purpose and essential means → separate controllers. EUI example: institution processing data for security purposes and, after an incident, transferring data to authorities for investigation → each a separate controller.
  • Art 28(1) arrangement: joint controllers determine responsibilities "in a transparent manner", "in particular" for data subject rights and information duties, "by means of an arrangement", unless and in so far as Union or Member State law already does so. Cimina: clear allocation = "an essential condition for the protection of the rights and freedoms of data subjects". Responsibility need not be equal — Wirtschaftsakademie: "the existence of joint responsibility does not necessarily imply equal responsibility … those operators may be involved at different stages … and to different degrees" → assessed case by case, by stage (e.g. the party that deals with data subjects will likely take on that responsibility).
    • Arrangement mandatory unless law allocates the roles. If EU legislation gives a clear allocation (even partial) in the operative part of the act or at the latest in an implementing or delegated act, no further arrangement needed — EDPS recommends such allocation in legislation; otherwise arrangement fills the gaps. Form: Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) or contract; an SLA may supplement an MoU with technical detail, and EDPS accepts that an SLA alone can suffice if it contains all required elements. Must be agreed by all joint controllers, not adopted unilaterally by one EUI, and define scope (subject matter, duration, nature and purpose, categories of data and data subjects). Substance should cover: roles/responsibilities; information duties under Arts 15 and 16; a contact point for requests; cooperation in replying to data subjects and in DPIAs; any processor engaged.
    • Art 28(2): "the essence of the arrangement shall be made available to the data subject" — through the data protection statement, separately, in a common notice, or by one designated controller.
    • "Irrespective of the terms of the arrangement" the data subject may exercise rights "in respect of and against each of the controllers" → Chapter III rights cannot be limited. Because organisationally complex → include cooperation obligations and a specific contact point (EDPS recommends a single one, e.g. email). "It is insufficient for a joint controller to merely forward a data subject's request to the responsible data controller: it must, in fact, also ensure that adequate follow-up is provided." Art 65 governs joint controllers' liability in the same way as a single controller's.

The CJEU trilogy (all decided under Directive 95/46, but with the new framework in mind)

Case Facts Holding
Wirtschaftsakademie Schleswig-Holstein (C-210/16, 5 June 2018, Grand Chamber) German education company ran a Facebook fan page. Facebook Insights (free, non-negotiable terms) gave administrators anonymous visitor statistics gathered through cookies with a unique user code active for 2 years. ULD (Schleswig-Holstein authority) ordered page deactivated; company argued processing was Facebook's Joint controller with Facebook Ireland: by defining parameters (target audience, objectives, promotion of its activities) it took part in determining purposes and means; could request anonymised demographic (age, sex, relationships, occupations), lifestyle/interest and geographic data. Using a platform didn't exempt it. Access unnecessary; joint responsibility ≠ equal responsibility. Mahieu & van Hoboken: "the responsibility of a controller should be dependent on the stages of the processing in which it is involved and on the degree of this involvement". Cimina accepts the imbalance between small administrator and Facebook but says it doesn't defeat the ruling since aim is "fundamental and complete protection"
Jehovan todistajat / Jehovah's Witnesses (C-25/17, 10 July 2018) Finnish authority prohibited the community from collecting data in door-to-door preaching; members noted names, addresses, religious beliefs, family circumstances as memory aids, without people's knowledge. Community gave no written instructions but made maps allocating areas, recorded preachers/publications, kept list of people refusing visits (1) Manual processing covered where data forms or is meant to form part of a filing system — notes qualified, no need for data sheets, lists or search methods. (2) Community and preaching members = joint controllers: by organising, coordinating, encouraging the preaching it participated in determining purposes and means, with general knowledge data were collected to spread the faith; absence of written instructions or access didn't exclude it. Control can arise from organisation alone; individuals can be joint controllers with an organisation
Fashion ID (C-40/17, 29 July 2019) Online clothing retailer embedded Facebook's "Like" button; visitors' data transmitted automatically to Facebook Ireland without awareness, whether or not members or clicked. Verbraucherzentrale NRW sued; OLG Düsseldorf referred Joint controller with Facebook for collection and disclosure by transmission: embedding the plug-in "exerted a decisive influence", for own commercial advantage (publicity/visibility on Facebook). NOT a controller for Facebook's subsequent processing (couldn't determine purposes/means). Duties follow same line: inform at collection (identity and purposes); where consent needed, obtain it only for operations it jointly controlled. EDPB generalised: for any preceding/subsequent operations an entity decides alone → sole controller. "Phase-oriented" approach narrows the breadth of Wirtschaftsakademie
  • Cimina's 7 conclusions (CJEU clarifications): (1) full and complete protection of data subjects is key → they must always be able to exercise rights easily and know each entity's role; (2) specific influence over processing for one's own purposes, by participating in purposes and essential means, may be enough for joint control; (3) an entity is joint controller only for operations in which it participates → duty to inform data subjects of its identity and purpose at collection; (4) joint control ≠ necessarily equal responsibility; (5) lack of written guidance/instructions doesn't exclude decisive influence; (6) clearly defined roles foster data subject protection and underline accountability; (7) no access to data doesn't exclude joint control.

Critical assessment

Beyond the wording issue on purposes/means: (1) Over-inclusiveness of Wirtschaftsakademie and Jehovah's Witnesses — every site with a plug-in or fan page becomes a co-controller; unclear liability and compliance burdens for small actors who cannot influence large platforms. Fashion ID's phase limit, "joint ≠ equal" and later EDPB "mere commercial benefit alone doesn't create joint control" moderate this; AG Bobek (Fashion ID) warned against making everyone responsible for everything†. (2) Power imbalance: fan-page admin had no negotiating power over Facebook yet was jointly responsible; EDPB Guidelines refuse to let small controllers hide behind imbalance (→ Compliance Gap reading, C4). (3) EUDPR liability gap: Art 65's vague reference to the Treaties leaves allocation between EUI controllers and processors unclear until CJEU rules. (4) DGs can be controllers but not processors — an internal-organisation nuance unique to the EU institutions.

C3. EDPB OPINION 22/2024 on certain obligations following from the reliance on processor(s) and sub-processor(s) (adopted 7 October 2024)

Background and questions

  • Scenario: a public body moves to the cloud; the cloud provider (initial processor) relies on subcontractors, who may use further subcontractors. Under Art 4(8) the term "processor" covers not only the initial processor but also the processor's processor "and so on along the processing chain". GDPR governs the chain mainly via Art 28: controller may use only processors with sufficient guarantees (28(1)); processor needs controller's prior written authorisation for sub-processors (28(2)); contract must contain Art 28(3) clauses; each processor must pass same data protection obligations to sub-processors (28(4)). Above these: accountability (Art 5(2)) and Art 24 (TOMs in light of risk).
  • Trigger: EDPB Coordinated Enforcement Framework (CEF) report of 17 Jan 2023 on public sector's use of cloud-based services identified eight challenges and left the extent of some obligations unclear. On 5 July 2024 the Danish SA asked the Board for an opinion under Art 64(2) ("matter of general application"). Deadline extended under Art 64(3) (8 weeks + 6); Opinion adopted 7 Oct 2024, signed by Chair Anu Talus. Admissible because it concerned interpretation of Arts 5(2), 24, 28, was reasoned, and hadn't already been answered (Guidelines 07/2020 gave only partial guidance).
  • Limits of the Opinion: Board did not decide who is controller/processor in any arrangement ("primarily up to the parties", subject to SA checks); did not address liability to data subjects (Art 82); stressed that meeting the Art 28(1) verification duty doesn't mean the controller complies with other provisions (lawfulness, Art 32 security, Chapter V).
  • Danish SA's questions: Q1 (controller's duties along the chain): 1.1 must the controller identify all sub-processors or only the first line, and how far must it verify safeguards of (sub-)processors and the content of sub-processing contracts? 1.2 how far must it, under Art 28(1) and Art 44, assess and show that protection isn't undermined when (sub-)processors transfer data outside the EEA on its instructions? 1.3 do these obligations vary between low-risk and high-risk processing? Q2 (contract wording): 2 must the contract contain the Art 28(3)(a) exception "unless required to do so by Union or Member State law to which the processor is subject"? 2a is a broader exception covering third-country law, "unless required to do so by law or binding order of a governmental body", in itself an infringement? 2b should such a broader exception be read as a documented instruction from the controller?

Q1.1 — Knowing the whole chain

  • Controller must know the whole chain, not just the first line. Guidelines 07/2020: "although the chain may be quite long, the controller retains its pivotal role". "Identity" = name and address of each processor, a contact person (name, position, contact details) and a description of the processing with a clear delimitation of responsibilities where there are several sub-processors. Mirrors Annex IV of Commission Controller–Processor SCCs (Decision 2021/915) and Annex III of International Transfer SCCs (Decision 2021/914).
  • Three reasons: (1) choice of recipients, incl. processors, is one of the essential means of processing (which data used, how long, who has access and whose data) which only the controller may make. (2) Art 28(2) authorisation system depends on it: under specific authorisation the controller approves each sub-processor individually (silence = refusal); under general authorisation it approves an initial list and must have enough time to object to changes which the processor must actively flag. (3) Processors are "recipients" (Art 4(9)), so transparency and data subject rights depend on knowing them: Arts 13(1)(e) and 14(1)(e) require recipient information "as specific and concrete as possible" (WP29 transparency guidelines: generally means naming them); Art 30(1)(d) requires them in the RoPA. RW v Österreichische Post (C-154/21, 12 Jan 2023): Art 15(1)(c) access right gives data subject the actual identity of recipients unless identification impossible or request manifestly unfounded/excessive; under Art 19 data subject may learn which specific recipients were told of rectification/erasure/restriction.
  • Conclusion: controllers should have the identity of all processors and sub-processors "readily available at all times" → answer access requests without undue delay, react quickly to breaches along the chain, cope if chain breaks (sub-processor unreachable, unwilling or insolvent). Applies "regardless of the risk". To make workable: processor should proactively provide the info and keep it up to date, in a format the contract may specify.

Verifying "sufficient guarantees"; how risk affects it

  • Proximus (C-129/21): Arts 5(2) and 24 "impose general accountability and compliance requirements", obliging controllers to "take appropriate steps to prevent any infringements"; accountability remains with controller even when using processors. Central principle: "the engagement of processors should not lower the level of protection for the rights of data subjects compared to a situation where the processing is carried out directly by the controller" — applies to sub-processors and sub-sub-processors. Controller must ensure the whole chain consists only of processors with sufficient guarantees and be able to prove it took GDPR requirements into serious consideration, "even if the chain of processing can be long and complex". Due diligence in both selection and oversight.
  • Initial processor: verification case by case (nature, scope, context, purposes, risks). Codes of conduct (Art 40) and certification (Art 42) can help demonstrate (Art 28(5)). Non-exhaustive methods: questionnaire; requests for documentation; publicly available information; certifications or audit reports from trustworthy third parties; on-site audits. Guarantees must be demonstrated "to the controller's satisfaction"; verification repeated at appropriate intervals.
  • Risk (Q1.3): risk-based approach doesn't let a controller "neglect or deviate from its obligations" because it considers the risk "low". Following Natsionalna agentsia za prihodite (C-340/21): the duty to implement appropriate TOMs always applies; only the measures vary. Art 28(1) doesn't mention risk but implies it; for high-risk processing (e.g. special categories) controller may set stricter TOMs processors must be able to meet. Answer: the obligation to verify sufficient guarantees "should apply regardless of the risk", but its extent varies — lower risk allows less extensive verification; higher risk makes verification of the entire chain more important (controller may check sub-processing contracts itself or require extended verification and documentation from the initial processor). Separates the existence of the duty from its intensity.

Relying on the processor without giving up responsibility

  • Verifying a long chain could be impossible, so the Board eases it: (1) processor must assist (Art 28(3)(e),(f)) and make available all information necessary to demonstrate compliance and allow audits (28(3)(h)) — controller may rely on this "provided that the information … actually demonstrates compliance", and processor should share proactively; duties pass down chain under 28(4). (2) Before a sub-processor is engaged, a general authorisation should be supplemented with criteria (TOMs, expertise, reliability, resources); processor supplies list of intended sub-processors with locations, tasks, proof of safeguards; initial processor proposes only those with sufficient guarantees and remains fully liable to the controller (Art 28(4)).
  • No burden shift: processor's Art 28(4) duty "does not lift the responsibility of the controller". Key sentence: "the ultimate decision on whether to engage a specific sub-(sub-)processor and the pertaining responsibility, including with respect to verifying the sufficiency of the guarantees provided by the (sub-)processor, remains with the controller." SAs should ask whether the controller can show verification "has taken place to the controller's satisfaction". Controller may rely on processor's information and build on it, but where incomplete, inaccurate or raising questions, or risk warrants, controller must ask for more and verify, complete or correct it; for high-risk processing it should increase verification across the chain. "Reliance is allowed; blind reliance is not."

The sub-processing contracts and the Art 28(4) flow-down

  • Art 28(4) directly obliges processors to impose "the same data protection obligations" on sub-processors; Art 28(3)(d) makes this contractual too → initial processor "legally and contractually required" to pass them down. "Same" read functionally (identical wording not required); under Module Three of the Transfer SCCs the exporter warrants it has done so. If a sub-processor fails: "ultimate responsibility" rests with controller, but initial processor "will remain liable to the controller" (contractual claim).
  • Can controller see the contracts? Art 28(3)(h) requires the processor to provide them on request; Clause 7.7(c) Controller–Processor SCCs and Clause 9(c) Transfer SCCs let controller request a copy, business secrets redacted ("an expression of the controller's right of audit"). But SCCs don't say whether the controller must ask. Board: "the controller does not have a duty to systematically ask for the sub-processing contracts." Decide case by case, and as part of its audit right it should have "a process in place to undertake audit campaigns … sampling verifications" that contracts contain necessary obligations. Request a contract where there are doubts or the SA asks (e.g. after a data breach at the sub-processor; public/other information suggests a problem such as a sub-processor's template not meeting Art 28(3)).
  • Caveat: a copy may help but "contractual clauses cannot – by themselves – demonstrate that the sufficient guarantees are effectively implemented" → paper compliance ≠ real compliance (links straight to C4).

Transfers along the chain (Q1.2)

  • "Transfer" per Guidelines 05/2021 — remote access from a third country can be a transfer. Controller decides whether transfers are allowed at all: processors must not transfer outside its instructions (Art 29), instructions pass down chain (Art 28(4)), controller can prohibit transfers or limit to specific countries.
  • Controller's responsibility persists though the processor is the exporter: dual duties under Art 44 and under Art 28(1) (whose reference to "the requirements of this Regulation" includes Chapter V). Art 44 binds both controllers and processors — both must ensure protection is not undermined whatever the transfer ground (Schrems II, C-311/18), both individually liable; controller can claim back from processor (Art 82(5)). Duties apply before the transfer and while data is processed abroad; verification again required regardless of risk, though a transfer may itself increase risk (Recital 116). Controller may refuse a sub-processor whose engagement would entail a transfer. Controller is ultimately responsible for any Art 28(1) infringement; "practical difficulties" invoked in controlling sub-processor engagement, esp. third-country transfers, "do not exonerate the controller" — weak bargaining power is no excuse (cloud/Big Tech imbalance).
  • Non-exhaustive documentation processor/exporter prepares and controller assesses, builds on, and can show SA:
    1. Transfer mapping ("know your transfers" — Recommendations 01/2020): which data goes where incl. by remote access, for which purposes; controller should ask for more if a sub-processor's location is missing or remote access isn't mentioned; needed before engagement, for transparency (Arts 13(1)(f), 14(1)(f), 15(1)(c), 15(2)) and the RoPA (Art 30(1)(d)–(e)).
    2. Ground for transfer: under adequacy decision (Art 45) controller checks decision is in force and covers the transfer; for onward transfers it need not check onward-transfer rules itself since adequacy decision already covers them (Art 45(2)(a)), though must still ensure sufficient guarantees under Art 28(1). Under Art 46 safeguards (SCCs or binding corporate rules (BCRs)), a Transfer Impact Assessment (TIA) is needed in line with Schrems II and Recommendations 01/2020: assessment of whether third-country law and practice, esp. public-authority access, would undermine safeguards, with supplementary measures where needed. For onward transfers, controller need not verify the Art 46 tool's rules as such but should be able to show importers actually comply.

The wording of Art 28(3)(a): "unless required by law"

  • Art 28(3)(a): processor acts "only on documented instructions … including with regard to transfers … unless required to do so by Union or Member State law to which the processor is subject", in which case it must inform the controller beforehand unless that law forbids it on important grounds of public interest.
  • Q2: Board began from the principle that contracts cannot override the law or bind third parties such as public authorities. Every contract must address situations where the processor may be legally required to process outside instructions; the duty to inform the controller is a core element to include. Outside the EEA a reference to "EU or Member State law" "may not be very meaningful", and the Commission's International Transfer SCCs omit the "unless" wording, handling it via Clause 8.1 (inform immediately if unable to follow instructions) and Clause 14(e) (notify laws preventing compliance). Board: including the clause is "highly recommended, but not strictly required", without prejudice to the mandatory duty to inform. Where EU/Member State law clearly relevant, the wording helps demonstrate compliance.
  • Q2a (broader "law or binding order of a governmental body"): two situations. EU/EEA Member State law falls within Art 28(3)(a) anyway, because EU and Member State law share the same constitutional tradition (Art 16(1) TFEU; Arts 8(1) and 52(1) Charter); if only this is covered, the wording has no impact. Third-country law doesn't share that tradition and cannot automatically be treated the same (Art 44). The GDPR's "legal obligation" and "public interest" refer to EU or Member State law (Art 6(3), Recital 40, Art 49(4)), and Art 29 permits processing without instructions only where "required to do so by Union or Member State law". For transfers, contracts may in principle address third-country legal requirements, but merely including the broad wording is "highly unlikely to suffice". Better models: Clause 14 Transfer SCCs (pre-signing assessment, warranty of no conflicting laws, notice of changes, suspension or termination), Clause 15 (notify exporter, review legality of a government request and challenge it where appropriate), and similar BCR clauses. Common standard: third-country laws that respect the essence of fundamental rights and do not exceed what is necessary and proportionate in a democratic society to safeguard an Art 23(1) objective do not undermine GDPR protection (Recommendations 02/2020 on the European Essential Guarantees). Contracts must distinguish laws that undermine protection from those that do not, with supplementary measures where needed. Even within the EEA, a processor may face third-country law (e.g. via a US parent and the CLOUD Act†) but such wording "does not release the processor from its obligations under the GDPR". Conclusion: the broad wording "is a prerogative of the contractual freedom of the parties and does not infringe Article 28(3)(a) GDPR per se", but it doesn't exonerate either party from its obligations, esp. duty to inform and transfer rules.
  • Q2b: No. An "instruction" = controller specifying what processing the processor should carry out on its behalf and how, precisely enough to cover specific processing, and it must always be withdrawable. A provision merely recognising legal compulsion isn't a request to process on the controller's behalf. Controller's influence ends where law requires processing it cannot steer or halt (Belgian State (Official Journal), C-231/22; Art 4(7)) → a reminder to obey the law is a contractual safeguard, not an instruction; same for third-country law. The word "unless" itself shows the exception is processing not on instruction. Broad wording "cannot be construed as a documented instruction by the controller", and "the controller remains responsible where it has not ensured that the (sub-)processor processes personal data only on its documented instructions". Doesn't apply where processing is required by EU/Member State law or by third-country law ensuring an essentially equivalent level of protection.

Why it matters; criticisms

  • Takeaways: tightens controller accountability over long cloud/SaaS chains via full visibility of the chain, risk-scaled verification, no excuse from practical difficulties; makes processors proactive information providers. Pragmatic: no duty to collect every sub-processing contract, sampling is enough, controllers may rely on processor documentation → balances accountability with feasibility. Targets boilerplate in US hyperscalers' DPAs — "unless required by law or binding order of a governmental body" covers US government access such as FISA 702 or the CLOUD Act†: not illegal per se, but insufficient for transfers and not an instruction.
  • Central tension: law places ultimate responsibility on the controller, yet many controllers (esp. small businesses and public bodies) face take-it-or-leave-it contracts and opaque chains. EDPB answers with legal duties; C4 answers with technology. India: DPDP s 8(1) makes the Data Fiduciary responsible "irrespective of any agreement to the contrary", s 8(2) requires a valid contract, but no prescribed content and no express sub-processor regulation → supply-chain gap likely wider.
  • Criticisms: (1) burdens small controllers who lack bargaining power/expertise to verify multi-tier hyperscaler chains (CEF cloud report; Compliance Gap article). (2) Relies heavily on processor self-reporting → "paper compliance" despite its own warning about contractual clauses. (3) Chain-wide identity requirement hard to meet where sub-processing is dynamic (CDNs, support centres). (4) It's an opinion, not binding law (though SAs follow Art 64 opinions for consistency).

C4. BAQUERO, RESTREPO AMARÍLES et al. (2025) — "The compliance gap in data supply chains: contract specification languages and smart contracts as compliance technologies" (Artificial Intelligence and Law)

Interdisciplinary team of lawyers and computer scientists.

The problem: a compliance gap at the back end

  • Privacy research/regulation has focused on the front end (business-to-consumer, B2C: privacy policies, consent, empowering data subjects). Far less on the back end — the "data supply chain" through which a controller passes data to processors, who pass it to sub-processors. Serious blind spot: what happens to people's data depends on whether the whole chain complies.
  • Ponemon (2018): data protection compliance = "the most difficult regulatory challenge" corporations face (vast data flows + growing use of algorithmic decision systems (ADS)). Controllers outsource storage, cleansing, annotation, computational analysis, algorithm training, visualisation, app development → division of labour makes it very hard to know what happens to data once it leaves the controller's hands. Firms invest in compliance technology, yet no effective tool exists for monitoring the data supply chain → the back end is a "black box" for controllers, consumers and regulators alike. UK ICO (2019): monitoring the data supply chain = one of the most urgent compliance issues; firms rely mainly on contractual mechanisms and neglect monitoring and technical/organisational controls.
  • Central claim: "individuals' right to data protection may lack effectiveness if it is not possible to monitor compliance regarding the data flowing in data supply chains." Ties to the critique of notice and consent (Unit 2): telling people in a privacy policy what will happen achieves little if no one can check whether companies further down the chain follow the rules; B2C transparency is not enough. Compliance failures undermine consumer trust (Pavlou; Milne & Boza); and (Acquisti et al.) when firms skew the "privacy/value balance" through unbridled profit maximisation, regulators respond (GDPR; CCPA).

Why existing research and legal instruments fall short

  • Privacy research is "data-subject centric" (readability/vagueness of policies, opt-out mechanisms, summarisation tools: P3P, Do Not Track, CLAUDETTE) — assumes people will trust a company with a privacy policy; neglects internal company documents (data processing agreements, processing instructions) that actually govern the back end.
  • AI & Law work has its own limits: legal ontologies and logic programming (ODRL, PrOnto) face the knowledge acquisition bottleneck (turning legal texts/facts into formal logic is slow, laborious human work) and the open texture of legal language (terms often too abstract/ambiguous to become fixed rules). Real documents (Word, Excel, emails) are unstructured; NLP performs poorly on them.
  • The "compliance gap" defined: data is processed iteratively and dynamically, but the instruments governing it have four defects:
    1. Dispersed — across many documents rather than one place (hard to update and monitor).
    2. Static — can't track dynamic processing.
    3. Convoluted — complex language, puzzling cross-references.
    4. Legalistic — vocabulary remote from the technical/business staff who apply them. → controllers are "disempowered" and can't effectively monitor their own chains. Consequences: companies — higher non-compliance risk; data subjects — lose trust through lost control and information asymmetry; SAs — can't spot violations or systemic risks.
  • Eleven requirements (REQ1–REQ11) — each defect becomes a requirement: | REQ | Requirement | | |---|---|---| | REQ1 | One integrated source of contractual information | answers dispersed | | REQ2 | Dynamic updating with real-time monitoring | answers static | | REQ3 | Clear and concise language | answers convoluted | | REQ4 | Accessible vocabulary | answers legalistic | | REQ5 | Integrate internal company documents | | | REQ6 | Combined conceptual and technological framework | | | REQ7 | Extract information from enterprise documents | | | REQ8 | Monitor the entire chain incl. post-contractual obligations | | | REQ9 | Control unplanned and scattered international flows | | | REQ10 | Ensure all processors follow the controller's rules | | | REQ11 | Keep flows aligned with the controller's overall purpose | |

The data supply chain and the law

  • Term from Kneese et al. (2014); ICO: data may be processed "by hundreds of organizations". Like industrial supply chains, based on division of labour, but with two complications: data is immaterial and intangible (harder to monitor/protect against leaks; "can be easily copied", esp. serious for sensitive data) and the chain is international by nature (data flows globally unless costly technology stops it).
  • Is "supply chain" even the right term? It implies a leading firm coordinating everyone towards an overall objective, whereas data often flows in an "unplanned, scattered manner". Cambridge Analytica: Facebook, app developer Kogan and Cambridge Analytica shared no common purpose; only "careless management" creating flows each party used for its own benefit. Authors nevertheless conclude business practice and GDPR create genuine supply chains: controllers hire suppliers to achieve a purpose and the GDPR requires controllers to ensure all processors follow the rules and act on contractual instructions → "an overall purpose to be achieved in coordination".
  • GDPR provisions showing the stakes: Art 28(1) only processors with sufficient guarantees; Art 28(3) processing governed by contract/legal act with controller's instructions; Art 28(4) processor must cascade all instructions to sub-processors and is fully liable to controller for them (presumption in EU law that initial processor liable by default); Art 30 records of processing SAs can inspect; Art 82(2) controller liable for damage, processor liable where it acts against/outside instructions; Art 82(4) each responsible party liable for entire damage → "heightened liabilities" for both. Financial stakes: fines up to 4% of worldwide turnover, record fines vs Google, British Airways, Marriott; FTC 5billionfineonFacebook *  * (CambridgeAnalytica); annualcompliancecosts * *7.7 million to $30.9 million, up 45% since 2011.
  • ⚠ SLIP FOR EXAMS: the paper attributes the accountability-based "burden of proof" to "Article 7 GDPR". In fact accountability lies in Art 5(2) and Art 24, while Art 7(1) only places on the controller the burden of demonstrating that the data subject gave consent. Spotting this error and giving the correct provisions shows close reading.

The Atos case study: why contracts are not enough

  • Atos — one of Europe's largest IT companies; operates globally, often within long chains, acting as processor for its clients and client of its own suppliers, sometimes both in the same chain. Authors map the documents Atos uses (broad → specific): | Document | Function | |---|---| | Supplier data protection policies & certifications | Evidence of compliance before hiring | | Master Services Agreement (MSA) | Between controller and first processor; services, liabilities, commercial terms; broad data protection clause referring to the DPA and binding all later sub-processors | | DPIA (Art 35) | Identifies high risks before processing | | Data Protection Addendum (DPA) | Contains Art 28(3) obligations (subcontracting, security, confidentiality, breach procedure, audit) | | Compliance Assessment of Data Processing (CADP) | Documents processing details incl. subcontracting and risks; part of the record of processing; reviewed by DPO; Excel versions for when Atos is controller (CADP-C) or processor (CADP-P), a Word version for passing work to sub-processors (CADP-S) | | Technical and Organisational Measures (TOM) | A security questionnaire | | SCCs and Binding Corporate Rules | Govern international transfers | Almost all are highly standardised; only the constantly updated CADP is a truly "living" document. Lesson: "the more a particular document has to be handled by different firms … and by different levels of one particular firm, the higher will be the level of standardization required".
  • Five compliance functions: (1) framework agreements/master contracts define legal and ethical obligations and standards; (2) DPIA, DPA, CADP assess risks; (3) DPA and CADP instruct the processing (down the chain, from managers to operations); (4) technical/operational documents (TOMs) define operational measures; (5) CADP is used to audit and monitor processing.
  • By purpose, three types: prescriptive (give instructions: MSA, DPA, SCCs, BCRs), evaluative (collect info to assess compliance: TOMs), evaluative-prescriptive (do both: CADP and DPIA).
  • Why an Art 28 DPA can't solve it alone: controllers face long chains with many recipients and real difficulty building a structured monitoring system "even if the companies are intent on doing that". Breaches show the danger: April 2021 healthcare providers reported patient data exposed through hacked third-party vendors; Airbus saw four of its vendors attacked for technical documents; attempts to obtain COVID vaccine cold-chain information. In principle documents should be "living" (evaluative documents tracking changes; prescriptive documents updated with new instructions). In reality most Atos documents are standardised and never updated, and even the CADP (a long Excel questionnaire with space for client instructions) fails.
  • Four practical challenges: (1) Audits complex and "burdensome" (multiple documents, formats, actors); (2) documents not transversal — the CADP "gets stuck in one particular department" instead of moving between IT, legal, finance; different people fill different documents so nothing guarantees coherence; (3) interoperability across firms is poor → hard to keep instructions consistent as they pass down the chain; (4) no real-time compliance — DPO must constantly screen documents for updates, causing delays or preventing effective compliance.
  • Point for a law student: an Art 28 contract is a static text — it can say what processors must do but cannot show whether they're doing it. Links to paper vs effective compliance debate: Guidelines 07/2020 insist contracts must not merely restate the GDPR; Opinion 22/2024: "contractual clauses cannot – by themselves – demonstrate that the sufficient guarantees are effectively implemented". EDPB answers with legal duties of verification; this paper answers with technology.

The proposed tools in plain words

  • Smart contract (Unsworth): "self-enforcing agreement, written in the form of computer code into an electronic platform" — a small program that runs automatically when set conditions are met. Behaves predictably → Hildebrandt: "code-driven regulation"; promises to cut monitoring and transaction costs. Difficulty (Binns): translating natural-language contracts into code.
  • Contract specification languages (CSLs) bridge the gap: a structured way of writing a contract that humans can still read but is precise enough for a computer to process and convert automatically into a smart contract. Sorts wording into standard legal categories: who owes a duty (debtor), to whom (creditor), what must be done, under what conditions, what powers each side has.
  • Crucial: aim is NOT full automation. Goal: "automate the identification of potential inconsistencies and violations between data protection artifacts and data protection rules, to be further examined by … data protection officers or other humans-in-the-loop". System flags possible problems; a human decides what to do.
  • Why Symboleo: meets 3 criteria: (1) links data protection concepts to higher-level categories (e.g. "storage of data", "retention period", "retention of data"); (2) supports automated inferences that flag potential violations (e.g. exceeding a storage period) to a human; (3) comes with tools to verify generated smart contracts flag violations when, and only when, their conditions occur.
  • Symboleo ontology: a fixed vocabulary of legal concepts. A contract = set of obligations and powers between roles (controller, processor) held by parties (the client, Atos) concerning assets (the data). Obligation = a debtor's duty to a creditor to bring about a result when a condition holds, created when a triggering event occurs; may be conditional, unconditional, or surviving (outlives the contract, like deletion or confidentiality duties). Power = authority to create, change, suspend, resume or terminate obligations or contracts, taking effect only when exercised. Events (e.g. "delivered") = fixed points in time. Language has 33 axioms, 13 of which govern runtime monitoring.
  • Three tools: Symboleo editor (write specifications); SymboleoPC (translates into code for the nuXmv model checker — explores every possible way the contract could unfold); Symboleo2SC (generates smart contracts for Hyperledger Fabric, an open-source private blockchain where only approved participants take part).

Method and findings

  • Authors annotated Atos's natural-language contract documents, converted them into Symboleo, verified the result and generated monitoring smart contracts. They call it "an illustrative example rather than an experience report". Worked example: simplified DPA (client = controller, Atos = processor): client gives documented instructions and must adapt them on receiving an infringement notice; Atos must process only on instructions, warn the client of infringing instructions, keep a record of processing, supply a copy on request. Mapping: client's duty to adapt instructions = obligation triggered by the infringement notice (mirrors Art 28(3) duty to inform of infringing instructions); Atos's record-keeping = obligation triggered each time data is processed (Art 30(2)); duty to supply a copy echoes Art 28(3)(h); Atos's right to suspend service if client fails to adapt = a power, triggered by client's violation, matching the suspension clause EDPB recommends; client's right to have service resumed is also a power.
  • Finding 1 — verification at design time: human-drafted contracts can contain conflicting obligations that make performance impossible → amendment, rollback, disputes, voidability. SymboleoPC and nuXmv check every possible course for liveness (every performance ends within bounded time) and safety (nothing undesirable happens). E.g. test that if processing is suspended and client never adapts, contract eventually terminates unsuccessfully. A failed check produces a counterexample (step-by-step trace). Can reveal a party exposed to unlimited liability, a contract ignoring a party's primary intentions, or impossible performance (one clause requiring client data to be delivered and another requiring it to be deleted).
  • Finding 2 — monitoring at runtime: the blockchain ledger stores every change of state and every event → contract history trackable and tamper-proof. In an illustrative KYC ("know your client") identity-checking service offered via an API, the smart contract records all processing so violations by client or provider can be found and reported to a human-in-the-loop, who can then trigger a power such as suspension. Shows which entities processed users' documents, where, and what computations were performed; a regulator or watchdog could help maintain the ledger.
  • Finding 3 — evaluation against the 11 requirements: Symboleo well supports REQ2 (real-time dynamic monitoring), REQ3 (clear language) and REQ9 (control of unplanned flows) because it is event-based. Partially supports the rest: REQ1 (integrated source), REQ4 (accessible wording), REQ5 (integrating internal documents — converting DPA/CADP is manual), REQ7 (extraction via templates), REQ8 (whole-chain & post-contractual monitoring via surviving obligations), REQ10 and REQ11 (processor alignment with controller's rules and purpose).

Limitations, criticisms and wider links

  • Authors admit: Symboleo still in development; smart contracts lack access control (role-based controls being added). Above all, the system cannot verify the truthfulness of its inputs — "garbage in, garbage out": the ledger is only as reliable as what the parties enter. Translating contracts into Symboleo remains manual (the knowledge acquisition bottleneck again); SymboleoNLP handles only templates; LLMs speed things up but make many errors and can't handle CADP-style Excel info. Sub-contracts (vital for long chains) are supported by the language but not yet well by the tools. False positives/negatives require continuous debugging to build trust. Humans must still decide → human–machine interaction matters; contracts may need adapting to fit the technology. No CSL study has addressed different jurisdictions or changes in the law. On open texture, authors say not a major problem because rules are first assessed for whether they're specific enough to formalise and "data protection rules tend to be specific" — debatable, since standards like "appropriate", "sufficient guarantees", "without undue delay" plainly require judgement.
  • Distinguished from earlier work on smart contracts and GDPR (mostly basic consent via fixed-type contracts): Symboleo handles any conditions (usage, security, retention), complex obligations and powers, design-time checking and runtime monitoring. Caution: smart contracts are not legal contracts (which can be implied, e.g. buying clothes in a shop); agree with Kelbert & Pretschner that "sticky policies" travelling with the data are "rather unrealistic" across firms with different technologies and jurisdictions.
  • Balanced answer:
    • For: fits GDPR accountability (Arts 5(2), 24, 28(1)) and Opinion 22/2024's demand for chain-wide visibility and continuous verification; turns paper compliance into operational compliance; empowers controllers (EDPB recognises they're weak against powerful processors); supports auditing and liability allocation under Art 82(4)–(5).
    • Against: the ledger records claims, not reality; formal rules can't capture open-textured standards ("appropriate", "proportionate"); blockchain immutability sits uneasily with erasure (Art 17) and storage limitation (Art 5(1)(e)), though the ledger stores events rather than personal data†; costs and adoption barriers burden SMEs, interoperability needs network effects, unclear who runs the ledger; risk of tech-solutionism (substituting formal compliance for substantive protection — critiques of "compliance-ism"); study is exploratory, using a simplified DPA → scalability unproven; and the fact that the one dynamic document (the CADP) is still an Excel file suggests the gap stems from organisational silos, not technology alone.
  • India link: DPDP Act s 8(1) (fiduciary responsible "irrespective of any agreement to the contrary") and s 8(2) (processors only under "a valid contract") — but no Art 28-style mandatory contract content and no explicit sub-processor regime → supply-chain compliance gap may be even wider.
  • Conclusions: formalising data protection instructions in a CSL harmonises and clarifies instructions scattered across documents and formats. Partial automation warns a human of inconsistencies within instructions, between instructions and the law, and between instructions and actual processing, cutting monitoring time. The blockchain gives a reliable, tamper-proof record of how each company processed data. System serves a preventive function (flag violations before they materialise) and a repressive function (identify the party responsible for a breach — for companies and potentially regulators). Running cost "is negligible". Value: shifts attention from the data subject to the companies behind the controller, complementing EDPB's legal answers by showing how hard the controller's responsibility is to discharge in practice. Core message: data subjects' rights are only as effective as the controller's ability to see and control its supply chain, and static contracts cannot provide that visibility. A balanced answer credits this while stressing the tools support human judgement rather than replace it and cannot guarantee that what is recorded is true.

C5. THE KEY CJEU CASES + UNIT 3 CONNECTION TO THE DPDP ACT 2023

Why the Court reads the roles broadly

  • Roles are functional: follow what the entity actually does, not what a contract calls it; allocation "not negotiable". GDPR didn't change the concepts or criteria inherited from Directive 95/46 → Directive-era CJEU judgments still govern. Thread: "controller" must be read "in a sufficiently broad way", favouring "effective and complete protection" and avoiding gaps/circumvention, "while at the same time not diminishing the role of the processor".

"Phase-oriented" approach limits breadth of Wirtschaftsakademie

Cimina (EDPS Legal Officer) draws the same lessons from the trilogy, adding that clear roles foster both data subjects' rights and accountability.
  • EDPB working test: joint participation = common decision or converging decisions that complement each other, are necessary for the processing and have a tangible impact on purposes/means. Key question: would the processing "not be possible without both parties' participation" → each party's processing "inseparable, i.e. inextricably linked". Brake: "the mere existence of a mutual benefit (for ex. commercial)" doesn't create joint control; an entity "merely being paid for services rendered" is a processor. Same functional logic in reverse for processors: under Art 28(10) a processor that starts deciding its own purposes and means becomes a controller for that processing and is in breach.

Proximus (C-129/21), Natsionalna agentsia za prihodite (C-340/21), MediaMarktSaturn (C-687/21) — reinforce accountability: controllers must take appropriate steps to prevent infringements, measures assessed concretely, and the controller bears the burden of proving its security was appropriate.

The cross-cutting debates

  1. Functional approach vs contractual labels. For: prevents circumvention (nobody can contract out of controllership; "subcontractor" ≠ processor — WP29 SWIFT). Against: parties can't always know their role in advance; SAs can set contracts aside. EDPB middle position: follow the contract where it reflects reality.
  2. Expansion of joint controllership via the trilogy. For: complete protection; avoids gaps where a platform and its users each disclaim responsibility; incentive for website operators to choose privacy-friendly tools. Against: over-inclusion (every fan-page admin or plug-in user becomes a co-controller); duties loaded on small parties who can't influence platform's non-negotiable terms; diffused responsibility; burdens on SMEs; vagueness of "converging decisions" and "inextricably linked"; AG Bobek warned against making everyone responsible for everything. Mitigations: "joint ≠ equal", Fashion ID's phase limit, EDPB rule that mere commercial benefit isn't enough.
  3. Power imbalance with hyperscale processors. EDPB: imbalance is no justification for non-compliant terms; controller that freely accepts a provider's terms assumes full responsibility; Opinion 22/2024: "practical difficulties … do not exonerate". Critics: ignores market reality — SMEs and public bodies can't negotiate with AWS, Microsoft or Google and chains are opaque. Suggested responses: SCCs, codes of conduct and certification (Art 28(5)), coordinated enforcement, collective procurement. Linked to paper vs effective compliance: "contractual clauses cannot – by themselves – demonstrate that the sufficient guarantees are effectively implemented" → supports continuous verification through audits rather than signed documents alone.
  4. Processor liability. Art 82(2): processor liable only for breaching processor-specific obligations or acting outside or contrary to lawful instructions; Art 82(4): parties jointly and severally liable for whole damage. Cimina: processor following strict instructions isn't liable; controller remains main responsible party towards data subjects. In India the debate is sharper (below).

THE UNIT 3 CONNECTION: Fiduciaries and processors under the DPDP Act 2023

  • Functional mapping: Data Fiduciary — s 2(i) = any person who, "alone or in conjunction with other persons", determines the purpose and means of processing personal data. Data Processor — s 2(k) = any person who processes personal data on behalf of a Data Fiduciary. Your Unit 1 notes stress classification is functional not contractual — what a party actually decides about purpose and means controls, regardless of what a commercial agreement calls it. Course notes teach Wirtschaftsakademie and Google Spain alongside these definitions, presenting search engine operators as "controllers / fiduciaries" → the CJEU's functional reasoning is the natural guide to reading the Indian terms.
  • Course running example: SafeShield Insurance, deciding to collect health data for underwriting, = fiduciary (decides why and how; bears primary obligations under s 8; answers to the Data Protection Board and data principals). Third-Party Administrator (TPA) processing claims for SafeShield = processor (follows instructions, no independent purpose, not directly answerable to principals).
  • Section 8(1) — "single point of accountability": fiduciary is responsible for compliance for processing "by it or on its behalf by a Data Processor", "irrespective of any agreement to the contrary", and also irrespective of a principal's own failure to perform her s 15 duties. In the notes' phrase: "outsourcing the processing does not outsource the liability." An indemnity clause can shift money between fiduciary and processor but cannot shift statutory responsibility → a processor's breach = the fiduciary's breach. Other duties repeat the point: s 8(5) security duty covers processing by processors; s 8(7)(b) requires the fiduciary to cause its processor to erase data.
  • Section 8(2) — the contractual link: a fiduciary may engage a processor for any activity related to offering goods or services to principals only "under a valid contract". ⚠ Slip: the Unit 1 slide attributes the mandatory processing agreement to s 8(6), but Unit 3 makes clear the hook is s 8(2); s 8(6) is the breach-notification duty. Striking gap: s 8(2) says nothing about what the contract must contain. The only content rule in the whole DPDP framework is Rule 6(1)(f) of the DPDP Rules 2025, requiring "appropriate provision in the contract … for taking reasonable security safeguards". Everything else must be borrowed from GDPR Art 28(3) or sectoral rules. Substantive fiduciary duties come into force only on 13 May 2027, so this is anticipatory compliance.
  • Comparison via SafeShield: under the GDPR, SafeShield = controller, TPA = processor, and Art 28 would require a binding contract with detailed mandatory content (processing only on documented instructions, staff confidentiality, Art 32 security, sub-processor conditions, assistance with data subject rights and Arts 32–36, deletion/return, information and audits). TPA would also carry direct duties (records Art 30(2), security Art 32, breach notice to controller Art 33(2)), could be fined and held liable under Art 82, and under Art 28(10) would become a controller if it used claims data for its own purposes. Under the DPDP Act, the TPA has no direct statutory duties: everything flows through the fiduciary and the contract. Sub-processors not expressly regulated (the 2022 draft's s 9(9) had allowed them "if permitted under its contract"); no equivalent of Art 28(10); no compensation provision for principals, only penalties imposed by the Board. So if SafeShield's TPA held claim files under an unsigned contract silent on security and attackers copied them (the Unit 3 problem question), the plea "the TPA is responsible" would fail under s 8(1), and the contract itself would breach s 8(2) and Rule 6(1)(f).
  • Joint control — wider gap: "in conjunction with other persons" in s 2(i) implies there can be joint fiduciaries, but the Act has nothing like Art 26: no required arrangement on who does what, no duty to give data subjects its essence, no rule like Art 26(3) letting them exercise rights against each fiduciary. India's model concentrates responsibility on the fiduciary — simple and protective in theory — but without the Art 28 machinery the supply-chain compliance gap is likely to be wider, especially in Indian IT and BPO outsourcing chains.

UNIT 3 EXAM TOOLKIT

1. "Which role?" decision method (use in problem questions)

  1. Is there a legal designation / legal task requiring processing? (Art 4(7) second limb; implicit competence) → that body = controller.
  2. Who decides the purpose (the "why") and the essential means (which data, whose data, how long, who gets access)? If yes → controller. If it merely acts on another's documented instructions, deciding at most non-essential means (software, hardware, detailed security) → processor.
  3. Is there a separate entity? (department ≠ processor; employees = Art 29; group company can be processor.)
  4. Does it have a purpose of its own or benefit beyond payment for services? → points to controller. "Merely being paid for services rendered" → processor.
  5. Is the processing impossible without more than one party's decisive influence (common or converging decisions; inseparable, inextricably linked)? → joint controllers for those stages only; before/after stages decided alone = sole controller.
  6. Does the processor go beyond instructions / use data for its own purposes? → Art 28(10) → treated as controller for that processing (and in breach).
  7. Contract label? Starting point only — functional test overrides ("not negotiable").
  8. Then apply consequences: Art 28 contract (8 clauses) / Art 26 arrangement (essence to data subjects; rights against each); liability (Art 82(2), (4), (5)); fines (Art 83(4)(a): €10m/2%); Indian analogue: s 8(1), (2), Rule 6(1)(f).

5. Common traps

  • Cimina's "not necessary to equally determine both purposes and means" vs EDPB "decide both" → reconcile via essential vs non-essential means.
  • GDPR: department ≠ controller (organisation is). EUDPR Art 3(8): a DG can be a controller. DGs not listed as processors.
  • Joint controllers ≠ equal; no access needed; control limited to jointly determined phases.
  • Specific authorisation: silence = denial. General authorisation: silence = authorisation.
  • Opinion 22/2024: no duty to systematically ask for sub-processing contracts; sampling; request where doubts.
  • Art 28(3)(a) "unless required by law": highly recommended, not strictly required; third-country wording doesn't infringe per se but isn't an instruction.
  • Art 28(3): eight clauses (a)–(h). (Guide in Unit 2 R7 lists 2 describing items + 9 bullets splitting sub-processor rules 28(2)/(4); count carefully.)
  • C4's "Article 7" attribution is wrong (should be Arts 5(2)/24).
  • DPDP: contract hook is s 8(2) (not s 8(6)). No Art 28 content, no direct processor duties, no Art 26, no compensation.
  • Dedalus: processor fined despite joint obligation argument; not possible under DPDP.
