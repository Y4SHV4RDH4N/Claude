THE BIG DEBATE THAT TIES UNIT 2 TOGETHER

Can notice-and-consent be saved? Three camps appear across the readings: | Camp | Position | Readings | |---|---|---| | Fix the form | Enforce consent that's active, specific, informed; design banners fairly | Planet49, Santos et al., Lindegren et al. | | Fix the content / reader | Make policies fully comprehensive; let LLMs/tools do the reading | Pałka et al., Pardo & Le Métayer (Pilot) | | Change the architecture | Don't hand data over at all; computation goes to the data | Najdi et al. | | Law + design + tech are complementary | Art. 25 GDPR (data protection by design & default) is the bridge | Exam-winning conclusion | Counterweights: McDonald & Cranor (reading costs too high → self-regulation fails), Dove & Chen (consent isn't always the right lawful basis), Custers & Vrabec (rights don't reach inferences).

R1. PLANET49 (C-673/17) — CJEU Grand Chamber, 1 Oct 2019

One sentence: consent to cookies via a pre-ticked checkbox is not valid; applies whether or not the cookie data is personal; users must be told how long cookies operate and whether third parties can access them.

Procedure: preliminary reference under Art. 267 TFEU from the German Federal Court of Justice (Bundesgerichtshof). Advocate General Szpunar (Court followed him on key points). ECLI:EU:C:2019:801.

Facts: Claimant = Bundesverband der Verbraucherzentralen (German consumer federation). Defendant = Planet49 GmbH, online gaming company. 24 Sept 2013 promotional lottery on dein-macbook.de. Users entered postcode, name and address, then saw two checkboxes:

  1. Not pre-ticked — consent to sponsors/cooperation partners contacting by post/phone/email/SMS. Link opened list of 57 companies each with "Unsubscribe"; if not unsubscribed from enough, Planet49 would pick up to 30 partners itself. Ticking was a condition of entering the lottery.
  2. Pre-ticked — consent to web analytics service Remintrex: cookies on device to track surfing/advertising partners' sites for interest-based advertising. Cookie held a random ID linked to name and address → personal data. Federation sued in Frankfurt (injunction + EUR 214). Regional court upheld in part; Higher Regional Court (OLG) said pre-ticked claim unfounded (user could untick; text clear). Federation appealed on point of law (Revision) → BGH referred to CJEU.

Legal framework:

  • Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive 2002/58 (as amended 2009/136): storing/accessing info in "terminal equipment" only if user has given consent after "clear and comprehensive information". Exceptions: technical storage solely for transmission; strictly necessary for a service the user explicitly requested.
  • ePrivacy doesn't define consent → Art. 2(f) points to old Directive 95/46 Art. 2(h): "freely given specific and informed indication of his wishes". Art. 7(a): "unambiguously". Art. 10: info "at least" (identity, purposes, recipients).
  • GDPR Art. 4(11): "freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous… by a statement or by a clear affirmative action". Art. 6(1)(a) consent as lawful basis. Art. 7(4): "utmost account" of whether service conditional on unnecessary consent. Recital 32: consent by clear affirmative act, e.g. ticking a box; "silence, pre-ticked boxes or inactivity should not constitute consent".
  • Questions: (1) Does a pre-ticked box the user must deselect give valid consent (and does it matter if data is personal)? (2) What "clear and comprehensive information" is required, incl. cookie duration and third-party access?
  • Temporal point: GDPR applied from 25 May 2018 (after reference), but injunction concerns future conduct → GDPR applies ratione temporis; Art. 94 GDPR repeals Directive 95/46, references read as to GDPR. Court answered under both.

Reasoning on pre-ticked boxes:

  • Autonomous and uniform interpretation of EU terms (SM Algerian kafala, Tarola), in light of wording, objectives, context, origins (Wightman).
  • Wording: Art. 5(3) requires user "has given" consent → action needed; Recital 17 mentions "ticking a box when visiting a website".
  • Context: Art. 2(h) "indication" implies active not passive behaviour (AG Szpunar). A pre-ticked box involves no active behaviour. Art. 7(a) "unambiguously" — Art. 7 is exhaustive list of lawful grounds (ASNEF, Breyer) so only active behaviour qualifies.
  • Evidential argument: impossible to know objectively whether user who didn't untick actually consented or was informed (may not have read text or noticed box) → passive behaviour is ambiguous.
  • Legislative history: original Art. 5(3) gave a "right to refuse" (opt-out); Directive 2009/136 replaced with "has given consent" → no more presumption.
  • "Specific": must relate to the processing in question, not inferred from wishes for another purpose → clicking to enter the lottery ≠ consent to cookies.
  • A fortiori under GDPR: Art. 4(11) "even more stringent"; Recital 32 expressly rules out pre-ticked boxes.
  • What the Court did NOT decide: whether making consent to advertising a precondition of entering the lottery (first checkbox) is compatible with "freely given" under Art. 7(4) — the coupling/bundling issue (not referred). Strong exam point.

Personal data or not? On facts it was personal data, but Court answered anyway: Art. 5(3) refers to "storing of information" and "gaining access to information already stored" — not limited to personal data. Protects user's private sphere (Recital 24: terminal equipment is part of private sphere protected by ECHR; spyware, web bugs, hidden identifiers). ePrivacy protects integrity and privacy of the device — broader than data protection.

Information required: "clear and comprehensive" = info allowing user to easily determine consequences of any consent and ensuring it's well informed; clear, detailed enough to understand how cookies function. For advertising cookies: duration (Art. 10 "at least" → non-exhaustive; long/unlimited duration = lots of info on surfing/visit frequency; GDPR Art. 13(2)(a) storage period) and third-party access (= "recipients or categories" under Art. 10(c)/Art. 13(1)(e)).

Three rulings: (1) Consent under Art. 2(f)/5(3) ePrivacy read with Art. 2(h) Dir 95/46 and Arts 4(11), 6(1)(a) GDPR is not validly constituted by a pre-checked box the user must deselect. (2) No difference whether data is personal or not. (3) Information must include duration of cookies and whether third parties may have access.

Why it matters / links: sets the legal floor — consent must be opt-in, active, specific, informed. Santos et al. cite it for "unambiguous indication through clear affirmative action" (unmarked "X" is ambiguous) and "informed of consequences" (careful: Court said "consequences of any consent he or she might give", not "consequences of refusing"). Links to Pałka (individuals rational; costs of self-management > expected losses → collective bodies like the Federation), Pilot, scoping review. Critique: "Planet49 regulates the click, not the choice architecture around it." Users can still click "Accept" without reading; dark patterns satisfy it formally; bundling left open; says nothing about design manipulation. Conclusion: cookie consent in EU is formally stronger but practically weak without design rules and enforcement; browser-level consent one fix. Law works after the event and depends on detection and proof → law, design, technology are complementary.

R2. SANTOS, GRAY, BIELOVA & AHUJA (2026) — "Usable and lawful: can consent be both?"

Argument: lawful and usable consent is achievable when design improves understandability and ease without unjustifiably steering users or breaking their trust. Uses heuristic evaluation to link legal requirements to usability.

Problem:

  • GDPR + ePrivacy: any website must get valid consent from EU users before tracking: freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous, revocable, "readable and accessible" (Arts 4(11), 7).
  • Cookie banners are often built with dark patterns: design choices that "supplant user value… in favor of shareholder value" by psychological/behavioural/perceptual tricks (Gray et al.; Mathur et al.). Codified in EU law 2023 by the Digital Services Act Art. 25 + Recital 67.
  • Usability (ISO 9241-11:2018) = "extent to which a system can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context of use". No universal standard for consent UI. Law has usability-like demands: Art. 12 GDPR ("concise, transparent, intelligible and easily accessible form, using clear and plain language"); Art. 7(2) + Recital 42 ("readable and accessible").
  • Gap: EDPB mentions usability only for icons/dashboards; UK ICO warns desktop banner may be unusable on mobile; Johansen derived 30 usable privacy goals from GDPR (not about consent); Habib/Cranor proposed 7 requirements for usable consent but didn't align with EU law or apply inspection methods. Nobody had systematically used an HCI usability-evaluation method to argue for consent that is both usable and lawful.

Method: heuristic evaluation (Nielsen & Molich 1990) = experts check interface against principles to find usability "bugs". Used evaluatively (find defects in existing designs) and generatively (imagine new ones) — bi-directional: usability → law (map each heuristic to consent requirements and banner designs) and law → usability (start from doctrine, case law, DPA decisions, guidelines to anticipate undeployed designs). Uses EDPB concept of UX / user journey. Only 3 of Nielsen's 10 heuristics (richest outcomes; limitation): A match between system and real world; B visibility of system status; C user control and freedom. Legal materials: binding (GDPR, ePrivacy, case law, DPA decisions) and non-binding (recitals, EDPB/WP29 guidelines, user studies). Interdisciplinary team (law, CS, design, HCI) discussed a shared spreadsheet over months. Result: 10 design choices (3 under A, 4 under B, 3 under C).

Heuristic A — match with real world ("speak the users' language")

  • A.1 The box with an "X" ("Unmarked X", Soe et al.). "X" usually means close, not consent/refuse. Low-level requirement: affirmative action design. Three lines: (1) UI must unambiguously indicate acceptance — Planet49, WP29 Opinion 15/2011, EDPB Guidelines 05/2020; Recital 32 allows "another statement or conduct which clearly indicates acceptance", WP208 "no limits as to the form consent can take" provided clear. (2) DPAs: "X" OK if explained — Spanish AEPD (explain less obvious actions); French CNIL (closing window can signal refusal if clearly indicated); Italian Garante (top-right "X" should close banner without consent, keep defaults, with warning); Latvian DPA ("X" = visual sign of closing). (3) Authors' own: "X" is ambiguous and should mean "Not yet" — neither accept nor reject. Bouma-Sims et al.: UK/US users who closed banner with "X": 24.0% expected no cookies, 17.2% expected some or all enabled. Evaluatively: unmarked X ≠ unambiguous consent. Generatively: make "Not yet"; if used as rejection, wording/framing must be tested.
  • A.2 Colours of accept/reject. Accept bright, reject dull (Facebook blue "Accept everything" vs grey essential-only). Traffic-light bias (green go, red stop; UK House of Lords recommended traffic-light privacy policies); brand colours push path of least resistance. Legal hook: unambiguity + low-level "balanced choices" (fairness, transparency, protection by default: Arts 5(1)(a), 25(2)) → accept/reject equal in size, tone, location, contrast. EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce (17 Jan 2023): can't impose colour standards; checks case by case. Dark pattern: "Hidden in Plain Sight". Conflicting DPA decisions: Danish DPA 2022 (JP/Politikens Hus): green "Accept all", red "Only necessary", grey "Customize" = "guiding", incompatible with Art 5(1)(a); Belgian DPA 113/2024 (Mediahuis): prominent "agree and close" vs light-grey alternatives "undoubtedly lead"; but Belgian 161/2023: controller decides, could make "refuse all" more attractive; Spanish DPA 2023/2024 colours misleading; German court LG Passau (9 Apr 2024): blue "Allow all cookies" does NOT breach Art 25(2) (common visual highlight, not default); Austrian DPA (Oct 2024): buttons must be equivalent in colour, size, contrast, placement, emphasis. Empirics: Gray "aesthetic manipulation"; Utz et al. 57.4% of banners steered users to accept by highlighting accept; ad-funded sites more often "concealed" reject by matching branding; Bravo-Lillo: salient fields help only if they match mental models.
  • A.3 Match between UI and what site does. Users expect browser to store exactly what they chose, nothing if nothing chosen. Matte et al.: consent stored before the user chose on 141 of 1,426 websites; "accept" stored after user rejected on 27 of 560; Bollinger et al.: trackers set despite refusal. Non-necessary trackers (Google Analytics) declared "necessary" (Mejtoft; Bollinger) = dark pattern "sneak into basket". Legal hook: "correct consent registration" (Art 7(1) — controller must demonstrate consent). Violations: (i) consent not registered on first visit → pressure on later visits (CNIL: keep choices; Italian DPA: repeated banners after refusal = "nagging"; ask again only if can't know choice stored e.g. cookies deleted, and ≥6 months passed); (ii) banner says "necessary" but non-necessary processing happens (Spanish DPA v Vueling). CMPs (consent management platforms) store consent → errors from site or CMP; hard to tell mistake from deliberate. Generatively: consent in the browser interface (errors sit with a few tens of browser vendors not millions of sites; draft ePrivacy Regulation Art. 10). Risk groups: low tech literacy, lower socioeconomic status, children.

Heuristic B — visibility of system status ("keep users informed… What can I do? What happened when I interacted?")

  • B.1 Visibility of the consent request — at extreme a consent wall (blocks site until accept/refuse). Engages "readable and accessible" (Art 7(2)) and "freely given" (7(4)); request must be clearly distinguishable (7(1)–(2)), may interrupt but not unnecessarily disruptive (Recital 32). Gray et al.: consent walls = dark patterns "forced action" and "obstruction"; Leenes: request may need to interrupt to be effective. Danish DPA: "right balance"; Italian DPA: banner big enough to perceptibly interrupt browsing. Behavioural research split: disruption obstructs freedom of choice (Sunstein) vs reflective patterns and friction (deliberate obstacles slowing users) help reason (Terpstra); Habib: without a full wall many keep browsing without deciding. Synthesis: friction is a necessary, not excessive, burden of informed consent; mobile worse.
  • B.2 Visibility of choice status. Users get no feedback after choosing (Kulyk: want confirmation). Hook: accessibility within "readable and accessible" (EDPB: data subject shouldn't have to seek out settings); teleological reading extends to visibility of choices already made; EDPB best practice "Data Protection Onboarding"; Feng et al.: systems treat consent as one-time/sequential but decisions are dynamic. Generatively: record, update, give feedback over time (on demand, periodic, just-in-time, at setup) without privacy fatigue; dashboard = high "fixing effort" but big market impact; some CMPs have persistent revocation icon.
  • B.3 Visibility of tracking status. Users want to see which identifiers are set, by whom, purpose, duration; "feedforward". Extensions (Disconnect, Ghostery) count trackers but miss many; server-side tracking harder. Link to informed consent only tangential (Recital 60, Art 13, WP29 distinguish necessary info vs further info for fair processing; tracking status = latter). Four challenges: interfaces not usable; flawed mental models; more technical knowledge confuses users and worsens threat appraisal (Strycharz); tracker purposes rarely map onto legal purposes. Preconditions: trackers grouped into meaningful categories and blocked per category not per company; users can install/configure/use tool with persistent indicators.
  • B.4 Visibility of consequences. Bielova et al. large-scale experiment: highlighting negative consequences of accepting increased refusal up to 3 times, high satisfaction. Two kinds: broken/less desirable experience after refusing; scope and risks of collection. Links: informed consent/transparency (Art 5(1)(a)), Recital 39 (awareness of risks), Planet49, CNIL v Google (2019) (users must understand specific consequences so not caught off guard); EDPB best practice "Explaining Consequences" neutrally. Counter-arguments: information overload; Leiser: little evidence transparency reduces agreement, raises transaction costs; Bielova: banner text doesn't affect decisions; Weatherill: disclosure in flawed bargaining environment may legitimise pernicious practice; Strycharz: legal knowledge empowers but lowers threat appraisal — "control paradox" (more control → more sharing); Borberg: users dismiss notices as annoyance. Synthesis: critical of law and design — both assume informed, rational, deliberate user; need choice-architecture cues without cognitive load (extra consequence text opens room for dark patterns like over-choice, long policies). Heuristic B rests on "more knowledgeable consumers are more empowered" but puts onus on data subject.

Heuristic C — user control and freedom ("clearly marked emergency exit", easy undo)

  • C.1 Yes / No / "Not yet" triad. Hook: revocability — Art 7(3) (withdrawal as easy as giving), Recital 42 (consent not free if person can't refuse/withdraw without detriment); Italian DPA supports "X" with warning defaults remain. Risk: choice overload (Iyengar & Lepper) → demotivation/postponement. Caveat: "Not yet" must not allow sites/CMPs to ask on every revisit → regulators set timeframe or number of visits. Generatively: add "Not yet" to consent walls with reminders or standard navigation element.
  • C.2 Dashboards and icons. Dashboards manage preferences after first choice (UK ICO); icons convey info quickly across languages/cultures. No high-level requirement; low-level: configurable banner with granular options incl. rejection. EDPB: just-in-time notices, hover text, dashboards, icons; Irish DPC: easy tool (radio button) to vary consent anytime; Italian DPA: footer link "Change your mind on cookies" + contest producing free CC BY 4.0 icon sets; Art 12(7) GDPR standardised icons; CNIL: "cookie" icon bottom left on every page. Icons should sit outside main reading flow but stay visible with text (Habib).
  • C.3 User-focused language. Facebook negative example: "accept everything" (what's "everything"?) vs "manage data definitions" (meaningless). Hook: "readable and accessible" → request must be intelligible to "average member of the intended audience" (WP29, Spanish DPA), purposes with "shared common understanding" (WP29 Op 03/2013); clear plain language = concrete definitive wording without ambiguous terms/legalese (EDPB); CNIL v Google (2019) meaningful choice given info architecture. Imprecise language can manipulate: dark pattern "false hierarchy", "aesthetic manipulation"; yet "there is no such thing as a neutral design" (Acquisti et al.), manipulation is context-dependent (Rudinow; Raz). Generatively: familiar accessible language, but fully neutral state may be impossible.
  • Paper's most striking legal idea: "Not yet" implies a new potential legal "right to be ambivalent" — users have only had right to accept or reject with same ease; they should also be free "to decide, or not to decide".

Results: 3 groups of the 10 design choices

  • Opportunities (usability + law aligned): "X" reinterpreted as "Not yet" (A.1), consent in browser (A.3), visibility/management of choices over time (B.2), "Not yet" option (C.1), dashboards & icons (C.2).
  • Challenges (aligned but hard for users): tracking status (B.3), consequences (B.4) — technical capacity, overload, "one size fits all" doubts; user-focused language (C.3) — content-based dark patterns.
  • Tensions (misaligned): colours (A.2) — traffic light is usable real-world convention but conditions automatic acceptance, vs balanced choices/fairness; visibility of consent request (B.1) — heuristic followed but friction meaningful or obstructive vs "freely given".
  • Friction: Guggenberger's Consent as Friction — friction preserves consent's warning function, enables deliberation, undermines economics of surveillance advertising → DPAs may insist. Macro fix: banners are a secondary task (Cranor) → system-wide automated consent via browser settings reduces requests and eases "freely given" tension while keeping visibility.
  • Usability vs dark patterns overlap: colour steering (A.2) = "hidden in plain sight", "aesthetic manipulation", "visual prominence"; UI-behaviour mismatch (A.3) = "sneak into basket". Poor usability often intentional but a usable design can still be a dark pattern (e.g. confirmshaming in user-focused language).
  • 5 evaluative stances and 7 generative identifications across the ten choices. Addressed to scholars, regulators, banner designers, CMPs.

Strengths: genuinely interdisciplinary; turns vague standards ("intelligible", "balanced") into inspectable design properties; generative as well as critical. Limits (some admitted): only 3 of 10 heuristics; expert reasoning over months, no new user testing; no concrete validated designs; relies on DPA decisions that contradict each other (esp. colours); heuristic evaluation inherently subjective; leans on others' user studies. Deeper critique: assumes a rational, informed user, undermined by its own sources (privacy paradox, control paradox) → links to Pałka: real problem = cost of self-management, to be reduced or shifted to collective bodies like consumer associations. Links: Planet49 — banner design routinely falls below the floor it sets (judgment regulates click, not choice architecture). Santos = "fix the form" camp. Provides evidence for both sides of friction debate; offers "Not yet", browser-level consent, regulator-set re-ask intervals. No neutral design + dark patterns persist → design alone not enough; law, design and technology complementary, with Art. 25 GDPR as bridge.

R3. PAŁKA, LAGIOIA, LIEPINA, LIPPI & SARTOR (2025) — "Make privacy policies longer and appoint LLM readers"

Reversal: scholars (Calo, Waldman, Emami-Naeini, Yerby & Vaughn) prioritised comprehensibility (simplify!). Authors say insist on full comprehensiveness even if policies become much longer, because simplifying/extracting answers can now be automated with LLMs (GPT-4). Core claim: the trade-off between comprehensiveness and comprehensibility exists only while humans are the sole readers. Not expecting consumers to paste policies into ChatGPT; automated warnings, specialised chatbots and collective bodies (consumer associations) could use LLMs. Counterfactual hypothesis: if policies were fully comprehensive, then LLMs could adequately answer consumers' questions.

Motivating example: ask your phone which corporations know you listened to Frankly, Mr. Shankly (The Smiths) and ordered baked tofu, and why. Policies use vague catch-alls ("we collect data about your use of our service") and open-ended lists ("such as", "including", "for example") → even a hypothetical expert with unlimited resources couldn't answer. Matters because of the inference economy (Solow-Niederman): ML draws sensitive conclusions (food/music → race/political convictions).

Theory: rational non-reading. People don't read policies (Bakos; Obar & Oeldorf-Hirsch), misunderstand them (Reidenberg), would need hundreds of hours/year (McDonald & Cranor 2008). Usual remedies: shorter/simpler policies or "privacy nutrition labels" (Kelley; Emami-Naeini). But Ben-Shahar & Chilton (2016): even simplified disclosures have close-to-zero effect → problem is content not just form. Authors' earlier work (Pałka 2023): vague categories/purposes let firms do things consumers can't predict → not reading may be rational: nothing to learn. Reframes the privacy paradox: gap between stated preferences (care) and revealed (click "agree"). Solove 2021, Waldman 2020 blame cognitive biases, bad design, deliberate obstacles. Authors: people care but rationally judge cost of managing privacy > expected loss (severity × probability). Evidence: stated preferences, public uproar after breaches, market reality (firms mostly use data for profit while keeping it secure → detrimental use more common than unwanted disclosure). Fix = cost reduction in two prongs: require fully comprehensive policies; let users query via LLMs. Not every consumer must; enough if some consumers and collective bodies expose bad practices.

NLP/law context: value of a policy depends on who/what reads it. CLAUDETTE (automated unfair-clause detection, Lippi 2019) gave less convincing results on GDPR policies (complex, incomplete, vague — Contissa 2018; Liepina 2019). LLMs (transformer architecture; Vaswani 2017) pass bar exams; PolicyGPT (Tang 2023) improved extraction → blurs machine-readable vs human-readable.

EU vs US: EU: policy = mandated disclosure / information obligation under Arts 12–14 GDPR, never itself source of right to process (that's Art 6 basis e.g. consent/contract); horizontal GDPR binds public & private controllers; enforcement by DPAs' administrative fines. US: no federal statute, state laws from CalOPPA, body of FTC decisions ("common law of privacy" — Solove & Hartzog) — failing to publish = deceptive omission; scholars debate whether policy = boilerplate contract (Davis & Marotta-Wurgler yes; Solove & Schwartz no); if contract: Carnival Cruise Lines v Shute (forum selection), AT&T Mobility v Concepcion, American Express v Italian Colors (arbitration). US model = "notice and choice"; EU has no equivalent name. Paper treats policies solely as information obligations (reduce information asymmetry).

Legal tension & authors' standard: California AG Kamala Harris 2014: "comprehensive overview" AND "plain, straightforward language… short sentences". EU: Arts 13–14 detail vs Art 12(1) "concise, transparent, intelligible and easily accessible form, clear and plain language" — "obvious tension". Unit of disclosure = single act of processing (Art 4(2)); for each: categories of data, purpose per category, legal basis (Art 6(1); name the interest if legitimate interests so proportionality can be assessed), storage period/criteria, recipients with role (controller/processor), purpose of sharing and its legal basis. WP29 Transparency Guidelines (EDPB-endorsed): "We may use your personal data to develop new services" = bad; "We will retain your shopping history and use details of products you previously purchased to make suggestions…" = good. Authors: "good" example looks fine alone but scales badly and still omits legal basis/storage period Art 13 requires — "At what point does the short and easy-to-read privacy policy become devoid of meaning?" Their standard: policy should contain enough to answer ALL REASONABLE CONSUMER QUESTIONS (fits GDPR informational self-determination and US law).

Study design — 9 questions about fictional "Orderoo" (food delivery chosen: banal data correlates with race/politics, interests health insurers, competitive market, wide-but-manageable catalogue): Q1 what data? Q2 purposes for email? Q3 who gets geolocation? Q4 data processed on consent + purposes? Q5 data shared with Facebook? Q6 shared with insurers? Q7 categories collected automatically? Q8 how to contact to exercise rights? Q9 how long keep delivery address? Scenario 1 (real policies): two legal experts read Deliveroo, DoorDash, Glovo, Just Eat, Wolt (downloaded 10 Jan 2024; 4,379 words (Deliveroo) to 13,765 (Glovo)), no time limit, marking each answer clear/ambiguous. 100% agreement. Only 2 of 9 answerable: Q6 (insurers, clear in 4 of 5 — not DoorDash) and Q8 (contact, clear in all 5). Causes: vague language ("personal data", "may", "as necessary", examples instead of exhaustive lists — Wolt's "for example, to… market", Deliveroo's "as long as reasonably necessary", DoorDash's "including, without limitation"), failure to give purposes and retention per data type (DoorDash: retention "may vary"). Not one policy fully GDPR-compliant; quality not linked to length. Second check (7 Oct 2024): Amazon, AliExpress, eBay, Shein, Zalando (4,533–20,512 words): same result, 100% agreement, only Q6 and Q8 answerable — confirms Contissa et al. 2018. Proposal — fully comprehensive format: law should require fully comprehensive policies, ideally standardised, relax comprehensibility requirement, leave automated tools to tailor content. Organised by data category, one paragraph per type, specific categories ("amount of time you spend in the app from opening to order", not "usage information"). Non-ambiguity test: if a reader could reasonably ask "but what do you mean by category X?", split or define exhaustively; "such as" and "for example" banned. Each paragraph: category, source, purpose + explanation, legal basis + explanation, storage period; if shared: recipient, role (processor/controller), purpose, legal basis. Written first for human experts so humans can always verify what an LLM says. Mock Orderoo policy = 6,880 words: email used for account access (contractual necessity), receipts (legal obligation), marketing (legitimate interest), profiling; shared with named processors (Cloud711, Microsoft); kept until account deletion + 3 months; geolocation shared with Facebook and Google as controllers for ad delivery on candid basis "legitimate interest: to make profit of available data". Scenario 2 (LLMs on mock policy): Q1,2,3,8 = simple retrieval; Q4,5,7 spread across document; Q6 needs negative answer (no insurers) → tests hallucination. Prompt 1 (Jan 2024: rely solely on text, mention everything, don't simplify); Prompt 2 (Oct 2024: adds "Please reason step by step" = chain-of-thought). Only GPT-4 (closed; 128k-token window) coped with long text; compared with Llama2-7B (open), no fine-tuning; each run 5 times; legal experts checked every answer; GPT-4 cost < $3 total. Metrics: precision (share of items given that are correct), recall (share of correct items found). GPT-4 Prompt 1: 6 of 9 questions flawless on every run, 33 of 45 fully correct, never hallucinated; weakest Q7 (recall 78.2) — missed "contact list details" and "call recordings" but answered correctly as yes/no (prompt sensitivity). Chain-of-thought: flawless on only 5 of 9, 29 of 45 correct; Q4 recall fell 92→72; new errors (inference "to determine trends in purchase behavior"), repetitions → no advantage, could reduce performance. Llama2-7B too messy for standard metrics → error types: Prompt 1 only 1 of 45 correct, 91% missing info, 62% hallucination; CoT: skipped 9 questions, 2 right, hallucinated 64%, called document "fictional"; invented "according to the document" that address kept "18 months after last order" and contact "123 Main St, Anytown". Scenario 3 (Prompt 1 on real food-delivery policies): of 45 answers each: GPT-4 9 correct, Llama 1. GPT-4 0% hallucination, but 47% missed information, 33% contained inferences; Llama hallucinated 38%, irrelevant info 58%; Llama said DoorDash would not share data in a merger (contradicting policy — "extremely dangerous"); mentioned "payment information" in 20 of 45 answers but correct in only 8 (relied on training-data typical policies). Key inference: errors occurred where policies lacked clear/comprehensive info — "due to the poor drafting of the analyzed policies"; on such policies LLMs can mislead consumers.

Conclusions: with LLMs both comprehensiveness and comprehensibility can be achieved; old trade-off no longer justifies "settling"; fully comprehensive policies are pro-consumer provided automated Q&A available. Needs more CS (linking answers to source text, automated quality control) and rethinking by lawmakers — "promising, albeit still heterodox, direction". Strengths: concrete legal proposal backed by experiments; clever counterfactual (mock vs real policies); shows bottleneck = content not reader capacity. Objections: Scenario 2 rests on one mock policy written by the authors (may be LLM-friendly), only 9 questions in one sector, 5 runs of stochastic models whose versions date fast; shifts burden to consumers (digital divide); open who is liable if LLM wrong; corporate resistance (trade secrets, flexibility); can't reveal mismatch between what a policy says and what a firm does; longer policies may entrench notice-and-consent (firms disclose, individuals choose) rather than substantive limits on processing (contrast Najdi, Santos). Typo: calls the consent question "Q6" at one point though it's Q4. Debate position: fix lies in content of policies and who reads them — unlike Planet49/Santos (fix form), Pardo & Le Métayer (automate user's side), Najdi (change architecture). Rational-user reframing points to collective bodies (consumer associations — the kind that brought Planet49). On Art 12 vs Arts 13–14 tension: leads "longer and complete" camp vs simplifiers; middle path = layered design with complete, machine-queryable base layer beneath summaries, icons, LLM answers.
