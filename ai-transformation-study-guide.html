Strategic Digital Transformation with AI

How converting, using and transforming with digital technology differ, why AI changes the strategic game, and why most AI pilots stall before they change the enterprise.

Topic 1.1

Digitization vs. digitalization (digital transformation): implications

In one line

Digitization changes the format of information. Digitalization changes how the business works and earns money. Digital transformation changes the whole organization, including its people and culture, so that it can keep changing.

The three terms

Digitization
To “change from analog into digital form” (Gartner IT Glossary). Scanning paper files, turning a paper form into a PDF, recording meter readings with a sensor. The data becomes digital, but the process stays the same.
Digitalization
“The use of digital technologies to change a business model and provide new revenue and value-producing opportunities; it is the process of moving to a digital business” (Gartner IT Glossary). The digital data is now used to run processes differently: e-invoicing that matches orders automatically, mobile banking, predictive maintenance.
Digital transformation
Organization-wide change in strategy, structure, processes, culture and skills, built on digitalization. Neeley and Leonardi stress that it depends on people having a digital mindset, and that it never finishes: “Digital transformation is not a goal that one achieves; it is the means to achieve one's unique goals.”

The syllabus writes “Digitalization (Digital transformation)” because many writers use the two words loosely. In the exam, show all three as a ladder of widening scope and say that digital transformation is the broadest.

Cases from the reading

Atos · IT services

CEO Thierry Breton (from 2008) doubled the workforce to 100,000 and launched the Digital Transformation Factory to certify 35,000 staff in digital skills and AI. The programme was voluntary, backed by internal marketing, peer and manager nominations and rewards. Over 70,000 people certified within three years. Revenue grew from $6.2 billion to close to $13 billion by 2019.

Moderna · biotech

A “tech company that happens to do biology.” Three components: huge access to data, cloud computing, and the ability to build AI algorithms for R&D. Data flows freely across teams. Within months of the Covid-19 outbreak it had built about 20 algorithms for vaccine and therapeutic work.

Unilever · consumer goods

400+ brands in 190 countries. Rahul Welde set up 300 remote, global agile teams of 10 people. Three parts: enabling technology and tools, redesigning processes for them, and making sure people had access, skills and motivation to use them.

Philips · health technology

Moved from supplying health products to digital solutions. Built an AI-powered learning platform with Cornerstone OnDemand where staff share “playlists” of lessons and find mentors. Leaders act as teachers.

Implications for managers

Redesign the process before you digitize it. Moderna CEO Stéphane Bancel: “Digitization only makes sense once the processes are done. If you have crappy analog processes, you'll get crappy digital processes.” He deliberately did not separate digital from operational excellence. The same idea returns in Topics 1.2, 1.3 and 4.3.

Mindset matters as much as tools. A digital mindset is “a set of attitudes and behaviors that enable people and organizations to see how data, algorithms, and AI open up new possibilities and to chart a path for success.” Employees who build one are more successful, more satisfied, more likely to be promoted, and have more portable skills.

Adoption depends on buy-in and confidence. Managers should place each team member on the Adoption Matrix below and move them toward “inspired.”

Pair a bold stroke with a long march. A bold stroke gets attention: a major reorganisation, an acquisition, reallocating resources, hiring a digital transformation czar who reports to the CEO, or announcing a legacy system's retirement. The long march follows: assess how people feel, build buy-in and confidence, keep training.

Upskill the people you have. Hiring enough digital talent is nearly impossible, so upskilling must supplement recruitment. Recruit internal influencers with a digital mindset as champions.

Business leaders, not only IT, choose tools. Most transformation tools are cloud-based (SaaS) and teams can buy them directly. Leaders should pick tools based on which local activities drive larger goals, then remove obstacles to adoption.

Treat change as constant. Classic change theory moves from current state to transition to future state. In a digital world the transition never ends. “A leader's task is not simply to adapt; it is to be adaptive.”

Promote buy-in (bottom row)

For the oppressed and indifferent. Stress that digital is a critical frontier for the company, run an internal marketing campaign so people can picture a digitally powered company, and help people see themselves as important contributors.

Promote confidence (left column)

For the oppressed and frustrated, once buy-in exists. Give hands-on experience with digital tools, share stories of peers who learned (people build confidence vicariously), and keep encouraging and reinforcing.

Six practices of a successful training programme

  1. Set a company-wide goal for training.
  2. Design learning for all functional roles, not only technical ones.
  3. Prioritise virtual delivery so learning scales.
  4. Motivate people through campaigns, awards and incentives.
  5. Make sure managers understand the offerings so they can guide their teams.
  6. Encourage projects with digital components and hands-on learning.

From the authors' book, The Digital Mindset (2022): the “30% rule.” You do not need to become a programmer. About 30% fluency in data, algorithms and AI, like knowing 30% of a language's words, is enough to ask good questions and work with technical experts. The book organises the mindset into three approaches: collaboration, computation and change.

Exam tip

Define all three terms, give one example at each level, then state the implication. The strongest single line: “Digitize a process only after you have redesigned it.” Support it with the Moderna quote and the Atos upskilling result.

Topic 1.2

AI as a strategic game-changer: transformation with AI

In one line

AI changes the economics of building and running a firm. The cost, time and head count needed to learn, build, sell and operate are collapsing, so AI reshapes strategy and competition, not just efficiency.

Why AI is different from earlier IT

Earlier software followed rules that people wrote. AI systems infer how to produce predictions, content, recommendations or decisions from the data they receive (OECD definition, see Topic 2.1). Newer agentic systems also act.

Iansiti and Lakhani (Competing in the Age of AI, 2020) show that firms rebuilt around data and AI run an “AI factory” of data pipelines, algorithms, experimentation platforms and infrastructure. This removes the traditional limits on scale, scope and learning that constrained firms for centuries.

Lee, Mantia and McNeill (HBR, 2026) name five converging trends: LLMs mature enough to reason and plan; LLMs that act as agents when given data and permission; frameworks that let multiple agents coordinate; low-cost APIs connecting IT systems; and falling cloud costs. Together they produce a “second great compression of entrepreneurship”: the cost, time and head count needed to build a prototype, test it and improve it have all collapsed.

From tools to teammates

Tool
Large language model

A tutor and assistant (ChatGPT, Claude). Compresses months of learning about an industry, market or regulation into days. Helps founders write business plans and pitch decks.

Worker
AI agent

Performs complex tasks when given context and connected to tools. A financial-analyst agent given the business plan and market research, with spreadsheet access, builds five-year projections.

Team
Agentic AI system

A network of specialised agents, coordinated by a digital manager, that plan, reason, adapt and pursue outcomes. At NTT Data it spots bugs across thousands of support tickets, writes and tests fixes, and creates help articles. Ema's onboarding assistant orders the badge, assigns a desk, stages the laptop, activates payroll, enrols benefits and books introductions, work that spans HR, IT and three managers.

The five forces of disruptive change

These forces reinforce one another, so the advantage compounds over time.

Why incumbents are threatened

  • Structural mismatch. Startups form around clean workflows, tight learning loops and data models built for continuous learning. Incumbents evolved for stability, specialisation and control.
  • Roles and incentives. Staff who mastered stable processes are unprepared for AI-augmented roles. Middle managers whose authority comes from controlling information resist changes to their span of control. Christensen warned that processes serving core markets “work so well that they blind those companies.”
  • Technical debt. Undocumented workflows, siloed data, brittle integrations and manual exceptions. The most common mistake is to automate before re-architecting. Michael Hammer (1990): “stop paving the cow paths … obliterate them and start over.”
  • Economics. When a rival gets five times the output from the same resources, or traction with one-fifth of the capital, the cost curve of a sector shifts. Incumbents must cut prices (risking stability) or hold them (risking slow erosion).

Where incumbents still have an edge

AI-native startups carry quality, reliability and compliance risk. Agents do not always give the same result twice, so startups must build audit trails, compliance reporting, incident response, root-cause analysis and postmortems that incumbents have refined for decades. Agentic AI also raises demand for top-tier employees with deep industry knowledge, and incumbents already employ them. As LLMs become commodities, the differentiators are proprietary data, the ability to re-engineer workflows around AI, and expertise in blending AI speed with human judgment.

What incumbents should do now

  1. Run a vulnerability audit against the five forces. Find workflows that slow down, rely on too many handoffs, or force decisions without good information: claims, referrals, loan operations, HR onboarding, supply-chain coordination. That is where an AI-native rival would attack first.
  2. Start with a bounded, high-friction process that matters, not an easy pilot. NTT Data's proposal agent writes a first draft of an RFP response in 20 minutes instead of hundreds of staff hours, and raised both response volume and win rate.
  3. Re-architect before you automate. Remove steps that no longer matter, clarify who makes each decision, simplify the flow. An agent in a messy process reproduces its flaws faster.
  4. Partner with AI-native ventures to see how a clean workflow runs. The U.S. Army's 18th Airborne Corps built the Maven Smart System with industry field engineers working directly with soldiers.
  5. Strengthen data quality and make handoffs to humans explicit. Exceptions should be visible, not buried in email. Human-in-the-loop is a vital part of agentic governance.
  6. Prepare people for shifting roles. Humans take judgment, edge cases and empathy; agents take setup, coordination and monitoring. Tampa General Hospital's call-centre agent “Aimee” handles routine inquiries and escalates complex cases, while staff control her knowledge base and logic.
“History suggests that waiting until the threat becomes undeniable is waiting too long.”Lee, Mantia and McNeill, HBR, July–August 2026

Topic 1.3

The last-mile problem: key issues and strategies to resolve them

In one line

AI pilots succeed on their own, but the gains do not scale, because the organization around them (processes, governance, roles and professional identity) has not been redesigned. “The bottleneck simply shifts.”

Background

The Frontier Firm Initiative (FFI) at Harvard Business School was founded by the Digital Data Design (D^3) Institute and Microsoft. It held a closed-door summit with senior leaders from about a dozen global organizations in healthcare, banking and industrial manufacturing. These firms were enthusiastic adopters, with hundreds of AI deployments and near-universal use of M365 Copilot, ChatGPT Enterprise or GitHub Copilot. Yet their tools formed “islands of productivity.” An agent could draft a complex contract in seconds, and the contract then sat in manual legal review for two weeks because risk and governance had not been redesigned for an agentic pace.

The seven frictions

FrictionWhat goes wrongExample from the summit
1. Proliferation of pilotsFirms are “pilot-rich but transformation-poor.” There is no repeatable path from proof of concept to a standard operating model.A bank with 250+ applications linking LLMs to its systems; a food and beverage firm with pilots across 185 countries; an apparel firm that automated 18,000+ finance processes.
2. Productivity gapIndividual gains never reach the balance sheet. Saved time is re-absorbed into meetings and email unless roles and budgets are redesigned to capture it.A payments network where 99%+ of staff use copilots; a manufacturer with double-digit gains for thousands of engineers. Finance still cannot find headcount shifts or shorter cycle times.
3. Process debtAI exposes brittle, exception-ridden processes built up over decades. The workflow itself must be re-architected, which needs techno-functional leaders who understand business logic and technical limits.A healthcare insurer where AI surfaced inconsistencies faster than it could resolve them; a professional-services firm in 170+ countries running the same process dozens of ways.
4. Identity problem of tribal knowledgeCritical tacit knowledge lives in long-tenured employees' heads and confers status. Asking experts to encode their judgment threatens their identity, so they resist.An engineering consultancy framed this as an identity problem, not a reskilling issue.
5. Governance in an agentic worldWhen agents take actions, traditional governance becomes a bottleneck. Human-in-the-loop controls that work for single cases collapse under multi-agent systems, leaving an accountability gap.An asset servicer running 100+ agents is planning for tens of thousands. Leaders now ask HR-style questions: how to onboard, evaluate, secure and retire a digital worker.
6. Architectural complexityA patchwork of AI across hyperscalers and application stacks. Tension between one platform (speed) and many vendors (flexibility). Platforms evolve faster than projects.An apparel firm spent months making agents talk across SAP, Microsoft and Google. A manufacturer is tempted to reset projects each time a new model appears.
7. Efficiency trapFraming AI as cost-cutting, like “a new form of offshoring,” makes middle managers defensive, narrows ambition, and risks hollowing out judgment and storytelling.A data and analytics advisory firm warned of this. The biggest gains come from rethinking value creation, not shaving minutes off tasks.

The blueprint for the AI-native (“frontier”) firm

The article presents its pillars as “direct structural counterweights” to the frictions and says they form one operating model, not a menu of tactics. Firms may start with their most painful friction, but long-term success needs a synchronised effort.

Pillar
Clean-sheet process redesign

Stop bolting AI onto legacy workflows. Ask: “If we were building this process today with modern AI agents, how would we do it?” Map the outer loop (strategic planning) and the inner loop (execution tasks) before writing code. Use agent-centric mapping to decide where an agent is the worker and where a human is the orchestrator.

Pillar
Strategic knowledge capture

Treat tribal knowledge as a strategic asset. Capture pre-harvest context (rules and logic needed before a task starts) and execution context (nuanced data passed between agents during a task). Pair senior experts with designers and frame it as “legacy building.” New higher-status roles: AI process architects and knowledge stewards.

Pillar
Managing the digital workforce

Move from model governance to agent governance. Control planes define who can create agents, what actions they may take, and where accountability sits. Agentic control planes are central dashboards that track agent performance, security permissions and accuracy, much like HR performance management.

Pillar
Role redesign and career pathing

Human roles shift toward design, orchestration and interpretation, so AI feels like career evolution, not erosion. Job descriptions ask for “learning agility,” “domain depth” and ownership of “agent orchestration.” Engineering firms add assurance and systems-thinking roles, advisory firms formalise storytellers, and banks assign managers to digital workers.

The article refers to “seven strategic pillars,” but the course-pack version describes four of them under their own headings. Learn these four by name. If your lecture notes list more, add them to the mapping below.

Topic 2.1

Understanding AI and its impact: the paradigm-shifting role of AI

In one line

AI moves computers from following instructions to inferring outputs and now to acting on goals. Value therefore moves from doing tasks to designing, directing and checking work.

What AI is

“An AI system is a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments. Different AI systems vary in their levels of autonomy and adaptiveness after deployment.”OECD definition, revised November 2023 (also the basis of the EU AI Act definition)

Three words carry the meaning: infers (it works out the output instead of following a fixed rule), autonomy (how much it acts without a human) and adaptiveness (whether it keeps learning after deployment).

Topic 2.2

The paradox of prompt engineering: workslop, its root cause and consequences

In one line

Generative AI makes it effortless to produce work that looks finished, so the real thinking gets skipped and pushed onto whoever receives it. The easier generation becomes, the more value sits in human framing, context and review.

The paradox

The syllabus calls this a paradox. The four points below are how the readings explain it.

1. The effort paradox

AI saves effort for the creator but adds it for the receiver. Workslop “transfers the effort from creator to receiver.”

2. The skill paradox

Good output needs a good prompt, and a good prompt needs the domain knowledge and context people hoped AI would replace. Gen AI “is not appropriate for all tasks, nor can it read minds.”

3. The durability paradox

Prompt engineering was hailed as the job of the future, yet as models get better at reading intent, clever wording matters less. The lasting skill is problem formulation (Acar, HBR 2023).

4. The mandate paradox

Mandates to “use AI everywhere” raise usage but lower quality. One executive: “Quantity and ‘use of AI’ is prized more than quality and effectiveness.”

Problem formulation (Acar)What it means
DiagnosisIdentify the core problem for AI to solve, looking past symptoms to the real objective.
DecompositionBreak a complex problem into smaller, single-focus sub-problems.
ReframingChange the angle from which the problem is viewed to open new solutions.
Constraint designSet the boundaries: input, process and output limits the solution must meet.

What workslop is

“AI generated work content that masquerades as good work, but lacks the substance to meaningfully advance a given task.”Niederhoffer et al., BetterUp Labs with the Stanford Social Media Lab, HBR.org, September 2025

Examples include well-formatted slides, long structured reports, articulate-sounding summaries of papers written by non-experts, and code that is unhelpful, incomplete or missing context. The term adapts “AI slop,” the low-quality AI content clogging social media.

Workslop is not ordinary cognitive offloading. Offloading to a machine (searching instead of remembering) moves effort from a person to technology. Workslop “uniquely uses machines to offload cognitive work to another human being,” who must decode it, infer missing or false context, and often redo it.

Root causes

The January 2026 follow-up: “It isn't a failure of the technology. It's a failure of management.” Workslop comes from pressure at both ends of the organization.

  • Unclear mandates. Indiscriminate imperatives yield indiscriminate usage. Blanket mandates also model “passing the buck” instead of giving policies, best practices and norms.
  • Overload and depletion. Employees say they send subpar AI work mainly because they are stretched too thin.
  • Low trust. BetterUp's data on 400,000+ employees shows a 2–6% decline since 2020 in foundational mindsets such as focus, agility and strategic planning. Edelman's 2025 Trust Barometer recorded an “unprecedented” three-point fall in employee trust in employers.
  • Mindset. “Pilots” (high agency, high optimism) use gen AI 75% more at work and 95% more outside work than “passengers,” and use it to enhance their creativity. Passengers are more likely to use AI to avoid doing work.
  • Self-reported sending. 53% admit sending low-quality AI work at least some of the time. One in 10 say half or more of the AI work they send is unhelpful.

How to stop it

Build competence and control

People who feel competent and in control with AI tools are half as likely to create workslop. Invest in AI literacy, bring hidden “shadow” practices into the open, and forward-deploy AI engineers into teams.

Build team trust

Trust in the team reduced workslop by 61%. People must feel safe to admit AI use, raise quality concerns and ask for feedback.

Read it as a signal

Each instance signals an organization under strain. Blaming individual laziness is the fundamental attribution error: it ignores the situation.

LevelSystem-level response (2026 article)
CultureRebuild trust through everyday collaboration: feedback, questions and dialogue.
PracticeClear norms for when and how to use AI, and explicit review processes that reinforce human judgment instead of offloading it.
AccountabilitySomeone fluent in technology and relationships, such as a forward-deployed “AI collaboration architect,” who finds friction and links AI to specific outcomes.

From the 2025 article: model purposeful AI use, set guardrails and acceptable-use norms, frame AI as a collaborative tool rather than a shortcut, adopt a pilot mindset, and “uphold the same standards of excellence for work done by bionic human-AI duos as by humans alone.”

A personal check before you send AI-assisted work (our synthesis of the recommendations): Do I understand the task and the reader? Did I give the AI the context? Topic 2.3

Building AI agents using no-code platforms

No lab exam. You are expected to narrate what an agent does and how it is put together.

In one line

A no-code agent is a language model given a job description (instructions), reference material (knowledge), hands (tools and actions), a start signal (triggers) and rules (guardrails), assembled in a visual builder instead of code.

Chatbot, automation, agent, agentic system

TypeHow it behavesExample
Chatbot or assistantAnswers questions in a conversation; takes no actions, or only scripted ones.A policy FAQ bot
Workflow automationRuns a fixed sequence of steps when triggered. Deterministic.“New form entry → add a spreadsheet row → send an email”
AI agentPursues a goal, decides which steps and tools to use, acts in other systems, asks for missing information and escalates.An onboarding agent that sets up everything for a new hire

Anatomy of a no-code agent

Common no-code and low-code platforms

Microsoft Copilot Studio (agents inside Microsoft 365), Zapier and Make (trigger-and-action automations with AI steps and agents), n8n (visual, low-code workflows with an AI agent node), and custom GPTs in ChatGPT (instructions, knowledge files and actions). Product names and buttons change quickly, so in the exam describe the components, not the menus.

Steps to build one

  1. Define the job. Who uses it, what goal it serves, how success is measured, and what it must never do.
  2. Write the instructions. Role, tone, step order, rules and when to ask a clarifying question.
  3. Add knowledge. Upload or connect the policies, FAQs and data it should rely on.
  4. Connect tools and actions with the least access they need.
  5. Set triggers: a chat message, a schedule or a system event.
  6. Add guardrails and human handoff for high-stakes or uncertain cases.
  7. Test with realistic, unusual and adversarial cases.
  8. Publish to a channel.
  9. Monitor and improve: accuracy, resolution rate, escalations and cost, ideally on a control plane.

Impact of AI on Work: Strategic Implications for Business

How AI reshapes the work, the worker and the workplace; which competencies rise and fall; and how workflows move from automation to autonomy.

Topic 4.1

Future of work (SHRM): three key aspects

In one line

SHRM frames the future of work around three linked areas: the work, the worker and the workplace. AI is changing all three at once, so HR and business leaders must redesign them together.

SHRM (the Society for Human Resource Management) describes itself as the authority on “all things work, worker, and workplace,” and organises its future-of-work research the same way. Deloitte uses the same three dimensions and labels them by question: work (the what), workforce (the who) and workplace (the where).

Aspect 1 · the what

Work

  • Tasks are unbundled: routine ones are automated or augmented by AI, and jobs are increasingly defined by skills instead of fixed titles.
  • Work is redesigned around human-agent teams. Humans take judgment, edge cases and empathy; agents take setup, coordination and monitoring.
  • SHRM 2026 research: 39% of organizations say AI has shifted job responsibilities, 24% say it created new roles, and only 7% report job displacement.
Aspect 2 · the who

Worker (workforce)

  • SHRM expects demand to rise for workers who can work alongside and manage AI systems; those who cannot adapt risk being left behind.
  • 57% of organizations report more upskilling and reskilling opportunities because of AI (SHRM 2026).
  • The talent pool is a continuum: full-time staff, contractors, gig workers, internal talent marketplaces, and now digital workers (agents) that must be onboarded, evaluated and retired.
  • Well-being and identity: burnout and depletion drive workslop, and experts fear becoming less essential.
Aspect 3 · the where

Workplace

  • Flexible and hybrid arrangements; the workplace becomes a network of relationships, not just a location (Deloitte).
  • SHRM's 2026 State of the Workplace: as AI adoption accelerates, “the true engine of organizational resilience remains human leadership and culture.” Employers name effective leadership and management as their top workplace need.
  • Norms, trust and AI governance. Team trust cut workslop by 61%.

Topic 4.2

Shifting competency requirements in a transformed workplace

In one line

As AI takes over execution, the scarce competencies become judgment, problem formulation, orchestrating people and agents, continuous learning, deep domain knowledge and the human skills AI cannot supply.

What the data says (WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025)

  • Employers expect 39% of key skills to change by 2030.
  • About 170 million jobs created and 92 million displaced between 2025 and 2030, a net gain of 78 million (7% of current employment).
  • Analytical thinking is the top core skill; seven in ten companies call it essential. It is followed by resilience, flexibility and agility, and by leadership and social influence.
  • AI and big data is the fastest-growing skill, followed by networks and cybersecurity, and technological literacy. Creative thinking, resilience, curiosity and lifelong learning are also rising.

Six competency clusters

Digital and AI fluency

The 30% rule: enough grasp of data, algorithms and AI to ask good questions and work with experts. AI and big data is the fastest-growing skill.

Thinking

Analytical thinking, problem formulation, systems thinking and creative thinking.

Orchestration and assurance

Directing agents, overseeing their work, interpreting exceptions and managing digital workers.

Human and relational

Leadership and social influence, empathy, storytelling, giving feedback and building trust.

Adaptive mindset

Resilience, flexibility, curiosity, lifelong learning, and the “pilot” mix of high agency and optimism.

Domain depth

Deep industry knowledge. Agentic AI raises demand for top-tier experts, and the minimum AI-native team pairs a domain expert with an AI engineer.

New roles named in the readings

AI process architect · knowledge steward · manager of digital workers · assurance and systems-thinking roles · storyteller who contextualises AI output · forward-deployed AI engineer · AI collaboration architect · digital transformation czar reporting to the CEO · AI engineer paired with a domain expert.

Implications for HR and managers

  • Rewrite job descriptions around learning agility, domain depth and ownership of agent orchestration.
  • Build career paths so AI feels like career evolution rather than erosion.
  • Upskill existing staff using the six training practices from Topic 1.1; hiring alone cannot close the gap.
  • Reward knowledge sharing so experts see encoding their judgment as legacy building.
  • Set quality standards for AI-assisted work so skills grow instead of eroding into workslop.
Exam tip

Lead with the WEF numbers, show three or four “from → to” shifts with sources, then name the new roles. Link back to the Adoption Matrix: competence alone is not enough without buy-in.

Topic 4.3

From automation to autonomy: redefining workflow dynamics

No lab exam. You are expected to draw the outline of a workflow.

In one line

Automation executes steps a human designed. Autonomy lets AI agents choose the steps toward a goal within guardrails, so people move from doing each step to designing the flow, setting limits and handling exceptions.

The spectrum from automation to autonomy

How workflow dynamics change

Design principles from the readings

  1. Re-architect before you automate: remove dead steps, name each decision owner, simplify.
  2. Start with a bounded, high-friction process that matters.
  3. Make handoff points to humans explicit, with human approval for high-stakes steps.
  4. Use accurate, consistent data and reliable signals; keep exceptions visible.
  5. Build testing, monitoring, escalation and audit trails, because agents are not deterministic.
  6. Govern agents like a workforce: who creates them, what they may do, who is accountable.
  7. Prepare people for new roles: overseeing agents, interpreting exceptions, improving the flow.

How to draw a workflow outline

Tools such as Zapier, Make, n8n and Microsoft Power Automate all use the same vocabulary: a trigger starts the flow and actions follow, with conditions and branches between them. Draw it with standard flowchart shapes.

Input or output data
Action or process step

AI step (our notation)

  1. Name the trigger.
  2. List the inputs.
  3. Write the actions in order.
  4. Add decisions with labelled yes/no branches.
  5. Mark which steps are AI and where a human checks.
  6. Show the outputs and notifications.
  7. Add the exception path.
  8. End with logging and the metrics you would track.
Exam tip

Always label the trigger, at least one decision with both branches, the human checkpoint and the end point. Then write one sentence on where the flow is rule-based and where it is autonomous. That sentence shows you understand “automation to autonomy.”

Eight questions with model answers

Write your own answer first, then open the model answer and compare structure, examples and numbers.

Q1Differentiate digitization, digitalization and digital transformation. What are the implications for managers?

Definitions. Digitization converts analog information to digital form (Gartner). Digitalization uses digital technology to change the business model and create new revenue and value (Gartner). Digital transformation is organization-wide change in strategy, structure, culture and skills built on digitalization.

Examples. Scanning invoices (digitization); automatic e-invoicing and payment (digitalization); finance rebuilt as a data-driven partner with new roles (transformation). Moderna, a “tech company that happens to do biology,” built on data, cloud and AI algorithms.

Implications. (1) Redesign processes first: “If you have crappy analog processes, you'll get crappy digital processes” (Bancel). (2) Build a digital mindset through voluntary, well-marketed upskilling; Atos certified 70,000+ people in three years. (3) Manage adoption with the Adoption Matrix: raise buy-in for the oppressed and indifferent, confidence for the oppressed and frustrated. (4) Pair a bold stroke with a long march. (5) Business leaders, not only IT, should choose tools. (6) Treat change as constant; be adaptive.

Q2“AI is a strategic game-changer.” Discuss using the agentic AI reading. How should incumbents respond?

Thesis. Agentic AI causes a “second great compression of entrepreneurship”: the cost, time and head count to prototype, test and improve have collapsed, which shifts the competitive baseline.

Five forces. Zero-latency iteration (Atomic's prototype in days versus 12–18 months and $1M+); automated go-to-market (implementations in a quarter of the time); autonomous business functions (domain expert plus AI engineer; one Tactix engineer does the work of ten); radical capital efficiency (DVx startups reach Series A with 80% less capital); the AI-driven flywheel (Anterior's ingestion engine and switching costs).

Threat. Incumbents are built for stability and control, carry technical debt, and tend to automate before re-architecting.

Response. Vulnerability audit against the five forces; start with a bounded, high-friction process (NTT Data's 20-minute RFP drafts); re-architect before automating; partner with AI-native ventures; improve data and make human handoffs explicit; prepare people for new roles. Incumbents still hold proprietary data, deep domain talent and a reputation for reliability and compliance.

Q3What is the last-mile problem in AI transformation? Explain the frictions and the strategies to resolve them.

Definition. The stage where technical capability must meet organizational design. Pilots succeed, but gains stay in “islands of productivity”; a contract drafted in seconds waits two weeks for legal review. Source: the Frontier Firm Initiative summit (HBS D^3 Institute and Microsoft).

Frictions. Proliferation of pilots (250+ LLM apps at one bank); productivity gap (saved time re-absorbed into meetings); process debt (the same process run dozens of ways); the identity problem of tribal knowledge; governance in an agentic world (100+ agents moving to tens of thousands); architectural complexity (months to connect SAP, Microsoft and Google agents); the efficiency trap (AI framed as offshoring).

Strategies. Clean-sheet process redesign (outer and inner loops, agent-centric mapping); strategic knowledge capture (pre-harvest and execution context, legacy building); managing the digital workforce (agentic control planes); role redesign and career pathing (learning agility, domain depth, agent orchestration). Map each friction to its pillar.

Conclusion. The last mile is blocked by operating models, governance and human identity, not technology.

Q4Explain the paradigm-shifting role of AI and its impact on organizations.

Definition. OECD: a machine-based system that infers from input how to generate predictions, content, recommendations or decisions, with varying autonomy and adaptiveness.

Evolution. Rules → predictive machine learning → generative AI → agentic AI.

Shifts. Tool to teammate; cheap prediction makes judgment more valuable (Agrawal, Gans and Goldfarb); AI factories remove limits on scale, scope and learning (Iansiti and Lakhani); resource advantage gives way to speed (AI-native startups); task automation gives way to work redesign; doing gives way to orchestrating and verifying.

Impact. Individual (41% of U.S. workers use AI for work, per SHRM; about half are unsure of outputs), team (workslop erodes trust), organization (over 40% of agentic projects may be cancelled by 2027, per Gartner, without redesign), industry (lower barriers to entry).

Q5What is the paradox of prompt engineering? Explain workslop, its root causes, consequences and remedies.

Paradox. AI makes polished output effortless, but useful output still needs human framing, context and review. Effort saved by the creator lands on the receiver. The durable skill is problem formulation (Acar): diagnosis, decomposition, reframing and constraint design.

Workslop. “AI generated work content that masquerades as good work, but lacks the substance to meaningfully advance a given task.” It uses machines to offload cognitive work onto another human.

Root causes. A management failure. From the top: vague mandates (41% got no detailed instructions). From below: overloaded, depleted staff and low trust. Result: performative AI use. Passengers (low agency, low optimism) use AI to avoid work.

Consequences. 40% received workslop in the last month; 1 h 56 min per instance; $186 per employee per month; $9M+ a year for 10,000 staff; 42% see the sender as less trustworthy; 32% less willing to work with them.

Remedies. Competence and control (half as likely to create workslop); team trust (61% less workslop); treat it as a signal and avoid the fundamental attribution error; act on culture, practice and accountability; same standards for human-AI duos as for humans alone.

Q6Narrate the functionality of an AI agent built on a no-code platform to process employee expense claims.

Purpose. Check and route expense claims so that routine claims are paid within a day and finance reviews only exceptions.

Trigger. An employee uploads receipts in Teams or the expense portal.

Knowledge. The travel and expense policy, per-diem limits and the approved-vendor list.

Orchestration and tools. The agent reads each receipt (amount, date, merchant, category), checks it against policy, asks the employee for a missing business purpose, looks up the cost centre in the HR system and creates the claim in the finance system.

Guardrails. It can auto-approve only claims under a set limit that fully match policy. It cannot change amounts. It flags duplicate receipts.

Human handoff. Out-of-policy or high-value claims go to the manager with a summary and a recommendation.

Output and monitoring. The employee is told the status; approved claims go to payroll; finance tracks cycle time, error rate, escalation rate and policy breaches on a dashboard. The agent's owner, access rights and accountability are recorded in the control plane.

Q7Explain SHRM's three key aspects of the future of work and the competencies the transformed workplace requires.

Work (what). Tasks unbundled and automated or augmented; human-agent teams; SHRM 2026: 39% of organizations report shifted responsibilities, 24% new roles, 7% displacement.

Worker (who). Rising demand for people who can work with and manage AI; 57% report more upskilling; a talent continuum that now includes digital workers; well-being and identity concerns.

Workplace (where and how). Hybrid and flexible; leadership and culture as the engine of resilience; norms and trust (trust cuts workslop by 61%).

Competencies. WEF 2025: 39% of skills change by 2030; analytical thinking is the top core skill; AI and big data grows fastest. Shifts: doing to orchestrating, knowing to encoding, prompting to problem formulation, specialist to techno-functional, fixed skills to learning agility, volume to quality. New roles: AI process architects, knowledge stewards, managers of digital workers.

Q8Draw an outline of a leave-request workflow and show where automation ends and autonomy begins.
  1. (Oval) START: an employee submits a leave request in the HR portal or Teams.
  2. (Parallelogram) Input: dates, leave type, reason.
  3. (Rectangle, automation) Fetch the leave balance and team calendar from the HR system.
  4. (Diamond) Enough balance? No → notify the employee with alternatives → END.
  5. (AI step) The agent checks team coverage and project deadlines and drafts a recommendation.
  6. (Diamond) Standard request? (for example 3 days or fewer and coverage is fine) Yes → auto-approve under policy.
  7. No → (dashed) Human checkpoint: the manager approves or rejects, using the agent's summary.
  8. (Rectangle) Update the HR system, payroll and shared calendar.
  9. (Parallelogram) Output: notify the employee and manager.
  10. (Rectangle) Log every decision for audit; review approval time and exceptions monthly.
  11. (Oval) END.

Where autonomy begins. Steps 3–4 are rule-based automation. Steps 5–6 are autonomous: the agent weighs coverage and deadlines and approves standard requests itself within policy. Step 7 keeps a human on the loop for exceptions.

Glossary

Adoption Matrix
Neeley and Leonardi's 2×2 of buy-in against confidence to learn: oppressed, frustrated, indifferent, inspired.
Agent governance
Rules for who may create agents, what they may do and who is accountable when they fail.
Agentic AI system
A network of specialised AI agents, coordinated by a digital manager, that plan, act and adapt toward an outcome.
Agentic control plane
A central dashboard that monitors agents' performance, permissions and accuracy, like HR performance management.
AI agent
An AI system that uses context and connected tools to carry out complex tasks toward a goal.
AI factory
Iansiti and Lakhani's term for a firm's data pipelines, algorithms, experimentation platform and infrastructure.
Bold stroke and long march
A dramatic signal of change followed by the sustained work of building buy-in and skills.
Clean-sheet redesign
Designing a process from scratch as if built today with AI agents, instead of adding AI to the old flow.
Cognitive offloading
Handing mental work to a tool. Workslop is offloading onto another person.
Digital mindset
Attitudes and behaviours that let people see how data, algorithms and AI open new possibilities.
Digitalization
Using digital technology to change a business model and create new value (Gartner).
Digitization
Converting analog information into digital form (Gartner).
Digital transformation
Organization-wide change in strategy, structure, culture and skills built on digitalization.
Edge case
A rare or extreme situation outside normal use that can cause unexpected errors.
Efficiency trap
Framing AI mainly as cost-cutting, which triggers defensiveness and narrows ambition.
Forward-deployed engineer
An engineer, or agent, embedded with the customer or team to fit software to real workflows.
Frontier firm
A company redesigned around AI and agents as a unified operating model.
Fundamental attribution error
Blaming a person's character while ignoring the situation that shaped their behaviour.
Generative orchestration
An LLM planning layer that interprets intent, breaks down requests and picks tools and knowledge (Copilot Studio).
Human in / on the loop
In: a person approves key steps. On: a person monitors and handles exceptions.
Islands of productivity
Individual AI gains that never connect into enterprise-wide value.
Last mile
The stage where AI capability must meet organizational design; where most transformations stall.
Pilots and passengers
Workers with high agency and optimism who use AI purposefully, versus those who use it to avoid work.
Pre-harvest and execution context
Rules needed before a task starts, and the nuanced data passed between agents during it.
Problem formulation
Diagnosis, decomposition, reframing and constraint design (Acar).
Process debt
Brittle, exception-ridden processes built up over years that AI exposes.
Product-market fit
Evidence that a product satisfies strong market demand.
RPA
Robotic process automation: software bots that follow fixed rules to repeat screen-based tasks.
Technical debt
Extra rework and cost caused by earlier suboptimal software design.
Tribal knowledge
Undocumented know-how held by long-tenured staff.
Vibe coding
Building software by describing it in natural language to an AI coding tool.
Workslop
AI-generated work that looks good but lacks the substance to advance the task.
Zero-latency iteration
Changing a digital product instantly in response to customer feedback.
