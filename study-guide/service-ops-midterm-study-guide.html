Part 1

Session 1: Nature of Services

Mostly theory — expect short answers / 1-2 marks. Learn the lists and the examples.

Core ideas

  • Definition by exclusion: services are defined by what they are not — economic reports call anything not “goods-producing” or “extraction-based” a service (retail, wholesale, transport, finance, lodging, education, government, entertainment…).
  • Evolution: Pre-industrial (domestic servants, sailors; relationships; little tech) → Industrial (assembly line, human as a “cog”, output focus, blue- vs white-collar) → Post-industrial (health, education, recreation; information rather than muscle; far higher share of service activity).
  • Service–product continuum: product-dominant (passenger cars, machine tools) ↔ mixed (restaurants, fitness centres, hospitals) ↔ service-dominant (consulting, legal, logistics, tourism, facilities maintenance).

  • High contact = pure services (hospitals, restaurants, banking) → mixed → Low contact = quasi-manufacturing (distribution centres, warehouses).

High-labour/high-customisation (professional) → hiring & training, SOPs, workforce scheduling, control of far-flung locations, making service “warm”, quality vs cost, customers intervening in the process.

Other slide points

  • Low-cost vs full-service airline: high volume/low cost, short hauls, secondary airports, no frills vs global network, long haul with partner connections, primary airports, economy-to-first cabins.
  • Service blueprint: strategic elements (service concept, service design) vs tactical/operational elements (facility, revenue management, queuing theory).
  • Sharing economy: classical inventory asks how much / when to order (controlled supply). On platforms supply and demand stimulate each other and are not controlled by the platform → the decision becomes matching a unit of supply to a unit of demand.
  • Internet service design elements: product, process, touch point, outcome; product/technology/task; customer/employee; performance.
  • New services (examples): AIaaS, carbon-footprint auditing, elder companionship, pet tech, digital detox (fast growth); circular economy, urban farming, skill barter, therapy bots (scalable); metaverse, space tourism, memory preservation (transformational).
Part 2

Waiting Lines & Queuing

The biggest numerical block. Master M/M/1, M/M/1/N, M/M/c, M/D/1 and the cost trade-off.

2.1 Concepts (1-2 mark theory)

  • Why lines form: (a) workload > capacity, or (b) variability in arrivals and/or service times — even when average workload < capacity. Class demo: single teller, workload 50 min vs 60 min available → total wait 52 min; two tellers (120 min available) → 7 min. So waiting is not linear in capacity.
  • Balking = arrival sees long queue and does not join. Reneging = leaves the queue before service. Jockeying = switches queues. (Single queue → no jockeying; multiple queues → jockeying.)
  • Channel = number of servers; phase = number of sequential steps. Single-channel/single-phase (one cashier); single-channel/multi-phase (car wash: wash → dry); multi-channel/single-phase (bank counters); multi-channel/multi-phase (hospital: registration → doctor → tests).
  • Kendall notation M/M/c/N/pop: arrivals Poisson (M), service Exponential (M) or Deterministic (D), c servers, N = max in system, population size.
  • Economics: cost of capacity rises with servers, cost of waiting falls; total cost is U-shaped; minimum = optimal capacity. Waiting costs = opportunity cost of time, psychological cost, abandonment.
  • Stability: for infinite queue models need ρ < 1.

Psychology of waiting (tactics)

Managing demand

  • Challenges: cyclical demand (daily/weekly), random vs planned arrivals (emergency vs appointments), heterogeneous demand sources (business vs leisure).
  • Strategies: use off-peak capacity creatively (hotel conferences); complementary services (kids zone in cinema); price incentives (night data packs, mid-week discounts); reservations (benefits: customer — no wait, guaranteed service; firm — steadier demand, caps demand). Risk: reserved demand that does not show up = lost revenue → leads to overbooking (Part 3).

2.3 Solved examples

EasyExample 2.1 — M/M/1 basics (class: railway counter)Customers arrive every 3 minutes at a railway ticket counter; the teller serves 25 customers/hour. Infinite queue and population. (a) % of time teller idle, (b) % of time there is no queue. Also find LQ and WQ.
λ = 60/3 = 20/hr, μ = 25/hr ⇒ ρ = 20/25 = 0.8.
(a) P0 = 1 − ρ = 0.20 → 20%.
(b) No queue ⇔ 0 or 1 in system: P0 + P1 = 0.20 + 0.8(0.20) = 0.20 + 0.16 = 0.36 → 36%.
LQ = λ2/[μ(μ−λ)] = 400/(25×5) = 3.2 customers. WQ = 20/(25×5) = 0.16 hr = 9.6 min. LS = 3.2+0.8 = 4.0; WS = 1/5 hr = 12 min.
(a) 20% (b) 36% LQ = 3.2, WQ = 9.6 min
Easy-ModerateExample 2.2 — M/M/1 probabilities (pharmacy counter)Arrivals every 5 min; average service 4 min; one pharmacist. Find LS, LQ, WS, WQ, the probability that 3 or more people are in the system, and that more than 4 are.
λ = 12/hr, μ = 60/4 = 15/hr, ρ = 0.8.
LS = ρ/(1−ρ) = 0.8/0.2 = 4. LQ = 144/(15×3) = 3.2 (check: 4 − 0.8 = 3.2 ✓).
WS = 1/(15−12) = 1/3 hr = 20 min. WQ = 12/(15×3) = 0.2667 hr = 16 min (check: LQ/λ = 3.2/12 ✓).
P(n ≥ 3) = ρ3 = 0.83 = 0.512. P(n > 4) = ρ5 = 0.85 = 0.328.
LS=4, LQ=3.2, WS=20 min, WQ=16 min, P(≥3)=0.512, P(>4)=0.328
ModerateExample 2.3 — Finite queue M/M/1/N (class, then extended)Same railway teller (λ=20, μ=25) but at most N = 10 customers in the system. (a) % idle, (b) % no queue. Then interpret vs the infinite case.
ρ = 0.8. P0 = (1−ρ)/(1−ρ11) = 0.2/(1 − 0.0859) = 0.2188 → 21.9%.
P1 = ρP0 = 0.175; no queue = P0+P1 = 0.394 → 39.4%.
Interpretation: idle time is higher with a finite queue (21.9% vs 20%; no-queue 39.4% vs 36%) because arrivals are turned away when full. Customer view: less congestion for admitted customers but risk of being blocked. Provider view: controls congestion and needs less waiting space, but lost customers and revenue.
21.9% and 39.4% (vs 20% and 36% for infinite queue)
DifficultExample 2.4 — M/M/1/N with lost revenueA car-wash bay has room for at most N = 4 cars in total (one being washed). Cars arrive at λ = 6/hr; the wash rate is μ = 5/hr. Each car brings Rs 200 contribution. Find P0, PN, λeff, LS, LQ, WS, WQ, and lost contribution per 10-hour day.
ρ = 6/5 = 1.2 (>1 is allowed because the system is finite).
P0 = (1−1.2)/(1−1.25) = (−0.2)/(1−2.48832) = 0.2/1.48832 = 0.1344.
PN = P4 = 1.24×0.1344 = 2.0736×0.1344 = 0.2786 (27.9% of arrivals are blocked).
λeff = 6(1−0.2786) = 4.328/hr. Lost = 6×0.2786 = 1.672/hr.
LS = ρ{1 + NρN+1 − (N+1)ρN} / [(1−ρ)(1−ρN+1)] = 1.2{1 + 4(2.48832) − 5(2.0736)} / [(−0.2)(−1.48832)] = 1.2(1 + 9.9533 − 10.368)/0.29766 = 1.2(0.5853)/0.29766 = 2.359.
LQ = LS − λeff/μ = 2.359 − 4.328/5 = 2.359 − 0.866 = 1.494.
WS = 2.359/4.328 = 0.545 hr = 32.7 min. WQ = 1.494/4.328 = 0.345 hr = 20.7 min.
Lost contribution/day = 1.672 × 10 × 200 = Rs 3,344. Two decimals of ρN+1 matter — keep 4 decimals.
PN=0.279, λeff=4.33/hr, LS=2.36, LQ=1.49, WS=32.7 min, WQ=20.7 min, loss ≈ Rs 3,344/day
ModerateExample 2.5 — Fire one mechanic, hire another? (class: Star Garage)Raju installs 3 mufflers/hr; customers arrive at 1/hr; shop open 8 hr/day. Waiting cost = Rs 500 per hour of customer time in queue. Raju is paid Rs 150/hr. Shyam does 4/hr but is paid 50% more. Should Apte replace Raju by Shyam?
λ = 1. Raju: μ = 3 ⇒ LQ = 1/[3(3−1)] = 1/6 = 0.1667. Waiting cost/hr = 500 × 0.1667 = 83.33. Wage 150. Total/hr = 233.33 ⇒ Rs 1,866.67/day.
Shyam: μ = 4 ⇒ LQ = 1/[4(3)] = 1/12 = 0.0833. Waiting cost/hr = 41.67. Wage = 1.5×150 = 225. Total/hr = 266.67 ⇒ Rs 2,133.33/day.
(Same answer using WQ: Raju WQ = 1/6 hr; cost/hr = 500×λ×WQ = 83.33 — identical because LQ = λWQ and λ = 1.)
Keep Raju: Rs 1,867 vs Rs 2,133 per day. Faster server does not pay for its wage premium.

Part 3

Revenue & Yield Management

Selling the right product to the right customer at the right time, through the right channel, at the right price.

3.1 Concepts

  • Definition: using pricing to increase the profit generated from limited (perishable) assets.
  • Works best when: value differs across segments (business vs vacationers); the product is perishable (seats, rooms); demand has seasonality/peaks; services are sold in advance. Core dilemma: accept an early discounted booking or hold the unit for a late high-paying customer?
  • Traditional pricing (Total revenue = P×Q on one demand curve) ignores segments with inelastic demand (business traveller booking last minute).
  • Fare classes (airlines): Corporate (buy any time, fully refundable/changeable), Vacationers (≥ 2 weeks advance, non-refundable), Internet special (when flight not expected full, non-refundable).
  • Two questions RM answers: how to differentiate segments so one pays more, and how to stop the low-fare segment consuming all capacity (→ capacity protection).
  • Overbooking applies when capacity is fixed & perishable and customers cancel/no-show. Denied service → airlines compensate/re-accommodate; hotels re-accommodate at other hotels; air cargo re-books; clinics use overtime.

3.2 Formula boxes

Critical ratio (newsvendor logic)
Cu = cost of under-protecting/under-booking
Co = cost of over-protecting/over-booking
CR = CuCu + Co
Q* = μ + zσ where P(X ≤ Q*) = CR (z from normal table)
Littlewood’s rule (capacity protection for the high fare)
Protect the next seat for the high fare while FH × P(DH > Q) > FL
At the optimum: P(DH > Q*) = FLFH ⇔ P(DH ≤ Q*) = FH − FLFH = CR
Cu = FH − FL (protected too few → sold a seat cheap that could earn FH)
Co = FL (protected too many → seat empty, could have earned FL)
Q* = μH + zσH = seats to protect for high fare  ⇒  max low-fare seats to sell = Capacity − Q*
σ = √variance. “CR probability = 1 − Littlewood probability”: Littlewood probability = P(DH > Q) = FL/FH.
Overbooking
Let X = number of no-shows (or no-show %). Overbook by q where P(X ≤ q) ≥ CR
Cu = cost when a booked customer doesn’t show and the room/seat goes empty (lost contribution)

ModerateExample 3.1 — Littlewood (class: budget airline)Full-fare and internet-special return tickets cost Rs 6,900 and Rs 4,900. Aircraft has 95 seats; ample demand for the internet special. Full-fare demand ~ Normal(mean 60, variance 225). How many internet-special tickets should be sold at most?
FH = 6900, FL = 4900. Cu = 2000, Co = 4900. CR = 2000/6900 = 0.2899.
σ = √225 = 15. Find z with P(Z ≤ z) = 0.2899 ⇒ z ≈ −0.55 (z-table: 0.2912 at −0.55; exact −0.554).
Q* = 60 + (−0.554)(15) = 60 − 8.3 = 51.7 → protect 52 seats for full fare.
Max internet specials = 95 − 52 = 43.
Sanity check: FL/FH = 0.71 ⇒ we protect only until there is a 71% chance of selling the marginal seat at full fare, so Q* is below the mean (51.7 < 60).
Protect ≈ 52 seats for full fare; sell at most 43 internet-special tickets.

Part 4

Service Facility Layout (slide 47 onwards)

Product layout, process layout, line balancing, bottlenecks, labour utilisation and cost of capacity.

4.1 Concepts

  • Product layout (line flow, like a vehicle assembly line: chassis drop → engine mounting → cab mounting → …). Used for high volume, low variety: standardised service to many customers (cafeteria line, airport security). Customer is an input moving through stations.
  • Process layout: functional departments; customers route differently (retail shop, hospital) → for high variety, low volume.
  • McDonald’s innovations: indoor seating (1950s), drive-through (1970s), breakfast (1980s), play areas (late 1980s), self-service kiosk (2004). 4 of 5 concern layout.
  • Service design factors: (a) objective of service (emergency ward: no traffic, easy approach; petrol pump: bright colour visible from afar); (b) space requirement & location (rural vs urban → vertical expansion); (c) security (CCTV, biometrics at immigration); (d) flexibility (adapt to new equipment, expansion provision).
  • Mongolian Grill: customers assemble their own bowls at open-grill/food-prep stations. Objective: raise capacity and throughput with self-service. Result: higher profit; small bottlenecks (≤ 2 min) cleared by cooks directing traffic; repeat customers who understand queues walk to the farthest prep area first and reduce bottlenecks — customer behaviour affects service quality/flow.
  • Bottleneck strategy: add parallel workers/equipment at the slowest station, or re-split the tasks, to balance the line — but every extra worker adds cost, so compare cost per customer.

ModerateExample 4.1 — Cafeteria line (class)Four stations, 1 worker each: drinks 20 s, starters & soup 30 s, main course 40 s, dessert 15 s. Lunch window 1 hr. (a) Capacity and utilisation as-is. (b) Ideal balanced line? (c) Option: double the main-course station.
Labour content = 20+30+40+15 = 105 s. 4 workers.
(a) As-is: bottleneck = main course 40 s ⇒ cycle time 40 s ⇒ capacity = 3600/40 = 90 customers/hr. Station capacities: 180, 120, 90, 240. Utilisation = 105/(40×4) = 65.63%.
(b) Ideal: cycle = 105/4 = 26.25 s each ⇒ capacity 3600/26.25 = 137.14/hr, utilisation 100% (theoretical; tasks rarely divide perfectly).
(c) Two workers at main course (effective 20 s): times 20, 30, 20, 15 ⇒ bottleneck 30 s (starters) ⇒ capacity = 120/hr; 5 workers: utilisation = 105/(30×5) = 70%.
Cost effect: workers 4 → 5 (+25%), capacity 90 → 120 (+33%) ⇒ cost per customer falls (4X/90 = 0.0444X vs 5X/120 = 0.0417X). Next bottleneck is starters — add a worker there only if demand > 120/hr.

Moderate-DifficultExample 4.3 — Sizing workers to meet demandA three-station service line: verification 30 s, processing 45 s, delivery 20 s. Target throughput = 100 customers/hr. How many workers per station? Capacity and utilisation?
Required cycle time = 3600/100 = 36 s per customer.
Workers needed per station = ⌈time / 36⌉: verification ⌈30/36⌉ = 1; processing ⌈45/36⌉ = 2 (effective 22.5 s); delivery ⌈20/36⌉ = 1. Total workers = 4.

ConceptExample 4.4 — Which layout, and why?Why did the cafeteria use a product layout, and how does it differ from a retail shop?
Cafeteria: standard service, high volume in a 1-hour lunch window → stations arranged in the sequence of service (drinks → starters → main → dessert); efficiency comes from balancing station times; the customer is an input moving along the line. Retail shop: many product categories and customers choose their own route → process (functional) layout; high variety, low volume.
High volume + low variety ⇒ product layout; high variety + low volume ⇒ process layout.

Standard pitfalls

  • λ and μ in the same unit and per hour. “Every 3 min” is arrival interval ⇒ λ = 20/hr.
  • Check ρ < 1 for infinite-queue models. If ρ ≥ 1, say the queue grows without bound.
  • M/M/1/N: N counts the customer in service. Use λeff for W. ρ may exceed 1.
  • M/M/c: P0 uses ∑ up to c−1 and the last term with (1−ρ); LQ uses r = λ/μ.
  • “% time no queue” = P0 + P1, not P0 alone.
  • Cost questions: waiting cost uses LQ if time in queue, LS if in system. Compare on the same basis (per hour or per day).
  • Littlewood: Q* is seats protected for high fare; discount seats = capacity − Q*. Use σ = √variance.
  • Overbooking: identify which cost is Cu (empty room / no-show) and which is Co (denied guest). Swapping them flips the answer.
  • Pricing: check whether capacity binds by summing the uncapacitated demands first. Profit uses true c.
  • Layout: cycle time = bottleneck effective time; utilisation denominator uses total workers (including extra ones).
  • Round protection levels/workers sensibly and state the rounding; always give a one-line recommendation.
